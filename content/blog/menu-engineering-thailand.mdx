Most restaurant owners in Thailand set their menu prices the same way: look at what the place down the street charges, add or subtract 10 baht, and hope for the best. That is not a pricing strategy. It is guesswork, and it leaves enormous amounts of money on the table.

Menu engineering is the process of analysing every item on your menu by two dimensions, profitability and popularity, and then making deliberate decisions about what to promote, what to reprice, what to redesign, and what to remove. I have used this framework with over 80 restaurants across Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Phuket, and Pattaya over the past 15 years, and the results are consistent: a properly engineered menu improves gross profit by 8-15% without raising overall prices or losing customers.

That 8-15% on a restaurant doing 1.5 million THB/month in revenue is 120,000-225,000 THB going straight to the bottom line. Every month. For a process that takes about two weeks to implement.

This guide walks through the full menu engineering process adapted for Thailand's F&B market: costing Thai dishes with their unique ingredient profiles, understanding local ordering behaviour, and redesigning your menu for better profitability.

Provincial prices may be 10-20% lower.

## What Is Menu Engineering?

Menu engineering was formalised in 1982 by Michael Kasavana and Donald Smith at Michigan State University. The original framework borrows from the Boston Consulting Group's growth-share matrix (the one with the stars and cash cows), adapted for restaurant menus.

The idea is straightforward. Every menu item has two measurable attributes:

1. **Contribution margin** — how much gross profit the item generates per serving (selling price minus food cost)
2. **Popularity (mix percentage)** — what share of total orders this item represents

Plot every item on a 2x2 matrix with these axes, and you get four categories. Kasavana and Smith gave them memorable names: Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, and Dogs.

The power of this framework comes from the specific actions each category prescribes. Instead of staring at your menu and wondering what to change, you have a data-driven playbook.

A quick note on why this matters in Thailand right now. Food costs have risen 18-22% across the board since 2023 — pork is up 25%, cooking oil is up 30%, and electricity costs are up 15%. Thai consumers are more price-sensitive than ever, the cost of living squeeze is real, and diners are choosing restaurants more carefully. In this environment, getting your menu right is not optional. It is survival.

## The Four Categories

### Stars (High Profit, High Popularity)

Stars are your best items — customers love them, they generate strong margins, and these are the dishes you build your restaurant's reputation around.

**Thai restaurant examples:**
- **Tom yum goong** priced at 280 THB with a food cost of 72 THB (74% margin, ordered by 35% of tables)
- **Pad kra pao with a fried egg** at 180 THB with a food cost of 38 THB (79% margin, ordered by 45% of tables)
- **Massaman curry** at 260 THB with a food cost of 68 THB (74% margin, ordered by 25% of tables)

**What to do with Stars:** Protect them. Do not change the recipe, do not change the portion size, and be very careful about raising prices. These items are pulling customers through your door. Feature them prominently on the menu — top of the page, in a box, with a photo. If you have a specials board, your Stars should be on it. Your servers should be trained to recommend them.

The one thing you can do with Stars is test small price increases. A dish that 35% of your customers order can often absorb a 10-20 THB increase without any drop in orders. On 100 covers a day, that is 3,500-7,000 THB extra per day — over 100,000 THB/month — from a single price change on one dish.

### Plowhorses (Low Profit, High Popularity)

Plowhorses are popular dishes that are not making you enough money. Customers order them constantly, but the margins are thin. These are often the dishes where an owner said "I want to keep the price down because everyone orders it." That is exactly backwards thinking.

**Thai restaurant examples:**
- **Pad thai** at 150 THB with a food cost of 68 THB (55% margin — too low)
- **Fried rice** at 120 THB with a food cost of 42 THB (65% margin — acceptable but low for a workhorse)
- **Som tum** at 100 THB with a food cost of 35 THB (65% margin — underpriced for the demand)

**What to do with Plowhorses:** Increase profitability without killing demand. You have several tools:

1. **Raise the price by 10-15%.** A 150 THB pad thai at 170 THB is still reasonable. Test it. Track the order volume for two weeks. Most of the time, you lose zero customers.
2. **Reduce the food cost.** Can you source a cheaper prawn? Use slightly less protein? Substitute one ingredient with a lower-cost alternative without affecting the perceived quality? A 5 THB reduction in food cost across a Plowhorse selling 50 servings/day is 150,000 THB/year.
3. **Reposition as a premium item.** Add a "deluxe" version. Pad thai with river prawns instead of regular prawns, at 280 THB. The original stays at 150 THB, but 20% of customers trade up to the premium version.
4. **Reduce portion slightly.** This is risky in Thailand where portions are closely scrutinised, but even a 5% reduction is rarely noticed if done carefully.

<Callout type="tip">
Pad thai is a Plowhorse in almost every Thai restaurant I audit. It is the most-ordered dish for international tourists and consistently underpriced relative to its ingredient cost (especially the prawns). If your pad thai has a food cost above 40%, it needs attention. A 20 THB price increase and a slight portion adjustment on the protein can shift it from a Plowhorse to a Star.
</Callout>

### Puzzles (High Profit, Low Popularity)

Puzzles are profitable dishes that nobody orders. The margins are great — if only more people would try them — and the challenge is figuring out why they are not selling.

**Thai restaurant examples:**
- **Gaeng hung lay** (Northern Thai pork belly curry) at 280 THB with a food cost of 55 THB (80% margin, ordered by 3% of tables)
- **Pla rad prik** (deep-fried fish with chilli sauce) at 350 THB with a food cost of 95 THB (73% margin, ordered by 5% of tables)
- **Yum som-o** (pomelo salad) at 220 THB with a food cost of 48 THB (78% margin, ordered by 4% of tables)

Puzzles are low-popularity for a reason. Common causes in Thai restaurants:

- **The name is unfamiliar.** Foreign customers do not know what gaeng hung lay is. Even Thai customers from other regions might not recognise it.
- **The price looks high.** 350 THB for a fish dish feels expensive relative to a 150 THB pad thai, even though the margin is better.
- **It is buried on page 3 of the menu.** Nobody reads that far.
- **No photo, no description.** Customers default to dishes they already know.

**What to do with Puzzles:**

1. **Add descriptions and photos.** "Slow-braised pork belly in a northern Thai curry with pickled garlic and ginger" sells better than "Gaeng Hung Lay."
2. **Move them on the menu.** Place Puzzles next to your Stars. The eye travels to the Stars and picks up the Puzzles in passing.
3. **Have servers recommend them.** "Have you tried our pomelo salad? It is one of our chef's favourite dishes." This alone can double a Puzzle's orders.
4. **Offer it as a pairing.** "Add a som-o salad to any main dish for 160 THB" (discounted from 220 THB). This introduces the dish to customers who would never have ordered it independently.

If a Puzzle still does not sell after 60 days of active promotion, it may be a Dog in disguise. Cut it.

### Dogs (Low Profit, Low Popularity)

Dogs are items that nobody orders and that make no money when they do get ordered. Every restaurant has them, and most owners keep them on the menu out of habit, sentimentality, or because a single regular customer orders it once a month.

**Thai restaurant examples:**
- **Steak (imported, on a Thai menu)** at 450 THB with a food cost of 220 THB (51% margin, ordered by 2% of tables)
- **Spaghetti carbonara (on a Thai restaurant menu)** at 180 THB with a food cost of 72 THB (60% margin, ordered by 1% of tables)
- **Mixed fruit smoothie** at 90 THB with a food cost of 42 THB (53% margin, ordered by 2% of tables)

**What to do with Dogs:** Remove them. Every Dog on your menu has costs beyond its food cost:

- **Inventory cost.** You are stocking ingredients that barely move and eventually expire.
- **Prep time.** A cook who has to remember how to make a dish they prepare twice a week is slower and less consistent than one who makes it 20 times a day.
- **Menu clutter.** Every Dog takes up space that could feature a Star or promote a Puzzle.
- **Cognitive load.** A longer menu means longer decision times, longer ordering times, and more errors.

The exception: a Dog that serves a strategic purpose. A kids' menu item that keeps families coming in. A low-cost rice dish that rounds out a shared meal. These can stay, but recognise them for what they are — cost centres, not profit drivers.

<Callout type="warning">
The most common Dog I see on Thai restaurant menus: Western dishes added "for the tourists." A mediocre club sandwich on a Thai restaurant menu is not attracting tourists — tourists came to eat Thai food. Meanwhile, the bread, deli meats, and cheese you stock for that sandwich are expiring in your fridge. If Western dishes represent less than 5% of your orders, remove them entirely and let your kitchen focus on what it does well.
</Callout>

## Step 1: Calculate Your Food Costs

You cannot engineer your menu without accurate food costs. This is where most Thai restaurant owners fall down. They have a rough idea that "pad thai costs about 50-60 baht to make" but have never calculated the actual cost per serving with current ingredient prices.

Here is how to do it properly:

1. **List every ingredient in the dish** — including oil, garlic, fish sauce, sugar, lime, garnish. Everything.
2. **Get the current purchase price** from your supplier or latest invoice.
3. **Calculate the cost per unit** used in the dish (e.g., 200g of prawns, 15ml of fish sauce).
4. **Add a 5-8% waste factor** — you will never use 100% of what you buy.
5. **Sum it up.** That is your food cost per serving.

## Step 2: Analyse Popularity

Profitability is only half the picture — you also need to know what people actually order, which requires POS data. If you do not have a POS system, stop reading this article and go set one up. FoodStory, Ocha, or StoreHub cost 1,000-3,000 THB/month and are not optional for a restaurant that wants to operate professionally.

Pull a report covering at least 30 days (ideally 90). You need:

- **Total units sold per menu item**
- **Revenue per menu item**
- **Percentage of total orders (mix percentage) per item**

The mix percentage tells you each item's share of total demand. In a typical Thai restaurant with 25 items on the menu, the average mix percentage would be 4% per item (100% divided by 25). Items above this average are "popular." Items below are "unpopular."

**Thai diner ordering patterns worth knowing:**

1. **Rice is not optional.** For Thai customers, steamed rice is ordered alongside almost every dish. This is essentially free revenue (rice costs 3-5 THB per serving and sells for 20-30 THB). Make sure it is easy to order and that servers confirm it. Every table without rice is leaving 20-30 THB on the table.

2. **Shared ordering dominates.** Thai dining is communal. A table of four does not order four individual mains — they order 3-4 shared dishes plus rice. This means your per-item profitability matters more than your per-plate profitability.

3. **Tourists order differently from Thais.** If your customer base is mixed, track these segments separately. Tourist ordering skews heavily toward pad thai, tom yum, green curry, and mango sticky rice. Thai ordering is broader and more stir-fry and curry heavy. You may find the same dish is a Star for one segment and a Dog for another.

4. **Lunch and dinner are different menus even if the physical menu is the same.** Lunch is faster, cheaper, and more individual. Dinner is shared, higher-spend, and more exploratory. If you have enough data, run the matrix separately for lunch and dinner.

5. **Seasonality exists.** Hot pot and warm soups sell better in Bangkok's "cool" season (November-February). Light salads and cold dishes sell better in the hot months. Your matrix may shift quarterly.

## Step 3: Map Your Menu Matrix

Now you combine the data. For each menu item, you have two numbers:

- **Contribution margin** (selling price minus food cost)
- **Mix percentage** (share of total orders)

Calculate the average contribution margin across all items and the average mix percentage. These two averages become your dividing lines on the matrix.

**Worked example — a 20-item Thai restaurant menu:**

Say your average contribution margin is 145 THB and your average mix percentage is 5%.

- **Pad kra pao:** Margin 142 THB, mix 12% → margin slightly below average, but popularity is very high → **Plowhorse**
- **Massaman curry:** Margin 192 THB, mix 7% → above average on both → **Star**
- **Gaeng hung lay:** Margin 225 THB, mix 1.5% → high margin, low popularity → **Puzzle**
- **Club sandwich:** Margin 85 THB, mix 0.8% → below average on both → **Dog**

Plot every item. You will typically find:

- **3-5 Stars** (your bread and butter, protect and promote)
- **4-6 Plowhorses** (popular but need margin work)
- **4-6 Puzzles** (profitable but need visibility)
- **3-5 Dogs** (remove or replace)

The items closest to the dividing lines are borderline cases. A Plowhorse that is 5 THB below the average margin and 1% above the average mix is practically a Star. A small price increase pushes it over. Pay attention to these edge cases; they represent the easiest wins.

## Step 4: Take Action on Each Category

Here is a concrete action plan for each quadrant, tailored to the Thai market:

### Stars: Protect and Showcase

- Feature with photos on the menu (invest in professional food photography, 1,500-3,000 THB per dish)
- Place in the "golden triangle" (top right of a two-page menu, or first items in each section)
- Train servers to recommend: "Our massaman curry is our most popular dish, and I personally love it"
- Never discount them
- Test small price increases (10-20 THB) once per quarter
- Monitor ingredient cost monthly. If the food cost creeps up, adjust before the margin erodes

### Plowhorses: Improve Margins

- Raise prices by 10-15% (test for two weeks, measure volume impact)
- Reduce portion size by 5-8% (adjust plating to maintain visual fullness)
- Negotiate better prices with suppliers for high-volume ingredients
- Create a premium version ("Pad Thai with river prawns, +80 THB")
- Bundle with high-margin items ("Add a Thai iced tea for 40 THB", cost to you: 10 THB)
- Remove from any discount or promotion. These are already selling well and do not need incentives

### Puzzles: Increase Visibility

- Add appetising descriptions (in both Thai and English if your clientele is mixed)
- Add a photo. Items with photos sell 25-30% more than items without
- Place next to Stars on the menu
- Include as a "Chef's Recommendation" with a small badge or icon
- Offer as a tasting portion or side dish to lower the entry barrier
- Brief servers: "If a table asks what to order, always suggest the yum som-o"
- Run a limited-time promotion to build trial: "Try our pomelo salad this week, complimentary with any curry order"
- If it still does not sell after 60 days of active promotion, reclassify it as a Dog

### Dogs: Remove or Replace

- Remove from the menu entirely (this is the default action)
- If it serves a strategic purpose (kids' item, dietary restriction option), keep it but bury it at the bottom of the menu
- Replace with a new dish that targets a gap in your matrix, ideally a potential Star with high margin and broad appeal
- Never promote, photograph, or feature a Dog
- Do not let emotional attachment override the numbers. "But my grandmother's recipe..." is not a business case

## Menu Design Psychology

The physical or digital layout of your menu directly affects what people order. This is backed by decades of eye-tracking research, and confirmed in every menu engineering project I have done.

### The Golden Triangle

On a two-panel menu (the most common format in Thailand), the eye naturally travels: centre of the right page, then top right, then top left. This "golden triangle" is your prime real estate. Your highest-margin items, Stars and promoted Puzzles, go here.

### Descriptions That Sell

Compare these two entries:

> **Gaeng Keow Wan Gai** ............ 220 THB

versus

> **Green Curry with Chicken** — Creamy coconut curry with Thai eggplant, bamboo shoots, and sweet basil. Medium spice, rich and aromatic. ............ 240 THB

The second version sells more, justifies a higher price, and reduces ordering anxiety (especially for foreign customers). Descriptive menu copy increases sales by 27% on average according to Cornell University research, and it takes one afternoon to write good descriptions for your entire menu.

**Rules for Thai restaurant menu descriptions:**

1. Include the English name alongside the Thai name. Always. Even if your restaurant is primarily Thai-language. You are leaving money on the table if any foreigner walks in and cannot parse your menu.
2. Mention the key protein and cooking method (grilled, stir-fried, slow-braised).
3. Indicate spice level. Thai food's heat is the number one barrier for international customers. A simple chilli icon system (one to three chillies) removes the guesswork.
4. Keep descriptions to 12-20 words. Do not write a paragraph — just enough to create a mental image.

### Pricing Display

Research from Cornell's Center for Hospitality Research shows that removing the currency symbol increases average spend. Instead of "฿220" or "220 THB", use just "220" with a small note at the bottom of the menu: "All prices in Thai Baht."

**Do not** use dot leaders (........) between the item name and the price. They draw the eye to the price, turning your menu into a price comparison exercise. Instead, nest the price at the end of the description or right-align it without a visual trail leading to it.

**Do not** list items from cheapest to most expensive within a section. This creates a "cheapest" anchor at the top and makes everything below it feel expensive. Instead, lead with a mid-priced Star, follow with your Puzzles and higher-margin items, and place Plowhorses later in the section.

### Photography

Professional food photography costs 1,500-3,000 THB per dish with a decent Bangkok food photographer — for a 20-item menu, that is 30,000-60,000 THB, and it pays for itself within the first month.

**Rules for menu photography:**

- Only photograph your Stars and Puzzles (items you want to sell more of). Do not photograph Dogs or Plowhorses.
- Consistency matters. Same lighting, same style, same plating standard. Mismatched photos look amateur.
- Update photos when you change the recipe or plating.
- Use photos on your physical menu, your digital menu, and your delivery platform listings. The ROI compounds across every channel.

## Digital Menu Considerations

### QR Code Menus

QR menus became standard in Thailand during the pandemic and many restaurants have kept them. From a menu engineering perspective, QR menus have advantages:

- **You can update prices and items instantly** without reprinting. This makes it easier to test price changes.
- **You can A/B test** — show different item orderings or descriptions to different tables.
- **You can track what people view** — which items are browsed but not ordered (potential Puzzles that need better presentation).

The downside: QR menus reduce the "browsing" effect. On a physical menu, customers scan the whole page and discover items they did not intend to order. On a phone screen, they scroll past items quickly. This means your Star and Puzzle positioning needs to work even harder in a digital format — they must appear in the first screenful of content.

### GrabFood and LINE MAN Listings

Delivery platforms are a separate menu engineering challenge. The key differences:

**Commission eats your margin.** GrabFood and LINE MAN charge 30-35% commission. A dish with a 70% margin in-house drops to a 35-40% margin on delivery. This means your delivery menu should be weighted toward your highest-margin items.

**Different ordering behaviour.** Delivery customers order fewer items per transaction and are more price-sensitive. They also cannot be upsold by a server. Your delivery menu pricing and item selection should reflect this.

**Platform ranking matters.** GrabFood and LINE MAN rank restaurants by order volume, ratings, and advertising spend. The dishes that appear in your "Top Picks" section should be your Stars — high margin, high satisfaction, consistent quality even after 20 minutes in a delivery bag.

<Callout type="warning">
A common mistake: copying your dine-in menu onto GrabFood and LINE MAN without adjusting prices or selection. Your delivery menu is a separate product with a separate cost structure. At 30-35% commission, any item with a food cost above 30% is losing money or breaking even on delivery. Either raise delivery prices by 15-20% (most platforms allow different pricing) or remove low-margin items from your delivery menu entirely.
</Callout>

**Recommended delivery menu strategy:**

1. Offer 60-70% of your dine-in menu on delivery (remove low-margin and presentation-dependent dishes).
2. Increase prices by 15-20% on delivery to offset commission.
3. Create delivery-only bundles (e.g., "Pad kra pao + rice + Thai iced tea" for 199 THB) that have a blended margin of 65%+.
4. Invest in packaging that keeps food quality high during transit. A 5 THB upgrade in packaging that prevents negative reviews is worth it.

## Common Mistakes

I have audited enough menus to see the same mistakes repeated across hundreds of restaurants. Here are the ones that cost the most money:

### 1. Too Many Items

A 50-item menu is not a sign of a versatile kitchen — it is a sign of an owner who cannot make decisions. The result is higher waste, slower service, inconsistent quality, and a menu that is impossible to engineer because half the items sell fewer than two orders per day.

Target: 20-30 items for a casual Thai restaurant. Fewer is fine. More is almost always worse.

### 2. Emotional Pricing

"I cannot charge 280 THB for green curry, that is expensive!" Compared to what? Street food? You are not running a street cart. Your rent is 100,000 THB/month, you have 15 staff, and your dining room has air conditioning and proper plumbing. Your prices must cover your costs, not match a roadside stall.

Price based on your cost structure and your target margin, then check if the resulting price is within the acceptable range for your location and concept. If a dish cannot hit your target margin at a price your market will bear, the dish does not belong on your menu.

### 3. Ignoring Prep Time

Food cost is not your only cost per dish. A tom yum that takes 8 minutes to prepare ties up a station for twice as long as a pad kra pao that takes 4 minutes. During a lunch rush, that prep time difference means fewer covers and less revenue.

When evaluating Plowhorses and Puzzles, factor in kitchen capacity. A high-margin Puzzle that takes 15 minutes to prepare may not be worth promoting if it slows down the line and reduces throughput on faster-to-execute Stars.

### 4. Never Updating the Menu

Your menu matrix shifts as ingredient prices change, customer preferences evolve, and new competitors open nearby. The menu you engineered six months ago may not reflect today's reality.

Run a full menu engineering review every 6 months, with a quick price-versus-cost check every quarter, and adjust continuously.

### 5. Treating All Customers the Same

A tourist couple ordering two dishes and two cocktails has a different spending pattern and price sensitivity than a Thai family of five ordering four shared dishes and water. If your POS can segment by customer type or daypart, use that data. You may find that a dish is a Star at dinner and a Dog at lunch.

### 6. Forgetting About Beverages

Many restaurant owners obsess over food cost and completely ignore the highest-margin category on the menu. Beverages, especially non-alcoholic drinks and cocktails, are where Thai restaurants can generate 78-88% margins.

An engineered beverage menu is just as important as an engineered food menu. Apply the same Stars/Plowhorses/Puzzles/Dogs framework to your drink list.

<Callout type="tip">
A simple high-impact change: add a "Recommended Pairing" note next to your Stars. "Pairs well with our lemongrass cooler (90 THB)" is subtle, non-pushy, and effective. If even 10% of customers order the suggested pairing, you are adding 65+ THB of profit per conversion because the drink cost is under 15 THB.
</Callout>

## Putting It All Together: A 14-Day Menu Engineering Sprint

Here is the process I use with consulting clients, compressed into a practical timeline:

**Days 1-3: Data Collection**
- Export 90 days of POS data (item-level sales, revenue, volume)
- Get current supplier pricing for every ingredient
- Cost every single menu item (use a spreadsheet, no shortcuts)

**Days 4-5: Analysis**
- Calculate contribution margin per item
- Calculate mix percentage per item
- Plot the matrix
- Categorise every item: Star, Plowhorse, Puzzle, Dog

**Days 6-8: Strategy**
- Decide actions for each item (promote, reprice, redesign, remove)
- Write new menu descriptions for Puzzles
- Set new prices for Plowhorses
- Select Dogs for removal
- Brief your photographer if you are adding photos

**Days 9-11: Menu Redesign**
- Lay out the new menu following golden triangle principles
- Place Stars and Puzzles in prime positions
- Ensure descriptions, photos, and formatting support your strategy
- Update your digital menu and delivery platform listings

**Days 12-14: Implementation**
- Print new menus (or update QR menu)
- Train servers on new recommendations and upselling scripts
- Brief kitchen on any recipe or portion adjustments
- Set a 30-day review date to measure results

**Day 30: Review**
- Compare same-item sales volume before and after
- Calculate new contribution margins
- Check if overall food cost percentage improved
- Identify items that did not respond to changes and plan next iteration

This is not a one-time exercise. The best restaurants I work with treat menu engineering as an ongoing discipline, a quarterly review at minimum, with continuous price-cost monitoring in between.
