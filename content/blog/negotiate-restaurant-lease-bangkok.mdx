After your concept, your lease is the single most consequential decision you will make when opening a restaurant in Bangkok. It determines your fixed costs for years. It constrains your renovation budget. It dictates your exit options if things go wrong. And unlike a bad menu or a weak hire, a bad lease cannot be fixed — you are locked in for the duration, or you pay dearly to get out.

I have reviewed and negotiated restaurant leases in Bangkok for over 15 years. In that time, I have seen leases that made restaurants viable and leases that bankrupted them. The difference was rarely the rent itself. It was the terms: the escalation clause that compounded to an unaffordable level in year four, the restoration clause that cost 800,000 THB to fulfill on exit, the missing exclusivity clause that allowed a competing restaurant to open on the same floor.

This article covers everything you need to know about negotiating a restaurant lease in Bangkok in 2026 — from key money and lease structures to specific clauses, red flags, and negotiation tactics.

All figures in this article are in Thai Baht (THB). At the time of writing, 1 USD is approximately 35.5 THB. Lease terms, key money, and rent are always denominated and paid in THB, regardless of the tenant's nationality. If you are budgeting in a foreign currency, build in a 10-15% buffer for exchange rate fluctuation.
</Callout>

## Why the Lease Matters More Than You Think

Let me give you a number that illustrates the stakes. On a typical 3+3 year lease for a 150 square meter restaurant space in a mid-tier Bangkok neighborhood at 1,200 THB per square meter per month, you are committing to:

- **Total base rent over 6 years:** approximately 12,960,000 THB (assuming 5% annual escalation)
- **Security deposit:** 540,000 THB (3 months at initial rent)
- **Key money:** 0-1,000,000 THB depending on location
- **Restoration cost at exit:** 200,000-500,000 THB

Your total lease-related commitment is 13.7-14.9 million THB over the life of the lease. That is more than many restaurants spend on their entire fit-out. Yet I routinely see operators spend months agonizing over whether to buy a 350,000 THB combi oven and then sign a 15-million-THB lease commitment after a single conversation with the landlord.

The lease deserves the same rigor you apply to your financial model, your concept development, and your [location analysis](/blog/how-to-find-restaurant-location-bangkok). Ideally more, because it is the one decision you cannot undo.

## Key Money: The Upfront Payment Nobody Explains Well

Key money — sometimes called a goodwill payment, transfer fee, or simply "premium" — is an upfront, non-refundable payment made to secure a lease. It is separate from the security deposit and rent. It is not returned when the lease ends.

### When Key Money Applies

Key money is common but not universal in Bangkok. You will encounter it in several situations:

**High-demand locations.** If a space on Thonglor or in a popular Charoen Krung building attracts multiple interested tenants, the landlord may charge key money as a price for securing the space over other bidders. This is a market premium — the space is worth more than the rent alone reflects.

**Transfer of an existing business.** When you take over a space from a previous tenant, the outgoing tenant may charge key money for the "goodwill" of the location — the built-in customer awareness, foot traffic patterns, and established presence. This is common in food-court and shophouse contexts.

**Landlord premium.** Some landlords simply charge key money as an additional revenue line. It is particularly common with older Thai landlords who own individual shophouses or small commercial buildings. There is no economic justification beyond the landlord's expectation of receiving it.

### When Key Money Is Worth Paying

Key money is worth paying when the location has demonstrated, measurable value that justifies the premium. Specifically:

- The space has proven foot traffic (you have counted it yourself — see my article on [finding a restaurant location](/blog/how-to-find-restaurant-location-bangkok))
- The previous tenant's business was profitable (and you can verify this through their actual numbers, not just their claims)
- The space includes fixtures, equipment, or buildout that you would otherwise need to pay for
- The location is genuinely scarce — there are no comparable alternatives within your target zone
- The key money amount, when amortized over your lease term, represents less than 5-8% of your total occupancy cost

### When to Walk Away From Key Money

Walk away if any of these apply:

- The key money exceeds 6 months of rent with no tangible justification
- The landlord cannot or will not explain what the key money is for
- The space needs significant renovation (you are paying a premium for a location and then spending millions more to make it usable)
- You have not independently verified the foot traffic and customer demand
- The outgoing tenant is charging key money for "goodwill" but has been losing money

<Callout type="warning">
Key money is non-refundable. If your restaurant fails in year one, you do not get key money back. Treat it as a sunk cost in your financial model, not an asset. I have seen operators pay 1.5 million THB in key money for a "perfect" location, spend another 3 million THB on renovation, and close within 18 months. The key money was the first 1.5 million THB lost.
</Callout>

### Negotiation Tactics for Reducing Key Money

Key money is almost always negotiable. Here is what works:

**Offer a longer lease commitment.** A landlord who receives 6 years of guaranteed rent is more valuable than one who receives 3 years plus key money. Offer a longer initial term (or a firm commitment to the renewal period) in exchange for reduced or eliminated key money.

**Propose a higher rent instead.** Some landlords want cash upfront; others want predictable income. If the landlord prefers the latter, offer 5-10% higher monthly rent in exchange for zero key money. The total cost over the lease may be similar, but you preserve your working capital.

**Pay in installments.** If you cannot negotiate key money down, negotiate the payment terms. Paying over 3-6 months instead of upfront reduces the cash flow impact on your launch.

**Show your business plan.** A credible, well-presented business plan signals that you are a reliable tenant who will pay rent consistently and maintain the property. Landlords with a long-term mindset will accept lower key money from a strong tenant over higher key money from a risky one.

## Lease Structures: 3+3 and Why It Matters

Understanding lease structures in Bangkok requires understanding one critical piece of Thai property law: **any lease with a term exceeding 3 years must be registered at the Land Department to be legally enforceable.** This is governed by Section 538 of the Thai Civil and Commercial Code.

Registration involves fees (1.1% of total rent over the lease term), requires landlord cooperation, and creates a public record. Many landlords prefer to avoid this — which is why the 3+3 structure exists.

### The 3+3 Year Lease

The most common restaurant lease structure in Bangkok is two consecutive 3-year terms:

- **Initial term:** 3 years (does not require Land Department registration)
- **Renewal term:** 3 additional years (exercised by the tenant, does not require registration because it is technically a new 3-year lease)

The catch: the renewal is only as good as the terms written into the original contract. If your lease says "the tenant may renew for an additional 3 years at rent to be mutually agreed," you have no protection. The landlord can demand any rent increase at renewal, and if you cannot agree, you are out.

<Callout type="tip">
Always negotiate the renewal rent terms explicitly in the original contract. The strongest position is a renewal clause that specifies either (a) the exact rent for the renewal period, or (b) a formula such as "current rent plus CPI plus 2%" or "current rent plus a maximum of 10% for the renewal term." Never accept "rent to be mutually agreed" — this gives the landlord all the leverage at renewal time.
</Callout>

### The 3+3+3 Year Lease

For larger investments — restaurants requiring 3 million THB or more in fit-out — I recommend pushing for a 3+3+3 structure (9 years total). This gives you three full renewal cycles to amortize your investment. The same principle applies: each renewal term must have pre-agreed rent or a clear formula.

A 9-year total term is particularly important for fine dining and heavily designed concepts where the renovation investment is 5-10 million THB or more. You need at least 5-7 years of operation to recover that investment through profits. A 6-year lease is tight. A 9-year lease gives you breathing room.

### Registered Leases (3+ Years)

If the landlord is willing to register the lease at the Land Department, you get a single-term lease of up to 30 years (for foreigners; Thais can go longer through leasehold structures). A registered lease is the strongest form of protection because:

- It is enforceable even if the property is sold to a new owner
- It cannot be terminated early except for cause as specified in the lease
- The terms are publicly recorded and unambiguous

The cost of registration: the tenant typically pays a registration fee of 1.1% of total rent over the lease term. On a 5-year lease at 180,000 THB per month, the total rent is 10,800,000 THB and the registration fee is approximately 118,800 THB. This is money well spent for a long-term commitment.

<Callout type="info">
For foreigners leasing commercial property in Thailand, the maximum registerable lease term is 30 years with an option to renew for an additional 30 years. In practice, most restaurant leases are 3-9 years. If your business plan requires a longer guaranteed term (for example, a heavily invested venue that needs 10+ years to generate adequate returns), a registered lease is essential. Consult a Thai property lawyer.
</Callout>

### Annual Rent Escalation

Almost every Bangkok commercial lease includes an annual escalation clause — a percentage increase in base rent each year. This is where landlords build in inflation protection and where tenants can get trapped.

**Typical escalation rates:**

- **3% per year:** Common and reasonable. On a 100,000 THB/month starting rent, you pay 103,000 in year 2, 106,090 in year 3, and so on. Over 6 years, your final-year rent is approximately 116,000 THB/month.
- **5% per year:** The upper end of acceptable. On the same 100,000 THB starting rent, your year-6 rent reaches approximately 128,000 THB/month — 28% higher than your starting rent.
- **7-10% per year:** I have seen these in high-demand locations. They are almost always unacceptable for restaurants. A 10% annual escalation on 100,000 THB/month puts your year-6 rent at approximately 161,000 THB/month — a 61% increase that will destroy your margins.

**Negotiation tactics for escalation:**

- **Cap the escalation.** Even if you agree to 5% annual increases, insist on a cumulative cap — for example, "annual escalation of 5% per year, not to exceed 20% cumulative over the initial 3-year term."
- **Negotiate a rent-free ramp.** Ask for the first year at a fixed rate with escalation starting in year 2. This gives your restaurant time to build revenue before rent increases begin.
- **Tie escalation to CPI.** Thailand's Consumer Price Index is published by the Ministry of Commerce. An escalation clause tied to CPI (for example, "CPI plus 1%, minimum 2%, maximum 5%") protects both parties from arbitrary increases.
- **Negotiate a flat rent for the first term.** Some landlords will agree to flat rent for the first 3-year term with an escalation step at renewal. This is the simplest and most predictable structure for financial modeling.

<Callout type="tip">
Always model your lease economics at the maximum escalation rate for the full lease term. If your restaurant's financial model does not work at year-6 rents with full escalation, the lease terms need to change or the location is wrong. Too many operators model based on year-1 rent and discover in year 4 that escalation has eaten their margin.
</Callout>

## Critical Lease Clauses Every Restaurant Operator Must Understand

Beyond rent and term, there are specific clauses in Bangkok commercial leases that make or break restaurant operations. Here are the ones I negotiate hardest on.

### Fit-Out Period (Rent-Free Period)

The fit-out period is a window of time — typically between lease signing and the start of rent payments — during which you can renovate and prepare the space without paying rent. You do pay for utilities during this period.

**What to negotiate:** 2-3 months of rent-free fit-out time is standard for a full restaurant build-out. If the space requires significant structural work (new grease traps, electrical upgrades, HVAC installation), push for 3-4 months. Every month of rent-free fit-out saves you 60,000-200,000 THB depending on the rent level.

**Common landlord pushback:** "We will give you 1 month rent-free." One month is not enough for any restaurant renovation beyond cosmetic work. If the landlord will not provide adequate fit-out time, consider whether the space is worth the cost of paying rent during construction.

### Restoration Clause

The restoration clause specifies what condition the space must be in when you return it at the end of the lease. There are three common versions:

**"Return to original condition."** This means you must remove all your installations — kitchen equipment, grease traps, flooring, wall finishes, lighting, everything — and return the space to bare shell. This can cost 200,000-800,000 THB depending on the extent of your build-out.

**"Return in good condition, fair wear and tear accepted."** This is the tenant-friendly version. You remove your equipment but leave the space in reasonable condition without stripping it back to bare shell. Costs are typically 50,000-200,000 THB for cleaning and minor repairs.

**"No restoration required; improvements become landlord's property."** The best-case scenario for tenants. You walk away and the landlord keeps whatever you built. This is uncommon but worth asking for, especially if your build-out improves the property value.

<Callout type="warning">
Never sign a lease without understanding the restoration clause. I worked with a restaurant owner who spent 4.2 million THB on a beautiful build-out, operated for 5 years, and then faced a 650,000 THB restoration bill because the lease required "return to original condition including removal of all plumbing modifications." The grease trap removal alone cost 180,000 THB. Negotiate this clause before signing, not on the way out.
</Callout>

### Early Termination

What happens if your restaurant fails and you need to exit before the lease ends? The early termination clause defines the penalty.

**Typical penalty:** 3-6 months of rent. On a 150,000 THB/month lease, that is 450,000-900,000 THB to exit early — on top of forfeiting your security deposit and potentially paying restoration costs.

**Negotiation targets:**

- Reduce the penalty to 2-3 months of rent
- Include a "break clause" at a specific point (for example, after 18 months) with reduced or no penalty
- Negotiate the right to sublease or assign the lease to a new tenant (which effectively eliminates the early termination cost if you find a replacement)

### Subletting and Assignment Rights

**Subletting** means renting all or part of your space to another tenant while remaining on the original lease. **Assignment** means transferring the entire lease to a new tenant.

Both rights are critical exit options. If your restaurant concept changes or fails, the ability to assign the lease to a new operator means you can recover some of your investment instead of paying an early termination penalty.

Many Bangkok leases prohibit subletting and assignment without landlord consent. Negotiate for "consent not to be unreasonably withheld" — this prevents the landlord from blocking a reasonable assignment.

### Exclusive Use Clause

An exclusive use clause prevents the landlord from leasing other spaces in the same building or complex to a directly competing food business. This is most relevant for restaurants in mixed-use buildings, shophouse clusters, and small commercial developments.

**What to negotiate:** "No additional food and beverage tenants serving primarily [your cuisine type] within the same building/complex during the lease term." Be specific about what constitutes competition. "No other restaurants" is too broad and landlords will not agree. "No other Thai fine dining restaurants" or "no other Japanese ramen restaurants" is specific and defensible.

### Operating Hours Requirements

Some leases — particularly in mixed-use buildings and mall-adjacent properties — include mandatory operating hours. A clause requiring your restaurant to be open 7 days a week, 11 AM to 10 PM, may not match your concept or staffing model.

Negotiate operating hours that align with your business plan. A dinner-only concept should not be contractually obligated to serve lunch. A restaurant that closes on Mondays for kitchen maintenance should not be penalized for doing so.

### Signage Rights

Exterior signage is advertising. If your lease does not include signage rights — the right to put your restaurant name, logo, and directional signs on the building exterior — you may end up in a beautiful space that nobody can find.

Negotiate specific signage rights: location, size, illumination, and approval process. In shophouse buildings, signage is usually straightforward. In multi-tenant buildings, signage rights are shared and the landlord controls placement.

### HVAC and Grease Trap Responsibilities

**HVAC (air conditioning):** Clarify who is responsible for HVAC maintenance, repair, and replacement. In many Bangkok commercial buildings, the HVAC system is centralized and the landlord handles maintenance, but charges tenants a monthly "common area" or "air conditioning" fee that can be 100-300 THB per square meter per month — a significant additional cost on top of rent.

**Grease traps:** Bangkok municipal code requires all food establishments to have grease traps. Clarify who installs the grease trap (tenant or landlord), who maintains it (always the tenant), and who is responsible for any plumbing modifications needed to connect it. Grease trap installation costs 30,000-150,000 THB depending on size and plumbing complexity.

### Insurance Requirements

Many leases require tenants to maintain specific insurance: property insurance for the fit-out, public liability insurance, and sometimes business interruption insurance. Annual premiums for a typical Bangkok restaurant run 15,000-40,000 THB. Budget for this.

## Commercial vs. Residential Zoning: A Critical Check

Operating a restaurant in a space zoned for residential use is one of the most common legal risks in Bangkok, and one that most first-time operators do not know to check.

### The Risk

Bangkok's zoning regulations (controlled by the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration and individual Khet — district — offices) designate areas for residential, commercial, mixed-use, and industrial purposes. Operating a full-service restaurant in a purely residential zone can result in:

- Complaints from neighbors leading to police visits
- Fines from the local Khet office
- Forced closure by municipal order
- Lease termination by the landlord (some leases include clauses allowing termination if the tenant violates zoning)

The risk is particularly high in converted residential buildings, shophouses in residential sois, and ground-floor units of condominium buildings.

### How to Verify Zoning

Before signing a lease for any space, verify the zoning:

1. **Visit the local Khet (district) office.** The Khet's Urban Planning section maintains zoning maps. Ask specifically whether the address is zoned for commercial food service. This is a free inquiry.
2. **Check the building's Ror. 4 certificate (occupancy permit).** The Ror. 4 specifies the approved use of the building. A building with a residential Ror. 4 cannot legally house a restaurant without modification to the permit.
3. **Ask the landlord for documentation.** A legitimate landlord should be able to provide the building's Ror. 4, the land title deed showing zoning, and any condominium juristic person rules that affect commercial use.

### Mixed-Use Buildings and Condominium Rules

If the space is in a condominium building (ground-floor commercial unit), read the juristic person rules carefully. Many condominium juristic persons restrict food businesses due to smell, smoke, pest concerns, and loading dock usage. Some prohibit restaurants entirely. Others allow cafes but not full-service kitchens.

The juristic person rules are a contract between the building management and all unit owners. Even if the landlord (who owns the commercial unit) says "it is fine to operate a restaurant here," the juristic person can override this and force you to close. I have seen this happen twice in Bangkok. Both times, the restaurant had signed a lease, spent millions on renovation, and was forced to shut down within months because the juristic person voted to prohibit food businesses.

<Callout type="warning">
Always verify zoning and building regulations independently of the landlord. A landlord who wants your rent may tell you "there is no problem" when there is a very significant problem. Spend the 500 THB cab fare and 2 hours at the Khet office. It could save you millions.
</Callout>

## Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

In 15 years of negotiating restaurant leases in Bangkok, I have learned to recognize certain signals that indicate a lease will end badly. If you encounter any of these, walk away.

### 1. The Landlord Refuses a Written Lease

Verbal lease agreements are legally enforceable in Thailand for terms under 3 years, but they are nearly impossible to enforce in practice because there is no documentation of the agreed terms. A landlord who insists on a verbal agreement or "just a simple one-page letter" is either unsophisticated or hiding something. Either way, you are exposed.

**Minimum requirement:** A written lease in Thai (the legally enforceable version) with an English translation (for your reference), covering all terms discussed in this article, signed by both parties with witnesses.

### 2. No Clear Ownership Documentation

Ask the landlord to show you the land title deed (Chanote, Nor Sor 3, or Nor Sor 3 Gor) and proof of building ownership. If the landlord cannot produce these, or if the documents show a different owner's name, stop. You may be dealing with a subletter without authority to lease, a property in disputed ownership, or worse.

### 3. The Building Has Structural Issues

Visible cracks in load-bearing walls, sagging floors, water damage on ceilings, or non-functioning elevators are not just renovation problems — they are indicators of deferred maintenance that will create ongoing headaches. A building that cannot maintain its structure will not maintain its plumbing, electrical, or HVAC systems either.

Hire a building inspector (5,000-15,000 THB) to assess the structural condition of any space before signing. This is especially important for older shophouses in Charoen Krung and Yaowarat, where buildings may be 50-100 years old.

### 4. The Previous Tenant Warns You

If you can contact the previous tenant and they warn you about the landlord — slow to maintain the building, hostile during disputes, surprise fees — take it seriously. Previous tenants have no incentive to lie, and their experience is the best predictor of yours.

### 5. Rent Is Suspiciously Below Market

If a space in Thonglor is offered at 600 THB per square meter when the market rate is 1,500-2,000 THB, something is wrong. Below-market rent usually means one of: the space has been vacant for a long time (why?), there are hidden costs (common area fees, air conditioning surcharges), the building has unresolved legal or structural issues, or the lease terms contain unfavorable clauses that offset the low rent.

### 6. The Landlord Wants a Personal Relationship Before Signing

Thai business culture values relationships, and it is normal for landlords to want to meet you and understand your business before signing. But if the landlord explicitly resists putting terms in writing, defers to "we can discuss that later" on key clauses, or wants to "see how it goes" before formalizing the agreement, they are maintaining flexibility at your expense. Everything that matters should be in the signed lease before you spend a single baht on renovation.

### 7. Pending Legal Disputes

Ask directly: "Are there any pending legal disputes related to this property?" Check with the Land Department for any registered encumbrances on the land title. A property involved in an ownership dispute, inheritance claim, or foreclosure proceeding can change hands without your consent, leaving your lease unenforceable.

## Negotiation Tactics That Work in Bangkok

Negotiating a commercial lease in Bangkok has specific cultural and practical dimensions that differ from Western markets. Here is what I have learned works.

### Negotiate Through a Thai Speaker

If you are a foreigner, having a Thai speaker — ideally a Thai business partner, lawyer, or consultant — lead the negotiation is significantly more effective than negotiating in English. This is not just about language; it is about cultural nuance, relationship dynamics, and the ability to read between the lines of what the landlord is saying.

Many older Thai landlords are uncomfortable negotiating with foreigners directly. They may agree to terms in the meeting and then backtrack later. A Thai intermediary can navigate these dynamics and achieve better outcomes.

### Present Your Business Plan Professionally

Bangkok landlords, particularly those who own multiple commercial properties, evaluate tenants as much as tenants evaluate spaces. A professionally presented business plan — including your concept, target market, projected revenue, and renovation plans — signals that you are a serious, reliable tenant.

Include renderings or reference images of your intended design. Show the landlord that your restaurant will enhance their property value. This is a genuine incentive for landlords who care about the long-term value of their building.

### Ask for Comparable Rents

"What do other tenants in this building pay?" is a legitimate question. In multi-tenant buildings, the landlord may have a standard rate card. In single-unit properties, comparable rent data from nearby properties (which your real estate agent should provide) gives you leverage.

If the asking rent is 20% above comparables, you have a factual basis for negotiation. If it is in line with comparables, your negotiation should focus on other terms: fit-out period, escalation, renewal conditions, and key money.

### Negotiate the Total Package, Not Just Rent

Landlords anchor on the rent number because it is the simplest metric. But the total occupancy cost includes rent, escalation, key money, security deposit terms, fit-out period, restoration obligations, common area fees, and signage rights. You can "lose" on rent and "win" on everything else.

For example: accepting 5% higher rent in exchange for 3 months of rent-free fit-out, zero key money, and a tenant-friendly restoration clause may save you 500,000-1,000,000 THB over the lease term compared to a lower-rent deal with unfavorable terms.

### Get Everything in Writing, in Thai and English

The legally enforceable version of your lease is the Thai-language version. The English version is for your reference. Both should say exactly the same thing.

Have the lease prepared in Thai by a lawyer. Have it independently translated to English (do not rely on the landlord's translation). Compare the two versions clause by clause. If there are discrepancies, the Thai version will govern in court.

### Use a Lawyer for Review

A Thai property lawyer will review a commercial lease for 10,000-30,000 THB. This fee covers reading the lease, identifying problematic clauses, suggesting modifications, and potentially participating in a round of negotiation with the landlord.

This is the single best investment in the entire lease process. A lawyer will catch things you will not: ambiguous Thai-language phrasing, missing clauses, escalation calculations that compound differently than you expected, and restoration obligations that are broader than they appear.

<Callout type="tip">
Choose a lawyer who specializes in commercial real estate, not a general practice lawyer. The Thai Bar Association maintains a directory, or ask for referrals from other F&B operators. </Callout>

## Land Department Registration: Process and Cost

If your lease term exceeds 3 years (including any single term, not cumulative with renewals), it must be registered at the Land Department to be enforceable. Here is the process:

### Steps

1. **Both parties agree to registration** — the landlord must cooperate; this cannot be done unilaterally
2. **Prepare the lease in Thai** — the Land Department requires a Thai-language lease in their standard format
3. **Visit the local Land Department office** together (landlord and tenant, or authorized representatives with powers of attorney)
4. **Pay the registration fee** — 1.1% of total rent over the lease term (typically paid by the tenant)
5. **Receive the registered lease** — stamped and recorded, with a copy in the Land Department records

### Cost Example

For a 5-year lease at 180,000 THB per month:
- Total rent: 180,000 x 60 months = 10,800,000 THB
- Registration fee: 1.1% x 10,800,000 = 118,800 THB
- Stamp duty: 0.1% x 10,800,000 = 10,800 THB
- **Total registration cost: approximately 130,000 THB**

### Why Registration Matters

A registered lease survives the sale of the property. If your landlord sells the building, the new owner must honor your registered lease. An unregistered lease does not have this protection — the new owner can potentially terminate your lease and ask you to vacate.

For any restaurant investment exceeding 3 million THB in fit-out, I strongly recommend a registered lease. The 130,000 THB registration cost is insurance against the catastrophic scenario of losing your location mid-lease because the property changed hands.

## The Occupancy Cost Rule: How to Know If You Can Afford the Space

Before signing any lease, apply this fundamental rule from my experience with [restaurant cost analysis](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok):

**Total occupancy cost should not exceed 12-15% of your projected monthly revenue.**

Total occupancy cost includes:
- Base rent
- Common area / maintenance fees
- Air conditioning surcharges (if centralized)
- Amortized key money (key money divided by lease term in months)
- Insurance

If your projected monthly revenue is 1,000,000 THB, your total occupancy cost should be 120,000-150,000 THB per month maximum. If the lease puts you above 15%, either negotiate the rent down, find a cheaper space, or increase your revenue projections with a credible plan for doing so.

## Common Lease Mistakes I See Restaurant Operators Make

### Signing Without a Lawyer

I have already said this, but it bears repeating. A lease review costs 10,000-30,000 THB. A bad lease costs millions. Every single restaurant operator who has come to me after signing a problematic lease has said some version of "I should have had a lawyer look at it."

### Not Counting the True Occupancy Cost

Many operators compare rents between locations without factoring in common area fees, HVAC surcharges, key money, and deposit terms. A space at 1,000 THB per square meter with zero key money and included AC may be cheaper than a space at 800 THB per square meter with 500,000 THB key money and a separate AC fee of 150 THB per square meter.

### Underestimating Renovation Time

If your lease starts on day one but your renovation takes 4 months, you are paying 4 months of rent before you serve a single customer. Negotiate the fit-out period aggressively. Every rent-free month you secure is cash that stays in your working capital.

### Not Checking What Happened to the Previous Tenant

The history of the space matters. If the previous three tenants all closed within 2 years, the space may have structural problems — poor visibility, inadequate parking, hostile building management, or unfavorable lease terms that you are about to inherit. Research the space's history before committing.

### Ignoring the Renewal Terms

The initial 3-year term is the honeymoon period. The renewal is where landlords make their money. If you have not locked in favorable renewal terms in the original contract, you have no leverage when the initial term ends. The landlord knows you have invested millions in the space and cannot easily relocate. They will use that leverage.

## Final Thoughts

A restaurant lease in Bangkok is a multi-million-baht commitment that extends over years. It deserves the same rigorous analysis you give to your concept, your menu, and your financial model. The operators I have worked with who took the time to negotiate properly — who hired lawyers, verified zoning, modeled escalation scenarios, and negotiated the full package of terms — are the ones still operating 5 years later.

The ones who signed quickly because they "loved the space" or "did not want to lose it" are disproportionately represented in my conversations about [why restaurants fail in Thailand](/blog/why-restaurants-fail-thailand).

Take your time. Do the work. Get professional help. The space will either still be available (confirming it was the right deal) or it will be gone (confirming it was not the only option). Either outcome is better than signing a bad lease.
