When two people open a restaurant together, they are usually friends. Sometimes they are married. Occasionally they are family. Almost always, they believe their shared enthusiasm for food and hospitality will be enough to sustain the partnership through whatever comes.

It is not. Enthusiasm is a terrible foundation for a business partnership, and restaurants -- with their relentless daily pressure, thin margins, and emotionally charged decisions -- stress-test partnerships harder than almost any other business type. I have consulted on more than 30 restaurant partnership situations in Thailand, and roughly half of them involved some form of dispute. Some were resolved amicably. Some required lawyers. A few destroyed friendships, marriages, and the restaurants themselves.

This guide is the one I wish every restaurant partner in Thailand would read before things go wrong -- and the one I hand to clients when things already have. It covers why partnerships fail, how to resolve disputes before they become legal battles, how buyouts work, what dissolution looks like in Thai law, and how to protect the business (and yourself) through the process.

## Why Restaurant Partnerships Fail

The reasons are remarkably consistent across every partnership dispute I have seen. The specifics vary, but the underlying patterns repeat.

### Different Visions

This is the most common root cause, and the most insidious because it does not feel like a problem at first. Two partners agree on the concept, the menu, and the location. They open the restaurant and it does well. Then one partner wants to expand to a second location while the other wants to maximize profitability at the current one. One wants to invest in a full renovation while the other wants to take more profit out. One wants to pivot toward a younger crowd while the other wants to maintain the current customer base.

None of these positions are wrong. They are just different, and when two people with equal decision-making authority have genuinely different visions for the business, every strategic decision becomes a negotiation. After enough negotiations, both partners feel like the other person is blocking their ideas, and resentment builds.

### Unequal Effort

Partner A is at the restaurant 60 hours a week. Partner B shows up for 15 hours on weekends. Both own 50% of the business. Partner A starts to feel exploited. Partner B thinks their contribution -- maybe they provided more capital, or they handle the marketing, or they manage the finances -- justifies working fewer hours on-site.

The effort dispute is almost never about hours per se. It is about perceived fairness, and fairness is subjective. What feels like an equitable arrangement on day one often feels deeply unfair two years later when one partner is exhausted from daily operations and the other seems to be enjoying the benefits without sharing the burden.

### Money Disagreements

Money conflicts in restaurant partnerships take predictable forms:

**Salary and draws.** One partner wants a larger salary because they work more hours. The other partner thinks salaries should be equal because ownership is equal. Or both partners are drawing money from the business at a rate it cannot sustain, and neither wants to reduce their draw first.

**Profit distribution vs. reinvestment.** The restaurant is profitable, and one partner wants to distribute 80% of profits while the other wants to reinvest 80% in the business. This is actually a vision disagreement in financial clothing -- the partner who wants distributions may be less committed to long-term growth, or may have personal financial needs the other partner does not understand.

**Spending authority.** Partner A approves a 200,000 THB kitchen equipment purchase without consulting Partner B. Partner B is furious, not because the equipment was unnecessary, but because the decision was made unilaterally. Spending authority is a power issue disguised as a money issue.

### Role Confusion

"We will figure out who does what as we go." I have heard this from partnership teams more times than I can count, and it ends the same way every time: both partners doing some of everything, neither fully owning anything, and critical tasks falling through the cracks because each partner assumed the other was handling it.

Role confusion creates two problems simultaneously. First, operational: things do not get done well because nobody is clearly responsible. Second, relational: both partners feel overworked and underappreciated because there is no shared understanding of who is supposed to do what.

<Callout type="info">
The partnerships that survive are the ones where roles are defined explicitly, preferably in writing, before the restaurant opens. "You handle kitchen and menu, I handle front-of-house and finance" is a simple division, but it works because each partner has a domain and clear accountability. The partnerships that fail are the ones where both partners do a little of everything and neither does anything completely.
</Callout>

## Common Partnership Structures in Thailand

The legal structure of your partnership affects how disputes play out and how they can be resolved. Here are the structures I encounter most often.

### 50/50 Equal Partnership

Two partners each hold 50% of the Thai limited company's shares. This is the most common structure among friends or couples opening a restaurant together -- and the most problematic when disputes arise.

**The core problem:** Deadlock. With equal ownership, neither partner can outvote the other. If they disagree on a major decision and cannot reach consensus, the business is paralyzed. Thai company law does not have a simple mechanism for breaking a 50/50 deadlock without going to court or invoking specific provisions in the shareholders' agreement (which often does not exist).

**Resolution options:** One partner buys the other out, both partners agree to sell the business to a third party, or they go to court and a judge orders dissolution. None of these are quick or painless.

### Majority/Minority Split

One partner holds 51-70% of shares, the other holds the remainder. This avoids deadlock because the majority shareholder has voting control, but it creates a different problem: the minority partner may feel powerless and disengaged.

**Common ratios in Thailand:**
- **51/49:** Functional deadlock prevention with near-equal economic ownership. The 51% partner can technically outvote the 49% partner, but exercising that power frequently destroys the relationship.
- **60/40 or 70/30:** The majority partner is clearly in control. The minority partner is closer to an investor with some operational involvement. This works when roles are clear -- one partner runs the business, the other provides capital and strategic input.

### Active/Silent Partnership

One partner operates the restaurant full-time while the other provides capital and remains uninvolved in daily operations. This is common when a first-time restaurateur partners with a financially stronger investor.

**When it works:** Clear expectations from the start. The silent partner gets quarterly financial reports and profit distributions. The active partner has full operational authority within agreed boundaries. Decision-making authority is documented.

**When it fails:** The silent partner starts micromanaging (they become a "not-so-silent" partner), the active partner makes major decisions without informing the silent partner, or the financial returns do not meet the silent partner's expectations and they want to exert more control.

### Thai/Foreign Partner Dynamic

This is the structure that produces the most emotionally charged disputes, because it involves not just business disagreements but also cultural differences, legal vulnerability, and trust issues that go deeper than money.

Under the Foreign Business Act, most restaurants in Thailand must be majority Thai-owned (at least 51% Thai shareholders). Many foreign operators partner with a Thai national -- sometimes a spouse, sometimes a friend, sometimes a business acquaintance -- who holds the majority shares while the foreign partner provides most of the capital and does most of the work.

**The legal reality for the foreign partner:** Regardless of side agreements, verbal understandings, or how much capital you invested, the Thai partner legally controls the company through their majority shareholding. If the relationship deteriorates, the Thai partner has significant legal leverage.

**Common dispute patterns in Thai/foreign partnerships:**
- The Thai partner wants more control over operations or finances than the foreign partner expected
- The foreign partner feels the Thai partner is not contributing enough to justify their ownership stake
- Cultural differences in business style (Thai indirect communication versus Western directness, different attitudes toward conflict, different expectations about family involvement in the business)
- Trust erosion -- the foreign partner becomes suspicious that the Thai partner is diverting funds or making side deals; the Thai partner resents being treated as a "nominee" rather than a real partner

For the full legal picture of foreign restaurant ownership, see my [foreigner restaurant guide](/blog/foreigner-opening-restaurant-thailand).

<Callout type="warning">
If you are a foreign restaurant owner in Thailand with a Thai partner holding the legal majority, and the relationship is deteriorating, get legal advice immediately. Do not wait until the situation becomes a crisis. A Thai business lawyer who understands both Thai company law and the Foreign Business Act can help you understand your actual position and options before you lose leverage. The cost of early legal consultation (15,000-30,000 THB) is negligible compared to the cost of losing your investment.
</Callout>

## The Shareholder Agreement: What Should Be in It from Day One

If you are reading this before you have entered a partnership, this section alone could save you millions of THB and years of grief. If you are reading this because you are already in a dispute, this section explains what you should have had and what you can try to put in place retroactively.

A shareholder agreement (SHA) is a contract between the partners that governs how the company is run and what happens when the partners disagree. It sits alongside the company's Articles of Association but covers situations that the standard Thai articles do not address.

**What a good restaurant SHA covers:**

**Decision-making authority.** Which decisions each partner can make independently, which require consultation, and which require unanimous agreement. At minimum, define thresholds: Partner A can authorize spending up to 50,000 THB independently; anything above requires both partners' approval. Major decisions (taking on debt, selling assets, changing the concept, opening new locations) require unanimous consent.

**Roles and responsibilities.** Who does what, in writing. Operations, finance, marketing, HR, procurement -- assign primary ownership to one partner with specific accountability metrics.

**Compensation.** Salary or draw for each partner, how it is determined, and under what circumstances it changes. A working partner should typically receive a market-rate salary for their role (what would you pay a hired GM or chef?) in addition to any profit distribution. A non-working partner typically receives only profit distribution.

**Profit distribution policy.** What percentage of net profit is distributed to partners versus reinvested in the business, how frequently distributions happen (quarterly is standard), and what the minimum cash reserve must be before any distribution is made.

**Dispute resolution mechanism.** What happens when partners cannot agree? Options include: mandatory mediation before litigation, a designated third-party tie-breaker (a trusted advisor, accountant, or lawyer), or a defined process for one partner to buy the other out (see "shotgun clause" below).

**Exit provisions.** How a partner can leave the business. This includes:
- **Right of first refusal:** If a partner wants to sell their shares, the other partner gets the first opportunity to buy at the proposed price
- **Shotgun clause (Russian roulette):** Either partner can offer to buy the other's shares at a stated price; the receiving partner must either accept the offer or buy the offering partner's shares at the same price. This forces fair pricing because you do not know which side of the deal you will end up on.
- **Tag-along rights:** If the majority partner sells to a third party, the minority partner can sell their shares on the same terms
- **Drag-along rights:** If the majority partner finds a buyer for 100% of the company, they can compel the minority partner to sell

**Non-compete provisions.** Neither partner can open a competing restaurant within a defined radius and time period while they are a shareholder and for a specified period after leaving.

**Death and disability.** What happens to a partner's shares if they die or become permanently incapacitated. Life insurance policies (with the company as beneficiary) can fund a mandatory buyout.

## Early Warning Signs of Partnership Trouble

By the time partners are shouting at each other in the kitchen, the relationship has been deteriorating for months. The early signs are subtler:

**Avoidance.** Partners stop having difficult conversations. Instead of discussing the declining food cost, the staffing problem, or the strategic disagreement directly, they work around each other. One partner starts making decisions unilaterally because it is easier than having another argument.

**Parallel communication.** Staff receive conflicting instructions from each partner. The kitchen team learns to check with Partner A before doing what Partner B told them to do. This is not just inefficient -- it signals that the partners are not aligned, and staff lose respect for both of them.

**Financial scrutiny.** One partner starts reviewing every expense the other partner approves, questioning purchases that would have been unremarkable six months earlier. This often signals a deeper trust issue that has nothing to do with the specific expense.

**Lifestyle divergence.** One partner buys a new car while the other is reinvesting their salary back into the business. One partner takes a three-week vacation while the other has not had a day off in four months. These visible lifestyle differences amplify the fairness issue, even if the partner's personal spending has nothing to do with the business.

**Outside advisors.** When one partner hires a lawyer or accountant without telling the other, the partnership is already in serious trouble. This is not just preparation -- it is a signal that one partner has mentally shifted from "us" to "me versus them."

**Staff taking sides.** If your employees know which partner they prefer working for and which one they avoid, the partnership dysfunction has already contaminated your team. This is one of the most damaging consequences because it undermines the management structure of the entire restaurant.

## The Money Conversation

Money disputes in restaurant partnerships follow predictable patterns. Understanding them helps you address the real issue rather than arguing about symptoms.

### Salary and Draws

The fundamental question: should partners be compensated for their work, and if so, how?

**My recommendation:** Every working partner receives a market-rate salary for their role. If Partner A works as the GM (a role that would pay 40,000-60,000 THB/month to a hired manager), Partner A should receive that salary regardless of their ownership percentage. If Partner B works as the head chef (a role that would pay 35,000-55,000 THB/month), Partner B receives that salary. Profit distribution is separate and based on ownership percentage.

This approach separates compensation for work from compensation for ownership, which eliminates the "I work harder so I should earn more" argument. Both partners earn for their work at market rates, and both partners share in the profits based on their ownership stake.

### Profit Distribution Disagreements

When one partner wants to distribute profits and the other wants to reinvest, the underlying issue is usually different time horizons.

**The distributor** may be thinking: "I need income now, I have personal financial obligations, and I am not sure this business will last forever. I want to take cash while there is cash to take."

**The reinvestor** may be thinking: "If we invest in the business now, it will be worth more later. Taking money out stunts growth and leaves us vulnerable to competition."

Both perspectives are valid, and the resolution is usually a policy rather than an argument: agree on a minimum reinvestment rate (e.g., 30-40% of net profit stays in the business) and distribute the remainder. This gives the reinvestor the capital they want for growth while giving the distributor the income they need.

### Spending Authority

Set explicit thresholds in your shareholder agreement:

- **Under 20,000 THB:** Either partner can approve independently
- **20,000-100,000 THB:** Both partners must be informed; either can veto within 48 hours
- **Over 100,000 THB:** Both partners must explicitly agree before the purchase

These numbers are illustrative -- adjust them to your business's scale. The point is that unilateral spending decisions are the number one trigger for money-related partnership fights, and a simple threshold system prevents most of them.

<Callout type="tip">
A shared business bank account with two-signature requirements for transactions above a certain threshold is a simple safeguard that prevents unilateral large spending. Most Thai banks offer this for corporate accounts. It adds a small friction to routine operations but can prevent the kind of surprise spending that destroys partnership trust.
</Callout>

## Role and Responsibility Conflicts

When two partners both think they are in charge of everything, nobody is in charge of anything. And when critical tasks (paying the rent, filing the VAT, renewing the alcohol license) have no clear owner, they get missed -- and each partner blames the other.

### Defining Roles Clearly

The cleanest division for a two-partner restaurant:

**Partner A (Operations):** Kitchen management, front-of-house oversight, staff scheduling, quality control, daily operations, vendor relationships, inventory management

**Partner B (Business):** Financial management, [accounting and bookkeeping](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand), legal and compliance, marketing, lease management, strategic planning, partnership and investor relations

Or, for a chef/business partner pairing:

**Chef Partner:** Menu development, kitchen team management, recipe standardization, food cost control, supplier selection (food quality), quality audits

**Business Partner:** Financial management, front-of-house management, marketing, HR and legal, lease and landlord, technology systems, growth planning

Within each domain, one partner has final authority. They consult the other on major decisions, but they make the call. Cross-domain decisions (opening a second location, major renovation, concept change) require both partners' agreement.

### Decision-Making Authority

Document three tiers of decisions:

**Tier 1 -- Independent decisions:** Each partner decides within their domain without needing the other's approval. Examples: menu changes under 10% of total items, staff scheduling, daily supplier orders, routine maintenance under 20,000 THB.

**Tier 2 -- Consultation required:** One partner decides but must inform and consult the other first. Examples: hiring or firing a manager, changing a major supplier, running a promotion over 30,000 THB, equipment purchases 20,000-100,000 THB.

**Tier 3 -- Unanimous agreement:** Neither partner can proceed without the other's explicit agreement. Examples: taking on debt, signing or terminating a lease, changing the concept, opening or closing a location, any single expenditure over 100,000 THB, selling company assets.

## Mediation and Conflict Resolution

If your partnership is in trouble but the restaurant is still operating and both partners want to preserve the business (or at least have a civil resolution), mediation should be your first step. It is faster, cheaper, and less destructive than going straight to lawyers.

### Before It Goes Legal

**Step 1: Acknowledge the problem.** This sounds simple but is the hardest step. Both partners need to admit -- to each other -- that the partnership is not working as currently structured. No blame, no accusations. Just: "We have a problem, and we need to address it."

**Step 2: Bring in a neutral third party.** This can be a professional mediator (available through the Thailand Arbitration Center or private firms), a mutual trusted advisor (an accountant, a mentor, an industry colleague), or even a respected friend who both partners trust. The key is neutrality -- this person must not be perceived as favoring either partner.

**Step 3: Define the issues.** With the mediator's help, list the specific issues -- not vague grievances like "they do not work hard enough" but concrete items: "We disagree on whether to distribute profits this quarter or reinvest in a renovation," or "We have no agreement on spending authority, and three unilateral purchases in the last month caused conflict."

**Step 4: Explore solutions.** For each issue, discuss possible resolutions. Some will be operational (define roles, set spending thresholds, create a distribution policy). Some will be structural (adjust ownership percentages to reflect actual contributions, bring in a third partner to break deadlocks, create an advisory board).

**Step 5: Document the agreement.** Whatever you agree to in mediation, put it in writing and have a lawyer formalize it. Verbal agreements between angry partners have the lifespan of a mayfly.

### Professional Mediation Services in Thailand

The Thailand Arbitration Center (THAC) offers mediation and arbitration services for commercial disputes, including restaurant partnerships. Private mediation firms in Bangkok also handle business partnership disputes. Expect 3-5 mediation sessions over 2-6 weeks for most restaurant partnership conflicts.

**Success rate:** In my experience, mediation resolves about 60% of restaurant partnership disputes in Thailand without escalation to litigation. The 40% that fail at mediation typically involve deep trust breakdowns, allegations of fraud or theft, or situations where one partner has already mentally exited the business and is using the process to negotiate their buyout terms.

## Buyout Structures: How to Value and Buy a Partner's Share

When the partnership cannot be repaired, one partner buying the other out is usually the cleanest resolution. The business survives, one partner exits with fair compensation, and the remaining partner gains full control.

### Valuing a Partner's Share

The partner's share is worth their ownership percentage of the total business value. The challenge is agreeing on the business value, because the selling partner will argue for a higher valuation and the buying partner will argue for a lower one.

**Valuation methods** are the same ones used in [selling a restaurant](/blog/sell-restaurant-thailand):

**EBITDA multiple:** The most common approach. Multiply annual EBITDA by an appropriate multiple (typically 2.0-4.0x for independent restaurants in Thailand), then apply the ownership percentage.

**Example:** Restaurant generates 2.4 million THB/year in EBITDA. At a 3x multiple, the business is valued at 7.2 million THB. A 50% partner's share is 3.6 million THB.

**Asset-based floor:** Calculate the fair market value of all assets (equipment, inventory, lease value, brand), subtract liabilities, and apply the ownership percentage. This sets the minimum acceptable price.

**Independent valuation:** Both partners agree to hire an independent business valuator (typically a licensed CPA firm) who examines the financials, assets, and market position and provides a formal valuation report. This costs 50,000-150,000 THB but provides a neutral number both parties can use as a starting point. Split the cost equally.

**Discount factors in partnership buyouts:**
- **Minority discount (15-30%):** A minority partner's share (under 50%) is worth less per percentage point than a majority share because the buyer is acquiring a non-controlling position. A 30% stake in a restaurant valued at 10 million THB is not worth 3 million THB -- it is worth 2.1-2.55 million THB after the minority discount.
- **Lack of marketability discount (10-20%):** Shares in a private Thai restaurant company cannot be easily sold on a market. This illiquidity reduces value.
- **Key person discount:** If the departing partner is the head chef or primary customer draw, their departure will reduce the business's value. This should be factored into the buyout price.

### Payment Structures

Few partners can write a check for a full buyout on the day of the agreement. Common payment structures:

**Lump sum at closing:** The buying partner pays the full buyout price on the day the shares transfer. Cleanest for both parties, but requires the buyer to have the capital available or obtain financing.

**Installment payments:** The buyer pays a down payment (typically 30-50% of the buyout price) at closing, with the remainder paid in monthly or quarterly installments over 12-36 months. The selling partner retains a lien on the shares until fully paid, providing security.

**Earn-out hybrid:** Part of the price is paid upfront, part is contingent on the restaurant's future performance over 12-24 months. This makes sense when the departing partner's exit might affect revenue -- the buying partner pays less if performance declines, protecting themselves from overpaying.

**Asset offset:** If one partner has personal loans to the business or business credit cards with outstanding balances, these are offset against the buyout price. Similarly, if one partner has taken excessive draws above the agreed amount, that difference is deducted.

<Callout type="info">
The most contentious buyout negotiations I have seen in Thailand were not about the total price -- they were about the payment schedule. The selling partner wants all cash now; the buying partner wants to pay over 3 years. My advice to selling partners: accept installments if the total price is fair and the security is adequate (retained shares, personal guarantee, or collateral). A 3.5 million THB buyout paid over 24 months is better than a 2.5 million THB lump sum that the buyer can barely afford -- because a financially stressed buyer is more likely to default or let the business deteriorate.
</Callout>

### Transition Period

After the buyout agreement is signed, the departing partner should remain available (not necessarily on-site daily) for a transition period:

- **30 days minimum** for introductions to key suppliers, staff reassurance, and knowledge transfer
- **60-90 days** if the departing partner holds significant operational knowledge or customer relationships
- **Compensation during transition:** The departing partner should receive their normal salary or a consulting fee for the transition period. This is separate from the buyout price and should be specified in the agreement.

## Legal Process for Partnership Dissolution in Thailand

When mediation fails and a buyout cannot be agreed upon, the remaining option is legal dissolution -- either restructuring the company or winding it up entirely.

### Company Director Removal

Under Thai law, directors can be removed by a shareholders' resolution at a general meeting. In a 50/50 partnership, this requires extraordinary measures because neither partner has a controlling vote. Options include:

- **Court petition:** Either partner can petition the Thai court to remove the other as director if they can demonstrate malfeasance, breach of fiduciary duty, or conduct harmful to the company. This is expensive (legal fees of 100,000-500,000 THB) and time-consuming (6-18 months).
- **Shareholders' agreement provisions:** If the SHA includes specific removal triggers (e.g., conviction of a crime, breach of non-compete, unauthorized expenditure above a threshold), those provisions can be invoked.

### Share Transfer

Transferring shares in a Thai limited company requires:

1. Board resolution authorizing the transfer
2. Completion of the share transfer form (Bor. Mor. Jor. 6)
3. Filing with the Department of Business Development
4. Updating the shareholder register
5. Payment of stamp duty (0.1% of the transfer value or the par value of the shares, whichever is higher)

If both partners agree to the transfer terms, this is straightforward. If one partner refuses to cooperate, a court order may be needed to compel the transfer.

### Asset Division

If the partnership cannot be resolved through a buyout and the partners agree to close the business, asset division follows Thai company liquidation procedures:

1. **Shareholders' resolution to dissolve** (requires 3/4 majority of votes at an extraordinary general meeting -- impossible in a 50/50 deadlock without a court order)
2. **Appointment of a liquidator** (can be one of the partners or an independent third party)
3. **Settlement of liabilities** (pay all debts, settle employee severance, clear tax obligations)
4. **Distribution of remaining assets** according to shareholding percentages
5. **Deregistration** with the Department of Business Development

**Timeline:** Voluntary liquidation takes 3-6 months in Thailand. Court-ordered liquidation can take 12-24 months.

**Cost:** Legal fees for dissolution run 50,000-200,000 THB for a straightforward case, higher if contested. Liquidation costs (accounting, compliance, asset disposition) add another 50,000-100,000 THB.

## Protecting the Business During Disputes

While partners are fighting, the restaurant still needs to operate. Customers do not care about your partnership problems, and staff become anxious when they sense conflict at the top. Protecting the business during a dispute requires conscious effort.

### Keep Disputes Out of the Restaurant

Do not argue in front of staff. Do not discuss the dispute where customers can overhear. Do not send angry LINE messages about partnership issues in the staff group chat. Handle all dispute-related conversations in private, preferably with a mediator or lawyer present.

Staff who witness partner conflict become demoralized, anxious, and prone to leaving. The best employees -- the ones with options -- leave first. This creates an operational crisis on top of a partnership crisis.

### Non-Compete and Non-Solicitation

If one partner is likely to leave, protect the business from competitive damage:

**Non-compete clause:** The departing partner should be restricted from opening a competing restaurant within a defined radius (typically 3-5 km) for a defined period (typically 2-3 years). This should already be in your SHA; if it is not, negotiate it as part of the buyout agreement.

**Non-solicitation of staff:** The departing partner should be restricted from hiring the restaurant's employees for 12-24 months after departure. This prevents the departing partner from gutting your team on the way out.

**Non-solicitation of customers:** While harder to enforce, a provision preventing the departing partner from actively soliciting your customers (e.g., using the restaurant's customer database to market a competing venue) is reasonable and important.

**Enforceability in Thailand:** Non-compete clauses are generally enforceable in Thai courts if they are reasonable in scope, duration, and geographic area. Overly broad restrictions (10-year non-compete, nationwide, all food-related businesses) will likely be thrown out. Keep the terms proportional.

### Customer Relationships

If the departing partner has strong personal relationships with key customers (common when the departing partner is the front-of-house personality), plan for that transition:

- Introduce key customers to the remaining partner and the new GM
- Frame the departure positively to customers: "Partner B is pursuing a new opportunity; we wish them well and the restaurant continues under Partner A's leadership"
- Increase your own visibility at the restaurant during the transition period
- Do not badmouth the departing partner to customers -- it makes you look bad, not them

### Staff Loyalty

During a dispute, both partners may (consciously or unconsciously) try to build staff loyalty toward themselves. This is destructive. Staff should be loyal to the restaurant, not to individual partners.

**What to do:**
- Reassure staff that their jobs are secure regardless of the partnership outcome
- Maintain normal operations, schedules, and compensation
- Do not ask staff to take sides or report on the other partner's activities
- If one partner is departing, announce it clearly and positively to the full team

## Tax Implications of Buyouts

The tax treatment of a partnership buyout in Thailand depends on the structure.

**Share transfer:** The selling partner pays personal income tax on the capital gain (sale price minus original investment/par value). For Thai residents, this is taxed at the individual's marginal rate (up to 35%). For non-residents, a flat 15% withholding applies. Stamp duty of 0.1% applies to the transfer.

**Asset sale (if the company sells its assets and distributes proceeds):** The company pays corporate income tax on any gain from asset sales (20% CIT rate). VAT at 7% applies to tangible asset sales. Partners then receive distributions that may be subject to dividend tax.

**Company dissolution and liquidation:** Remaining assets distributed to shareholders after settling liabilities are treated as a return of capital (to the extent of original investment) and then as dividend income for any excess. Dividend income is subject to withholding tax.

**Get professional tax advice.** The difference between a well-structured and poorly structured buyout can be hundreds of thousands of THB in unnecessary tax. A Thai tax advisor experienced in small business M&A is worth the 20,000-50,000 THB consultation fee. ## Preventing Disputes: Before You Have One

If you are entering a restaurant partnership or are currently in one that is working well, here is what you can do now to prevent the problems described in this article.

### Get the Shareholder Agreement Done

If you skipped it when you formed the company, do it now. A 30,000-80,000 THB investment in a proper SHA is insurance against a million-THB dispute. Every restaurant partnership in Thailand should have one, and fewer than 20% actually do.

### Schedule Regular Partnership Reviews

Meet quarterly, away from the restaurant, for a structured conversation about:
- How is the partnership working? What is going well? What is not?
- Are roles and responsibilities clear and fair?
- Are we aligned on the strategic direction for the next 6-12 months?
- Is the compensation structure still fair to both partners?
- Are there any unresolved grievances that need to be addressed?

These conversations are awkward, especially when things are going well and there seems to be nothing to discuss. Do them anyway. The issues that surface in a quarterly review over coffee are infinitely easier to resolve than the same issues surfacing in a lawyer's office a year later.

### Build Exit Clauses from the Start

Your SHA should include clear mechanisms for a partner to exit:
- **Voluntary exit:** Either partner can choose to leave, with a defined process for valuing and transferring their share
- **Forced exit:** Specific triggers (fraud, criminal conviction, bankruptcy, breach of SHA terms) that allow one partner to force the other out
- **Deadlock resolution:** If partners cannot agree on a major decision after a defined cooling-off period, a specific mechanism kicks in (mediator, shotgun clause, or pre-agreed third-party arbitrator)

### Maintain Financial Transparency

Both partners should have real-time access to:
- Bank account balances and transactions
- Monthly P&L statements
- POS sales data
- Expense receipts and invoices
- Tax filings and compliance records

Financial opacity breeds suspicion, and suspicion destroys partnerships faster than actual financial problems. If you do not trust your partner enough to share full financial access, you should not be in a partnership with them.

<Callout type="tip">
The single best predictor of a successful restaurant partnership in Thailand is not the concept, the location, or the capital. It is whether both partners can have uncomfortable conversations honestly and without defensiveness. If you cannot tell your partner "I think you are spending too much" or "I feel like I am working harder than you" without it becoming a fight, the partnership has a structural communication problem that will eventually surface as a business problem.
</Callout>

## When to Walk Away

Sometimes the right decision is to leave. Not every partnership is worth saving, and not every restaurant is worth fighting over.

**Walk away when:**

**The trust is gone.** If you genuinely believe your partner is stealing, lying about finances, or acting against the business's interests, and you have evidence (not just suspicion), the partnership is over. You can pursue legal remedies, but the relationship will never be repaired.

**Your health is suffering.** Partnership stress on top of restaurant stress is a health hazard. If you are losing sleep, experiencing anxiety, drinking more, or noticing physical symptoms of chronic stress, the cost of staying may be higher than the cost of leaving. No restaurant is worth your health.

**The fight is costing more than the business is worth.** If legal fees, lost productivity, and emotional energy spent on the dispute are approaching a significant percentage of the business's total value, it is more rational to settle quickly and move on than to fight for a theoretically higher number.

**You have a better opportunity.** If you are staying in the partnership out of fear (fear of losing your investment, fear of starting over) rather than genuine belief that the business and the partnership can improve, that fear is costing you opportunity. The skills, relationships, and knowledge you built in this restaurant are transferable to whatever comes next.

**The business itself is failing.** A partnership dispute in a profitable restaurant is worth resolving. A partnership dispute in a restaurant that is losing money is an argument about who gets to lose more. If the business fundamentals are broken and the partnership is also broken, cut your losses on both simultaneously.

## The Emotional Side

I want to end with this because it is the part nobody talks about, and it is often the part that hurts the most.

Breaking up a business partnership is, emotionally, very similar to a divorce. There is grief, anger, betrayal, self-doubt, and a sense of failure -- even when the split is objectively the right decision. If your partner was also your friend, you are losing a friendship. If your partner was your spouse, you may be losing a marriage alongside a business.

Allow yourself to feel all of that, but do not let it drive your business decisions. The financial and legal aspects of a partnership dispute should be handled by your rational brain (and your lawyer's), not your emotional one. Make the deal that is fair, not the deal that punishes the other person. Sign the agreement that lets you both move forward, not the one that keeps you locked in conflict.

Every restaurateur I know who has been through a painful partnership split has told me the same thing, usually 6-12 months later: "I should have done it sooner." The fear of the process is almost always worse than the process itself, and the freedom on the other side is almost always greater than expected.
