I had a client in Sathorn last year who was three months into his restaurant and panicking. Revenue was 650,000 THB/month, the dining room felt busy on weekends, and he was convinced something was wrong with his accountant because the bank balance kept shrinking.

Nothing was wrong with the accountant. The restaurant needed 780,000 THB/month to cover all its costs. He was losing roughly 130,000 THB every month and did not know it, because he had never calculated his break-even point before signing the lease.

Break-even analysis is the single most underused financial tool in the Thai restaurant industry. It is not complicated -- the formula fits on the back of a napkin -- but it answers the most fundamental question any restaurant owner faces: how much revenue do I need before I stop losing money?

This guide walks through the break-even formula, shows you how to identify your fixed and variable costs in a Thai context, and works through complete examples for three different restaurant types. If you have never done a break-even analysis, this is where you start. If you have done one but it sits in a drawer, this is your reminder to update it.

## What Break-Even Means for a Restaurant

Break-even is the point where your total revenue exactly covers your total costs. Below break-even, you lose money every day you are open. Above break-even, every additional baht of revenue contributes to profit.

The concept sounds obvious, yet most restaurant owners I work with in Thailand cannot tell me their break-even number. They know their rent, they have a rough sense of their food cost, and they hope the rest works out. Hope is not a financial strategy.

Your break-even number gives you three things:

**A daily revenue target.** Instead of vaguely hoping for a "good day," you know exactly how much revenue you need today. Post it in the kitchen. Track it on the POS. When the whole team knows the number, behavior changes.

**A reality check before you open.** If your projected revenue cannot reach break-even within a reasonable timeframe, the concept, the location, or the cost structure needs to change before you invest. This is exactly what we model in a [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) -- and it has saved more than a few clients from signing leases on restaurants that could never work.

**A framework for every financial decision.** Considering a renovation? A new menu? A rent increase? Break-even analysis tells you how each change affects the minimum revenue you need to survive.

## The Break-Even Formula

The basic formula is straightforward:

**Break-Even Revenue = Fixed Costs / Contribution Margin Ratio**

Where:

**Contribution Margin Ratio = (Revenue - Variable Costs) / Revenue**

Or equivalently:

**Contribution Margin Ratio = 1 - Variable Cost Percentage**

Every baht of revenue splits into two buckets: variable costs (which scale proportionally with revenue) and contribution margin (which goes toward covering fixed costs and, eventually, profit). Once total contribution margin equals total fixed costs, you have broken even.

The challenge is not the formula. The challenge is correctly classifying your costs as fixed or variable, because a mistake here throws off your entire calculation.

A few notes on classification in Thailand specifically.

**Rent is fixed, but beware revenue-sharing clauses.** Some mall and shopping center leases in Bangkok include a revenue-sharing component on top of base rent (typically 3-8% of gross revenue once you exceed a threshold). If your lease has this clause, the base rent is fixed while the revenue share is variable. Treat them separately.

**Utility costs are semi-fixed.** A restaurant's electricity bill has a base component (refrigeration, lighting, signage run whether you serve 10 or 100 covers) and a variable component (kitchen equipment, additional AC load during peak). I typically classify 60-70% of utilities as fixed and 30-40% as variable. For a Bangkok restaurant running 35,000 THB/month in electricity, roughly 22,000-24,000 THB is fixed.

**Salaried staff are fixed; daily-rate and part-time staff are variable.** Thailand's labor market makes this distinction natural. Most restaurants employ a core team on monthly salary (fixed) and supplement with part-time or daily-rate workers during busy periods (variable). If all your staff are salaried, your labor cost is almost entirely fixed, which raises your break-even point but gives you more predictable costs.

For a deeper breakdown of how all these costs interact in a restaurant P&L, see my [guide to restaurant financial statements](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand).

## Identifying Variable Costs

Variable costs rise and fall with revenue. Serve more customers, spend more on these line items.

**Food cost (COGS).** The biggest variable cost. Every dish you sell requires ingredients. Typical food cost runs 28-35% of food revenue for most Thai restaurant types. Track this as a percentage, not a flat number, because it scales directly with sales volume.

**Beverage cost.** Same principle as food cost. Typically 18-28% of beverage revenue depending on your mix of spirits, wine, beer, and non-alcoholic drinks.

**Part-time and casual labor.** Daily-rate staff, overtime payments, and extra weekend shifts. These fluctuate with volume.

**Packaging and takeaway supplies.** Every delivery order needs containers, bags, cutlery, and napkins. Typically 4-7% of delivery revenue.

**Credit card and QR payment processing fees.** In Thailand, credit card fees run 1.8-3% of transaction value, while QR/PromptPay payments through banking apps are typically 0-0.25%. Blended processing fees depend on your payment mix -- a tourist-heavy restaurant paying 70% by credit card faces higher processing costs than a local Thai restaurant where 80% of transactions are QR code or cash.

**Delivery platform commissions.** This is the large variable cost that many operators underestimate. Grab and LINE MAN charge 30-35% of the order value. If delivery makes up 20% of your revenue, commissions alone consume 6-7% of your total revenue. I covered the full economics of delivery in my [cash flow management guide](/blog/restaurant-cash-flow-management-thailand).

**Service charge distribution.** The portion of service charge passed through to staff is variable because it scales with dine-in revenue. Most restaurants pass through 80-100% of the 10% service charge.

**Variable utilities.** The 30-40% of your utility bill that scales with kitchen activity and dining room usage.

**Consumables.** Cleaning supplies, napkins, toiletries, and other items that scale loosely with traffic.

## Calculating Contribution Margin

Contribution margin is what remains after variable costs are subtracted from revenue. It is the portion of each revenue baht that "contributes" toward covering fixed costs.

**Per-cover contribution margin** is especially useful for restaurants because it translates the abstract financial concept into something tangible -- how many customers you need per day.

**Formula:** Average Revenue Per Cover - Variable Cost Per Cover = Contribution Margin Per Cover

**Example for a casual Thai restaurant:**
- Average check per cover (food + beverage + service charge): 420 THB
- Variable cost per cover (food cost 32% + beverage cost 4% + variable labor 8% + processing 1% + consumables 1% = 46%): 193 THB
- **Contribution margin per cover: 227 THB**

Every customer who walks through the door and pays their bill contributes 227 THB toward your fixed costs. Once enough customers have contributed enough to cover all your fixed costs for the month, every additional customer's contribution margin becomes profit.

**Contribution margin ratio:** 227 / 420 = 54%

This means 54 cents of every revenue baht goes toward fixed costs and profit, while 46 cents goes to variable costs. A higher contribution margin ratio means you need less revenue to break even.

Different restaurant types have very different contribution margin profiles, which is why a fine dining restaurant with 40 seats can break even with far fewer covers than a casual restaurant with 60 seats. The fine dining restaurant's average check -- and therefore its contribution margin per cover -- is much higher.

Break-Even Revenue = 370,500 / (1 - 0.45) = 370,500 / 0.55 = **673,636 THB/month**

Break-even covers per day = 370,500 / (227 x 30) = **54 covers/day**

<Callout type="tip">
When calculating break-even in covers, remember that not all seats fill evenly. Lunch might average 25 covers while dinner averages 60 covers on the same day. The daily target of 85 does not mean 42.5 per meal period -- it means your total across both periods needs to hit 85. If lunch is consistently weak, your dinner needs to carry more weight.

## How to Lower Your Break-Even Point

A lower break-even point means you need less revenue to stop losing money. There are three levers.

### Lever 1: Reduce Fixed Costs

Every baht you cut from fixed costs directly reduces your break-even revenue by approximately 1.8-2.4 THB (the reciprocal of the contribution margin ratio).

**Negotiate rent.** This is the highest-impact move. A 10,000 THB/month rent reduction in a restaurant with a 55% contribution margin lowers break-even by 18,182 THB/month. That is real money, and [lease negotiation](/blog/negotiate-restaurant-lease-bangkok) is a skill worth developing. Landlords in Thailand are often more flexible than operators assume, particularly for tenants with a strong track record.

**Restructure labor.** Instead of cutting staff, look at the fixed-to-variable ratio. Can some salaried positions become part-time during low-traffic periods? A kitchen that runs 5 salaried cooks at all times might only need 3 during slow weekday lunches, supplementing with 2 part-time workers who come in for dinner. This shifts labor from fixed to variable, lowering the break-even point without reducing total capacity.

**Renegotiate service contracts.** Accounting, pest control, insurance, technology subscriptions -- these small fixed costs add up. Review each annually and negotiate or switch providers. I have seen restaurants save 15,000-25,000 THB/month just by auditing their service contracts.

**Refinance or pay down debt.** Loan payments are fixed costs. If you can refinance at a lower rate or accelerate paydown, you reduce the fixed cost burden. Check with Kasikornbank or Bangkok Bank's SME refinancing programs -- rates have come down in 2026.

### Lever 2: Improve Contribution Margin

Contribution margin improves when you reduce variable cost percentage -- meaning you keep more of each revenue baht after variable costs.

**Reduce food cost.** The most impactful variable cost to optimize. A 2-point food cost reduction (from 33% to 31%) on a restaurant doing 1 million THB/month saves 20,000 THB. The tools: better purchasing, tighter portion control, reduced waste, and [menu engineering](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) to push the sales mix toward higher-margin items.

**Improve your delivery economics.** If delivery commissions are dragging down your contribution margin, consider strategies like: directing repeat customers to phone ordering (0% commission), negotiating preferred partner rates with platforms (some operators get 25-28% instead of 32-35%), or raising delivery menu prices by 10-15% to offset the commission (customers expect this, and platforms allow it).

**Optimize your payment mix.** Every table that pays by credit card costs you 1.8-3% in processing fees. QR code payments through Thai banking apps cost 0-0.25%. Promoting QR payments through table signage and staff scripting can shift your payment mix and save 0.5-1% of revenue in processing fees.

**Reduce waste.** Food waste is pure margin destruction. A restaurant producing 3% waste is losing 3% of its food purchases with zero revenue return. Proper prep planning, FIFO rotation, cross-utilization of trim, and staff meal programs that use excess inventory can bring waste below 1.5%. For restaurants at typical [Thai ingredient cost levels](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality), even a 1-point waste reduction matters.

### Lever 3: Increase Average Check

This is not technically lowering the break-even point -- it reduces the number of covers needed to reach it.

**Beverage attachment.** Increasing the percentage of tables that order drinks from 50% to 70% raises the average check substantially while adding only 20-25% in variable cost (beverage COGS). Beverage has the best contribution margin of any menu category. Train your staff to suggest pairings rather than just ask "anything to drink?"

**Appetizer and dessert attachment.** Same principle. If your average table orders 2 mains and nothing else, adding a shared appetizer and one dessert can increase the check by 25-35%.

**Strategic upselling.** Not aggressive upselling -- strategic. A server who says "the barramundi is excellent today, caught this morning" is upselling through expertise. A server who says "would you like to make that a large?" is annoying.

<Callout type="tip">
The fastest way to lower your effective break-even point is usually a combination of all three levers: a modest rent reduction, a 1-2 point improvement in food cost, and a 50-80 THB increase in average check. None of these individually transforms the business, but together they can reduce break-even by 15-20%. The compound effect is significant.
</Callout>

## Break-Even for New Restaurants: The Pre-Opening Timeline

New restaurants do not break even on day one. Understanding the typical path to break-even helps you plan your cash reserves and set realistic expectations.

**Month 1-2: Opening surge.** Most new restaurants in Bangkok experience a "honeymoon" period with strong curiosity-driven traffic. Revenue may look promising. Do not be fooled -- this traffic is not sustainable. It is fueled by novelty, influencer coverage, and friends-and-family visits.

**Month 3-4: The dip.** The honeymoon ends. Curiosity traffic fades and you have not yet built a reliable base of repeat customers. This is the most dangerous period. Revenue often drops 20-40% from the opening surge. Many operators panic and start discounting, which worsens the margin situation.

**Month 5-8: Building the base.** If your food is good and your operations are solid, word-of-mouth and repeat customers begin building. Revenue stabilizes and starts a slow climb. Most restaurants that will eventually succeed show clear week-over-week growth in this period.

**Month 6-18: Break-even range.** The typical timeline to reach monthly break-even for different types:

- **Cloud kitchen:** 3-6 months (low fixed costs, faster ramp)
- **Casual dining (good location, good concept):** 6-10 months
- **Casual dining (average location or competitive area):** 10-14 months
- **Fine dining:** 8-14 months (slower customer acquisition but higher check)
- **Destination restaurant (poor foot traffic, relies on reputation):** 12-18 months

These timelines assume the concept is sound and the execution is competent. A poorly located restaurant with mediocre food may never break even.

**Cash reserve implication:** If your monthly fixed costs are 400,000 THB and you expect to reach break-even in month 8, you need enough cash to cover the cumulative losses during months 1-7. With revenue ramping from 40% to 90% of break-even over that period, total pre-break-even losses typically range from 1.5 to 3 million THB for a casual dining restaurant. This working capital requirement is separate from your startup investment -- and it is the line item that sinks undercapitalized restaurants.

For a detailed breakdown of startup costs including working capital reserves, see my [cost to open a restaurant in Bangkok guide](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok).

## Break-Even for Menu Changes and Renovations

You can use break-even analysis for any investment decision, not just the overall restaurant.

### Menu Change Break-Even

When you add new dishes or reformulate existing ones, calculate the incremental break-even. If a new dish costs 15,000 THB to develop (recipe testing, photography, menu reprinting) and has a contribution margin of 85 THB per order, you need to sell 177 orders before the development cost is recovered. At 3 orders per day, that is about 2 months.

When you do a full menu overhaul, the costs are higher -- typically 50,000-150,000 THB including food photography, menu design, staff training, and test ingredient purchases. Apply the same logic: how many additional covers or how much additional contribution margin does the new menu need to generate to recover the investment?

### Renovation Break-Even

A 500,000 THB renovation that adds 10 seats to your restaurant changes your break-even equation in two ways: it increases fixed costs temporarily (the investment, depreciated or amortised) while also increasing your revenue capacity.

If those 10 additional seats generate an average of 15 additional covers per day at a contribution margin of 227 THB per cover, the additional monthly contribution margin is roughly 102,000 THB. The renovation pays for itself in about 5 months. That is a strong return.

But if the 10 seats only generate 5 additional covers per day (because your neighborhood does not have enough demand to fill them), the monthly incremental contribution margin is only 34,000 THB, and the payback stretches to 15 months. The renovation might still be worth it, but the decision looks different.

## Seasonal Break-Even Adjustments

Thailand's restaurant industry has distinct seasonal patterns that affect break-even calculations. A single annual break-even number masks important monthly variation.

**High season (November through February).** Tourism peaks, expat social calendars fill up, and cooler weather encourages dining out. Most Bangkok restaurants see revenue 15-30% above their annual average during these months. Tourist-oriented restaurants in Phuket, Chiang Mai, and Koh Samui may see revenue 40-60% above average.

**Shoulder season (March through May, October).** Revenue typically tracks at or slightly below annual average. March is Songkran prep, April is Songkran (mixed impact -- tourist areas surge, business districts empty), and May is the start of rainy season.

**Low season (June through September).** Revenue drops 10-25% below annual average for most restaurants. Tourist areas get hit harder. Rain suppresses walk-in traffic, especially for restaurants without covered access.

What this means for break-even: if your annual break-even revenue is 700,000 THB/month, you may comfortably clear it from November through February while falling short in July and August. The shortfall months consume the surplus from peak months.

**Practical approach:** Calculate break-even separately for high, shoulder, and low seasons. Adjust your cost structure accordingly. Many Bangkok restaurants reduce part-time staff hours during June-September, scale back marketing spend, and negotiate temporary rent reductions with cooperative landlords. The goal is to lower your break-even point during the months when revenue naturally drops.

<Callout type="warning">
Seasonal fluctuations are more extreme outside Bangkok. A Phuket restaurant that generates 2 million THB/month in January might do 500,000 THB in September. If your fixed costs require 900,000 THB/month to break even, you are profitable for 5 months and losing money for 7. This math kills restaurants that were built on high-season assumptions. Budget for the worst month, not the best.
</Callout>

## Break-Even for Delivery vs. Dine-In

Delivery and dine-in have such different cost structures that I always recommend calculating break-even separately for each channel.

**Dine-in channel:**
- Average check: 420 THB
- Variable cost: 40% (food 31%, beverage 4%, variable labor 3%, processing 1%, consumables 1%)
- Contribution margin: 252 THB per cover (60%)
- Allocated fixed costs (rent, salaried FOH, ambiance): ~280,000 THB/month
- Break-even: 280,000 / 252 = 1,111 dine-in covers/month = 37 covers/day

**Delivery channel:**
- Average order: 250 THB
- Variable cost: 68% (food 33%, commission 32%, packaging 5%, variable labor 1%)
- Contribution margin: 80 THB per order (32%)
- Allocated fixed costs (incremental kitchen staff, packaging setup, tablet): ~40,000 THB/month
- Break-even: 40,000 / 80 = 500 delivery orders/month = 17 orders/day

The contribution margin per delivery order (80 THB) is one-third of the dine-in contribution margin per cover (252 THB). This means delivery needs more than three times the volume to contribute the same amount toward fixed costs.

This does not mean delivery is not worth doing. If your kitchen has unused capacity during off-peak hours and your incremental fixed costs for delivery are low, even thin-margin delivery orders contribute positively. But if you are investing in dedicated kitchen staff and equipment specifically for delivery, the break-even threshold is higher than most operators expect.

## Sensitivity Analysis: Stress-Testing Your Numbers

Break-even analysis based on a single set of assumptions is useful. Sensitivity analysis -- testing what happens when those assumptions change -- is where it becomes powerful.

Here are the scenarios I run for every restaurant client:

**Rent increases 10%.** If your rent goes from 85,000 to 93,500 THB/month, fixed costs increase by 8,500 THB. At a 55% contribution margin, break-even revenue increases by 15,455 THB/month. Manageable for most restaurants, but worth knowing before you sign a lease with built-in annual escalation.

**Food cost rises 3 percentage points.** If food cost moves from 31% to 34%, your variable cost percentage increases and your contribution margin ratio drops from 55% to 52%. On fixed costs of 370,500 THB, break-even revenue jumps from 673,636 to 712,500 THB -- an increase of 38,864 THB/month. A 3-point food cost swing costs you almost 40,000 THB/month in additional revenue needed just to break even. This is why food cost monitoring matters so much -- I detailed the controls in my [profit margins guide](/blog/restaurant-profit-margins-thailand).

**You lose your best cook and need to hire a replacement at higher salary.** Replacing a line cook at 15,000 THB/month with one at 18,000 THB increases fixed costs by 3,000 THB. Small impact. But replacing your head chef at 38,000 THB with a new hire demanding 50,000 THB increases fixed costs by 12,000 THB. At 55% contribution margin, that is 21,818 THB/month in additional break-even revenue.

**Delivery commission increases from 30% to 35%.** If your delivery revenue is 200,000 THB/month, the additional 5% costs you 10,000 THB/month in variable costs. That shifts your overall variable cost percentage and raises your break-even. Platforms do adjust commission rates, and you should model the impact before it happens.

**Revenue drops 15% due to a new competitor opening nearby.** This is the most common real-world shock. Your break-even point does not change (costs remain the same), but your distance from break-even shrinks. A restaurant that was generating 1,035,000 THB with a 32% margin of safety above break-even drops to 880,000 THB -- still above break-even but with only a 15% margin of safety. Another shock could push you below.

I run these scenarios as a table: each row changes one assumption while holding others constant. The scenarios that push you below break-even are the ones that need contingency plans.

## Using Break-Even for Pricing Decisions

Break-even analysis should inform your menu pricing, not the other way around.

**The discount trap.** A restaurant with a 55% contribution margin considering a 15% across-the-board discount needs to understand what this means. The 15% price reduction does not reduce revenue by 15% if it drives volume, but the math is less favorable than most operators think.

If your average check drops from 420 THB to 357 THB (15% discount) and your variable costs per cover remain at 193 THB, your contribution margin per cover drops from 227 THB to 164 THB -- a 28% decline in contribution margin. To maintain the same total contribution margin, you need 38% more covers. If your restaurant is already at 70% capacity, you may not have the physical seats to serve 38% more customers.

**Price increases.** Conversely, a 5% price increase with zero volume loss increases your average check to 441 THB while variable costs stay at 193 THB. Contribution margin per cover rises from 227 THB to 248 THB -- a 9% improvement. You could lose 9% of your customers and still break even at the same point.

The question is always whether price changes affect volume, and by how much. In my experience with Thai restaurants, a 5-8% price increase on a menu that has not changed prices in 12+ months typically results in less than 2% customer loss. Most customers do not notice a 20-30 THB increase on a 400 THB check, especially if accompanied by a menu refresh that adds perceived value.

## Common Mistakes in Break-Even Analysis

After reviewing hundreds of break-even calculations from Thai restaurant owners and their accountants, these are the errors I see most.

**Classifying all labor as variable.** If your kitchen staff are on monthly salaries, that labor cost is fixed. It does not go away when the restaurant is quiet. Only daily-rate workers, part-time staff, and overtime are truly variable. Misclassifying salaried labor as variable understates your break-even point, making you think you need less revenue than you actually do.

**Ignoring delivery commissions.** Operators who treat delivery revenue the same as dine-in revenue get a nasty surprise. A restaurant that appears to have a 55% contribution margin actually has a 32% contribution margin on its delivery channel. If delivery is 30% of revenue, the blended contribution margin is closer to 48%, which raises break-even by roughly 15%.

**Using pre-opening revenue projections without adjustment.** The financial model you built before opening used estimates. Your actual food cost, actual labor cost, and actual revenue mix will differ. Update your break-even calculation with real numbers after 3 months of operation. And then again at 6 months. And then quarterly.

**Forgetting to include loan repayments.** Principal repayment does not show up on the P&L (it is a balance sheet item), but it absolutely consumes cash. If you are repaying 25,000 THB/month in loan principal, that is a fixed cash outflow that your revenue needs to cover. Include it in your break-even calculation even though your accountant does not include it in operating expenses.

**Not accounting for owner's salary.** If you work full-time in your restaurant and do not include a market-rate salary for yourself in the fixed costs, your break-even calculation is deceptively optimistic. What happens if you get injured and need to hire a GM? Suddenly you have an additional 40,000-60,000 THB/month in fixed costs that were previously invisible because you were working for free.

**One-time calculations.** Break-even is not a set-it-and-forget-it number. It changes every time your rent adjusts, you hire or lose staff, food prices shift, or you add/remove a revenue channel. Update it quarterly at minimum. The restaurants that track break-even consistently are the ones that avoid getting blindsided by gradual cost creep.

Share it with your accountant and update it together during your monthly financial review. ## Putting It Into Practice

Break-even analysis is not theoretical. It is a daily management tool. Here is how I recommend using it.

**Before you sign a lease:** Model your break-even at the proposed rent and estimate whether the location can generate that revenue. If break-even revenue exceeds what comparable restaurants in the area generate, the location does not work for your cost structure. Walk away or negotiate a lower rent.

**Before you open:** Update the model with actual costs (equipment, staffing, supplier quotes). Calculate how many months of cash you need to cover losses until you reach break-even. Make sure you have that cash plus a 20% buffer.

**During your first year:** Update break-even monthly with actual data. Compare your trajectory to the pre-opening model. If you are falling behind, diagnose whether the issue is revenue (not enough customers) or costs (higher than expected), and adjust.

**Ongoing:** Update quarterly. Use it for every significant financial decision -- price changes, renovations, new hires, delivery channel launches, lease renewals. The number will drift as costs change, and you need to know where it sits at all times.

The restaurant owner in Sathorn I mentioned at the start of this article eventually brought his revenue above break-even. It took renegotiating his lease (saving 12,000 THB/month), reducing food waste (saving about 15,000 THB/month), and a modest menu price increase (adding about 35,000 THB/month in revenue). Total impact: his break-even dropped by about 50,000 THB/month while his revenue grew by 35,000 THB. He crossed into profitability in month 7 and has stayed there since.

None of those changes required genius. They required knowing the numbers.
