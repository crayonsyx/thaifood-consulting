Over 600,000 restaurants closed in Thailand in the past two years. Not 600,000 over a decade — in two years. And roughly 50% of new restaurants close within their first six months of operation.

I have been consulting in Thailand's F&B industry for 15 years. I have helped open restaurants that are still thriving a decade later. I have also watched clients ignore my advice and close within months. The patterns are consistent. The same mistakes show up again and again, regardless of cuisine type, location, or the owner's background.

Here are the 12 most common reasons restaurants fail in Thailand, ranked roughly by how often I see them.

## 1. Undercapitalization

This is the number one killer. Not by a small margin — by a massive one.

Most restaurant owners calculate their budget like this: renovation + equipment + first month's rent + some inventory = total budget. Then they spend everything getting the doors open and have nothing left to cover the 3-6 months of operating losses that every new restaurant experiences.

A casual dining restaurant in Bangkok needs 600,000-1,500,000 THB in working capital after opening — money to pay rent, staff, and suppliers while revenue ramps up. If you spent that money on a fancier fit-out, you are in trouble by month two.

<Callout type="warning">
The rule I give every client: take your estimated total budget, add 20%, and make sure you still have 3 months of operating expenses left over after opening day. If the math does not work, you are not ready to open. Delay and save more money.
</Callout>

I had a client in 2024 who opened a beautiful Italian restaurant in Thonglor. Spent 8 million THB on renovation — imported tiles, custom lighting, a wood-fired oven from Italy. Gorgeous space. They ran out of cash in month three because they had budgeted zero working capital. The restaurant was doing 600,000 THB/month in revenue (not bad for month three), but expenses were 750,000 THB/month. That 150,000 THB/month gap killed them before they could reach breakeven.

## 2. Wrong Location

A restaurant in the wrong location is fighting gravity every single day. No amount of marketing, discounting, or Instagram promotion will compensate for a location that does not generate organic traffic.

Common location mistakes in Bangkok:

**Choosing based on rent alone.** A 40,000 THB/month space in a dead soi off Rama 9 is more expensive than a 120,000 THB/month space on a busy Ari street if the cheap space generates zero walk-in traffic.

**Ignoring accessibility.** Bangkok residents make dining decisions based on convenience. If your restaurant requires a 15-minute walk from the nearest BTS/MRT station or has no nearby parking, you have eliminated most of your potential customers.

**Overestimating foot traffic.** I once scouted a location with a client who said, "This street is always packed!" We counted actual pedestrian traffic on a Thursday evening: 23 people walked past in one hour. That is not packed.

**Choosing a location before choosing a concept.** Your concept dictates your location, not the other way around. A fine dining restaurant needs a different location profile than a quick-service lunch spot.

For a deeper dive on this topic, read: [How to Find a Restaurant Location in Bangkok](/blog/how-to-find-restaurant-location-bangkok)

## 3. No Clear Concept

"We'll serve a little bit of everything — Thai, Western, Japanese, some pizza, maybe sushi." This is not a concept. This is a recipe for a bloated menu, a confused kitchen, wasted inventory, and customers who do not know what your restaurant is.

Bangkok has over 100,000 restaurants. A customer deciding where to eat tonight needs a reason to choose yours. "We have everything" is not a reason. A clear concept means:

- **A defined cuisine or food style** (not three different ones)
- **A target customer** (who specifically are you serving?)
- **A price point** (where do you sit in the market?)
- **A dining occasion** (weekday lunch? Date night? Group celebration?)

The most successful restaurants I have worked with can describe their concept in one sentence. "Modern Thai shared plates for Thonglor professionals, 400-600 THB per person." That is a concept. It tells you the food, the customer, the price, and the occasion.

## 4. Pricing Mistakes

Pricing kills restaurants in two directions: too high, and you have no customers; too low, and you are busy but losing money on every plate.

The more common problem in Thailand — especially among foreign owners — is pricing too low. They see Thai street food at 50-60 THB and think their restaurant prices should be in the same range. But a restaurant with rent, staff, air conditioning, and proper licensing has a completely different cost structure than a street cart.

**The math that matters:** Food cost should be 28-35% of menu price for most restaurants. If a dish costs you 80 THB in ingredients, you need to sell it for 230-285 THB to hit your target margin. If you are selling it for 150 THB because you "want to be affordable," you are subsidizing your customers with your savings.

<Callout type="tip">
Run a full menu costing exercise before you open. Price every dish based on actual ingredient costs from your suppliers, with a target food cost percentage. If the resulting prices feel too high for your location, your concept is in the wrong location — not your prices. Our [menu engineering service](/services/menu-engineering) includes complete menu costing and pricing optimization.
</Callout>

The other pricing mistake is not adjusting prices as costs change. Ingredient prices in Thailand fluctuate significantly — pork prices rose 25% in 2025, and seafood prices vary by season. If your menu prices are static while your input costs are rising, your margins are shrinking every month.

## 5. Partnership Breakdowns

Roughly 70% of the foreign-owned restaurants I have seen close did so because of a dispute between the foreign investor and their Thai partner. Not because of bad food, not because of a bad location — because the partnership fell apart.

Common partnership failure patterns:

**No shareholder agreement.** The partners shook hands and "trusted each other." Then disagreed about reinvesting profits vs. distributing dividends. Or one partner wanted to sell and the other did not. Without a written agreement, there is no mechanism to resolve disputes.

**Unclear roles.** Both partners think they are in charge. Or neither partner is handling the accounting. Or one partner is working 80 hours a week while the other shows up occasionally.

**Financial disputes.** One partner suspects the other of skimming. Or one partner invested more money and feels entitled to more control. Or the Thai partner's family starts making demands on the business.

**Relationship-based partnerships.** A foreign man and his Thai girlfriend open a restaurant together. The relationship ends. The Thai girlfriend legally owns 51% (or more) of the company. The foreigner has no legal recourse without a shareholder agreement.

The fix is simple but uncomfortable: have the hard conversations before you sign anything. Write everything down. Use a lawyer. A shareholder agreement costs 15,000-40,000 THB. A partnership dispute costs millions.

## 6. Ignoring the Numbers

Many restaurant owners — both Thai and foreign — do not track their financials beyond checking the cash register at the end of the day. They have no idea what their actual food cost is, what their labor cost percentage is, or whether individual menu items are profitable.

You need to know these numbers weekly:

- **Revenue** (daily, broken down by food vs. beverage)
- **Food cost percentage** (target: 28-35%)
- **Labor cost percentage** (target: 25-35% including social security)
- **Prime cost** (food + labor — should be under 65% of revenue)
- **Average check per customer**
- **Customer count** (covers per day)
- **Waste and spoilage**

If you do not know your food cost percentage right now, you are flying blind. I have audited restaurants that thought they were running 30% food cost and were actually at 42%. That 12-point gap on 1 million THB/month in food revenue is 120,000 THB/month going straight to waste, theft, or over-portioning.

<Callout type="info">
POS systems like FoodStory, Ocha, or StoreHub cost 1,000-3,000 THB/month and track most of these metrics automatically. If you are still using a cash register and a notebook, you are making it impossible to manage your business properly.
</Callout>

## 7. Staffing Problems

Thailand has an unemployment rate below 1%. Finding good restaurant staff is hard. Keeping them is harder.

The typical problems:

**Paying below market.** Bangkok's minimum wage is 400 THB/day (as of 2026). If you are paying minimum wage for experienced cooks or servers, they will leave for the restaurant down the street that pays 450-500 THB/day. The cost of replacing and training a new employee far exceeds the cost of paying 10-15% above market.

**No training system.** Most Thai restaurants train by having the new hire shadow a senior staff member for a few days. This produces inconsistent quality and builds bad habits. Documented training programs — even simple ones — dramatically improve consistency and reduce turnover.

**Abusive management.** Yelling at staff, deducting pay for mistakes, or playing favorites destroys morale and drives turnover. Thai workplace culture values respect and harmonious relationships. Foreign managers who import aggressive Western management styles lose their best staff first.

**No career path.** A talented commis chef with no prospect of promotion will leave. Create clear roles, pay scales, and advancement criteria. Even in a small restaurant, showing staff that there is a path forward keeps them engaged.

## 8. Copying Instead of Competing

A new Korean BBQ place opens in Thonglor and does well. Within six months, five more Korean BBQ restaurants open in the same area. Four of them close within a year.

This happens constantly in Bangkok. Someone sees a successful concept and assumes they can replicate the result by copying the format. But the first mover had advantages: novelty, word of mouth, the best location, and the initial burst of interest from food bloggers.

By the time you open your copycat version, the market is saturated and customers have moved on to the next trend. You are competing on price in a crowded market with no differentiation.

**The alternative:** Find an underserved niche. Look for customer needs that are not being met. Bangkok has 500 Japanese restaurants but very few authentic Mexican restaurants. There are hundreds of coffee shops but almost no specialty tea houses. The opportunity is in the gap, not in the crowd.

## 9. Overbuilding the Menu

A 60-item menu requires more inventory, more kitchen equipment, more prep time, more staff training, and more waste than a 20-item menu. It also confuses customers and slows down the kitchen.

The restaurants I see thriving in Bangkok typically have 15-25 items on their main menu. They do those items well, with consistent quality, fast execution, and controlled food costs.

A large menu creates several specific problems:

**Higher waste.** More ingredients means more perishables that expire before they are used. A focused menu uses fewer unique ingredients, each at higher volume, which reduces waste.

**Slower kitchen.** A cook who makes 8 different dishes all day gets very good at those 8 dishes. A cook who makes 40 different dishes is mediocre at all of them and slower on every ticket.

**Higher training costs.** Every menu item is a recipe that needs to be learned, practiced, and maintained. More items means more training time and more opportunities for inconsistency.

**Misleading sales data.** With 60 items, your bottom 20 probably sell fewer than 2 orders per day. You are carrying inventory and prep for items that almost nobody orders.

<Callout type="tip">
Start with 15-20 items. Track sales data for 90 days. Drop anything that sells fewer than 3 orders per day. Add new items one at a time and measure their performance. This iterative approach builds a menu that your kitchen can execute well and your customers actually want.
</Callout>

## 10. Ignoring Marketing Until It Is Too Late

"If the food is good, people will come." No, they will not. Not in a city with 100,000+ restaurants. Good food is the baseline, not the differentiator.

The restaurants that survive in Bangkok do some form of marketing from day one:

**Pre-opening:** Build an Instagram/Facebook presence 4-6 weeks before opening. Share construction progress, menu previews, team introductions. By opening day, you should have 500-1,000 followers who are already planning to visit.

**Opening month:** Invite food bloggers and media for a soft launch. In Bangkok, a single well-placed post from a food blogger with 50,000+ followers can fill your restaurant for two weeks. Budget 30,000-100,000 THB for blogger outreach and hosting.

**Ongoing:** Post 3-5 times per week on Instagram. Respond to every review on Google, Wongnai, and TripAdvisor. Run monthly promotions or events. Partner with nearby businesses for cross-promotion.

**Delivery platforms:** If you are on Grab, LINE MAN, or Foodpanda, invest in platform advertising and promotions during your first 3 months. The algorithms favor new restaurants that run promotions and generate order volume.

Marketing is not optional. Budget 3-5% of revenue for ongoing marketing, or 10,000-30,000 THB/month minimum for a casual dining restaurant.

## 11. Lease Problems

Your lease is a legal and financial commitment that lasts 3-6 years. Getting it wrong has consequences that compound every month.

**Lease traps I have seen:**

- **No renewal clause.** The owner opens, builds out the space for 3 million THB, and the lease expires in 3 years with no guaranteed renewal. The landlord raises rent by 50%, and the owner has no option but to accept or walk away from a 3 million THB renovation.
- **Renovation restrictions.** The lease prohibits structural changes, but the owner needs to install a kitchen exhaust duct through the ceiling. Now they need landlord approval, which comes with additional costs.
- **Unclear utility responsibilities.** Who pays for the grease trap installation? The electrical upgrade? The water meter? These should all be specified in the lease.
- **Key money with no protection.** Key money (a lump-sum payment to the landlord beyond the deposit) is common in prime Bangkok locations: 200,000-1,000,000 THB. If you pay key money and the landlord terminates your lease early, you need a contract clause that requires refunding this money.
- **Transferability.** If you want to sell the business, can you transfer the lease to the buyer? Many leases require landlord consent for transfers, and some prohibit them entirely.

**Before signing any lease:** Have a Thai lawyer review it. This costs 10,000-20,000 THB and is the best money you will spend. Pay attention to renewal terms, rent escalation clauses, restoration obligations (what you must fix when you leave), and termination conditions.

## 12. Not Adapting

The restaurant industry in Thailand changes fast. Customer preferences shift, new competitors open, delivery platforms change their commission structures, ingredient costs fluctuate, and new regulations appear.

Restaurants that fail to adapt get left behind. Specific examples:

**Not adding delivery.** Pre-2020, delivery was optional. Now, 30-40% of restaurant revenue in Bangkok comes through delivery platforms. Restaurants that refused to adapt lost a third of their potential revenue.

**Static menus.** A menu that worked in 2024 may not work in 2026. Customer preferences change, ingredient availability shifts, and your cost structure evolves. Review and update your menu every 6-12 months.

**Ignoring feedback.** A string of negative reviews about slow service or inconsistent food is not a personal attack — it is free market research. Restaurants that read, respond to, and act on customer feedback improve. Restaurants that dismiss criticism stagnate.

**Failing to adopt technology.** QR code menus, online reservations, POS integration with delivery platforms, digital payment options. These are not luxuries — they are expectations. Customers who cannot pay with their phone or order online may simply go somewhere they can.

## The Common Thread

If you read through these 12 reasons, you will notice a pattern: most restaurant failures are not caused by bad food. They are caused by bad planning, bad financial management, and bad business decisions.

Cooking great food is a necessary condition for success. It is not a sufficient one. The restaurateurs who survive in Thailand combine culinary skill with financial discipline, market awareness, and operational rigor.

## What You Can Do About It

If you are planning to open a restaurant in Thailand, or if you are running one that is struggling, here is my advice:

1. **Be honest about your financial position.** Do you have enough capital to survive 6 months of losses? If not, do not open yet.
2. **Get your numbers right.** Track food cost, labor cost, and prime cost weekly. If you do not know these numbers, you cannot manage them.
3. **Choose your location with data, not emotion.** Count foot traffic. Analyze the competition. Talk to nearby business owners. Run the financial model before signing the lease.
4. **Write a shareholder agreement.** If you have a Thai partner, this is non-negotiable.
5. **Start lean.** Small menu, minimal staff, controlled costs. Expand after you have proven the concept.
6. **Get help.** A [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) before you invest saves more money than any cost-cutting after you open.
