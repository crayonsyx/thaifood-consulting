The first time I walked through Talad Thai at 3 AM, I was overwhelmed. Hundreds of vendors spread across a complex the size of several football fields, mountains of produce arriving on trucks from every province, buyers shouting prices in rapid Thai, and a chaos that somehow produces remarkably efficient price discovery. I bought too much of everything, had no idea how to evaluate quality at that hour, and half my vegetables wilted before lunch service because I did not understand cold chain.

That was twelve years ago. Since then, I have helped over 40 restaurants in Bangkok build their supplier networks, and the lesson is always the same: your ingredient sourcing strategy is not a logistics problem to solve once and forget. It is an ongoing relationship-management exercise that directly controls your food cost, your consistency, and your ability to put the same quality on the plate every single day.

Bangkok is one of the best cities in the world for restaurant sourcing. The variety of supply channels, from wet markets to multinational distributors to direct farm contracts, gives you options that operators in most cities do not have. But that variety creates its own challenge: knowing which channel to use for which ingredient, when to buy from a market versus a distributor, and how to build a supply network that does not collapse when one vendor has a problem.

This article covers every major sourcing channel available to Bangkok restaurants, how to evaluate and qualify suppliers, the real cost differences between channels, and the operational systems that keep your supply chain running when things go wrong -- because in Bangkok, they will.

## Bangkok's Food Supply Ecosystem

Before getting into specific sources, it helps to understand the four main channels and when each one makes sense.

**Wholesale markets:** The oldest and often cheapest channel. You buy direct from farmers, aggregators, and wholesalers at market prices. Best for fresh produce, herbs, proteins, and staples where you want the lowest possible price and can handle daily purchasing logistics.

**Distributors (Makro, Metro/Siam Makro):** One-stop sourcing with consistent quality and convenient ordering. Higher prices than wholesale markets for fresh goods, but competitive on dry goods, imported products, and packaged ingredients. Best for operators who value convenience and have limited procurement staff.

**Specialty importers:** For ingredients you cannot source domestically or where imported quality is necessary -- European cheese, Japanese wagyu, Italian olive oil, specialty wines. Higher cost, longer lead times, minimum order quantities.

**Direct farm relationships:** Contract growing or direct purchasing from farms for specific ingredients. Best unit price for high-volume restaurants, but requires planning, relationship management, and willingness to commit to volumes.

Most successful Bangkok restaurants use a combination of all four. The mix depends on your concept, your volume, and how much time and staff you can dedicate to procurement.

<Callout type="info">
The 70/20/10 rule works as a starting framework for most restaurants: 70% of your ingredient spend goes through 1-2 primary channels (usually a wholesale market + Makro), 20% goes to specialty suppliers for specific needs, and 10% is flexible for seasonal buying, direct farm purchases, and opportunistic deals. As your operation matures, you adjust the ratio based on data.
</Callout>

## Major Wholesale Markets

### Talad Thai (ตลาดไท)

**Location:** Pathum Thani, approximately 30-40 minutes north of central Bangkok (longer during morning rush hour).

**Hours:** The main wholesale market opens at midnight and runs until 10-11 AM. The busiest and best-priced period is 2-5 AM. Retail sections operate later in the day, but wholesale pricing disappears by mid-morning.

**What to buy here:** Fresh vegetables, fruits, herbs, and Thai staples. Talad Thai is the largest wholesale fresh market in Southeast Asia, handling roughly 8,000-12,000 tons of produce daily. Prices are the lowest you will find anywhere in the Bangkok metro area for domestic produce.

**Price advantage:** 20-40% below Makro on most fresh produce. A kilogram of Thai basil that costs 80-100 THB at Makro might be 40-60 THB at Talad Thai. Green bird's eye chilies run 60-80 THB/kg at Talad Thai versus 100-120 THB/kg at Makro. The savings compound quickly at scale.

**The trade-off:** You need to go yourself or send a trusted buyer at 3-4 AM. Quality varies between vendors, so you need relationships with specific sellers who consistently deliver what you need. There is no invoicing system for tax purposes (most transactions are cash), which complicates your accounting.

**Purchasing tips:**
- Build relationships with 2-3 vendors per product category. Walk the market several times, compare prices and quality, then commit to regular purchasing from the best vendors.
- Bring your own cooler boxes and ice. The ride back to your restaurant in Bangkok traffic without cold chain destroys leafy greens and herbs.
- Pay cash. Some vendors accept transfers, but cash gets you the best prices and fastest service.
- Go on Tuesdays and Wednesdays when the market is less crowded and vendors are more willing to negotiate.
- Bring a Thai-speaking team member if your Thai is not fluent. Price negotiation happens fast and entirely in Thai.

### Or Tor Kor Market (ตลาด อ.ต.ก.)

**Location:** Chatuchak, adjacent to the Chatuchak Weekend Market. Easily accessible by BTS/MRT.

**Hours:** 6 AM to 6 PM daily.

**What to buy here:** Premium Thai produce, organic vegetables, specialty fruits, and curated ingredients. Or Tor Kor is not a wholesale market -- it is a curated retail/semi-wholesale market known for the highest quality domestic produce in Bangkok.

**Price level:** 20-50% above Talad Thai, but the quality justifies it for certain items. Or Tor Kor vendors grade their produce more carefully, and what you see is consistent -- less sorting needed on your end.

**Best for:** Fine dining restaurants and concepts where ingredient quality is the selling point. If your menu charges 800-1,500 THB per main course, the produce at Or Tor Kor matches that positioning. If you are running a 150 THB lunch set, Talad Thai is more appropriate.

**Purchasing tips:**
- Or Tor Kor is excellent for discovering specialty Thai ingredients -- regional fruits, heirloom vegetables, artisan curry pastes -- that you will not find at Talad Thai or Makro.
- Vendors here are more accustomed to restaurant buyers and can arrange regular orders with delivery for consistent accounts.
- Many vendors accept card payment and issue proper receipts, making this easier for your accounting than Talad Thai.

### Khlong Toei Market (ตลาดคลองเตย)

**Location:** Central Bangkok, Khlong Toei district. Very accessible.

**Hours:** Open 24 hours, but wholesale activity peaks between midnight and 6 AM.

**What to buy here:** General produce, proteins (pork, chicken, seafood), dry goods. Khlong Toei is Bangkok's oldest and most central wet market. It is not as large as Talad Thai, but its location makes it practical for restaurants in central Bangkok that cannot justify the drive to Pathum Thani.

**Price level:** 10-20% above Talad Thai but 10-20% below Makro for fresh goods. The middle ground.

**Best for:** Restaurants in central Bangkok (Sukhumvit, Silom, Sathorn, Ploenchit) that need daily fresh produce without the Talad Thai commute. Also good for proteins -- the pork and chicken vendors at Khlong Toei are reliable and competitive.

<Callout type="warning">
Food safety standards at wet markets vary between vendors. Inspect meat and seafood carefully -- check temperature (should be cold to touch, not room temperature), smell, and visual freshness. If a vendor's stall does not have proper refrigeration or ice, walk past. The 10 THB/kg you save on chicken is not worth a food safety incident that closes your restaurant.
</Callout>

### Simummuang Market (ตลาดสี่มุมเมือง)

**Location:** Rangsit, Pathum Thani, not far from Talad Thai.

**Hours:** Wholesale hours are midnight to early morning, similar to Talad Thai.

**What to buy here:** Seafood. Simummuang is Bangkok's primary wholesale seafood market. Fresh fish, shrimp, squid, crab, and shellfish arrive daily from the Gulf of Thailand and the Andaman Sea. Prices are the best you will find in the Bangkok metro area for seafood.

**Price advantage for seafood examples:**
- White shrimp (size 40-50/kg): 220-280 THB/kg at Simummuang vs. 300-380 THB/kg at Makro
- Sea bass (whole): 140-180 THB/kg at Simummuang vs. 200-250 THB/kg at Makro
- Blue crab: 250-400 THB/kg (seasonal) at Simummuang vs. 350-500 THB at Makro
- Squid (whole): 120-160 THB/kg at Simummuang vs. 180-220 THB at Makro

**The trade-off:** Same as Talad Thai -- early morning hours, cash transactions, no formal invoicing, and you need to know what fresh seafood looks like. Bad seafood will ruin your restaurant faster than almost any other procurement mistake.

**Seafood evaluation checklist:**
- **Fish:** Clear, bright eyes (not cloudy or sunken); bright red gills; firm flesh that springs back when pressed; ocean-fresh smell with no ammonia
- **Shrimp:** Firm, translucent shells; no black spots on the head or body; slight ocean smell; legs intact
- **Squid:** White or translucent body; fresh smell; intact tentacles; not slimy
- **Crab:** Heavy for its size (light crabs have less meat); moving if sold live; no cracked shells

## Major Distributors

### Makro (Siam Makro)

Makro is the dominant food service distributor in Thailand, with over 150 locations nationwide. For most restaurant operators, Makro is the default supplier for a significant portion of their ingredient needs.

**What to buy here:** Dry goods (rice, flour, oil, canned products, sauces, spices), frozen proteins, dairy, beverages, disposables, cleaning supplies, and packaged ingredients. Makro's strength is the breadth of selection, consistent availability, and the convenience of one-stop purchasing.

**What not to buy here (if you have alternatives):** Fresh produce and herbs. Makro's fresh produce is adequate but more expensive than wholesale markets, and the selection, while reliable, lacks the variety and peak freshness you get at Talad Thai or Or Tor Kor. Makro's produce is typically 1-3 days older than what you find at wholesale markets because of the distribution chain.

**Pricing structure:** Makro prices are fixed and displayed. There is no negotiation at the shelf level. However, Makro offers several programs for regular restaurant buyers:

- **Makro HoReCa (Hotel, Restaurant, Cafe):** A dedicated program for food service professionals. Register at any Makro location with your business documents. Benefits include access to food service-specific pack sizes, occasional bulk pricing, and a dedicated HoReCa zone in larger Makro locations.
- **Makro PRO delivery:** Order online for delivery to your restaurant. Minimum order typically 1,500-2,000 THB. Delivery within 1-2 days for most Bangkok locations. Convenient but removes your ability to inspect products before purchasing.
- **Volume pricing:** For large regular orders (50,000+ THB/month), Makro's account team can arrange preferential pricing on specific products. Contact the store manager and ask for a HoReCa account review.

**Payment terms:** Cash, debit, credit card, or corporate account with 30-day terms (requires credit approval).

<Callout type="tip">
Makro's own-brand products (ARO brand for food service, Savepak for disposables) are typically 15-25% cheaper than national brands with comparable quality. We run ARO cooking oil, soy sauce, and rice at several client restaurants with no customer-facing quality difference. Test them before committing, but do not dismiss store brands automatically.
</Callout>

### Lotus's (formerly Tesco Lotus) and Big C

These hypermarkets are not primary restaurant suppliers, but they fill gaps. When you need 20 kg of onions at 8 PM because your regular supplier shorted you, Lotus's and Big C are open and stocked.

**When to use them:** Emergency purchases, small quantities of specific items, and specialty consumer products that your regular suppliers do not carry. Their prices for bulk items are typically 5-15% above Makro.

**Not for regular purchasing:** The prices, pack sizes, and product mix are designed for household consumers, not food service. Using Lotus's as your primary supplier means paying a retail premium on everything.

### Metro / Siam Makro Professional

Metro rebranded and merged its Thai operations with Siam Makro. The Metro wholesale model -- larger pack sizes, fewer SKUs, lower prices -- is being integrated into Makro's professional offering. For practical purposes in 2026, think of Makro as the combined entity.

## Specialty Importers

If your menu includes European cheese, Japanese wagyu, Italian cured meats, specialty olive oils, or premium wines, you need relationships with Bangkok's importers. The domestic supply chain does not cover these products, and what passes for "imported" at Makro is often limited to commodity-grade versions.

### Cheese and Dairy

**Bon Fromage:** The go-to cheese importer for Bangkok's restaurant scene. They carry French, Italian, and artisanal European cheeses with proper cold chain from origin. Minimum orders are typically 3,000-5,000 THB. They deliver weekly to Bangkok restaurants.

**Dairy Home:** Domestic Thai dairy (including an excellent buffalo mozzarella operation) plus imported butter and cream. Competitive pricing, and the Thai-produced items eliminate import markup.

**Makro's import section:** Carries commodity cheeses (cheddar blocks, processed mozzarella, cream cheese) at competitive prices. Fine for pizza operations and applications where the cheese is a component, not the star.

**Pricing reality:** A kilogram of genuine Parmigiano-Reggiano runs 1,200-1,800 THB through specialty importers. The "Parmesan" at Makro costs 400-600 THB/kg but is a generic hard cheese, not regulated DOP product. Your menu positioning and price point should determine which you use.

### Meat and Wagyu

**Meat Premium Thailand:** Premium beef importer carrying Australian wagyu, USDA Prime, and Japanese A5 wagyu. Japanese A5 wagyu runs 8,000-15,000 THB/kg depending on cut and marble score. Australian wagyu (MB4-6) is 1,200-2,500 THB/kg. They supply most of Bangkok's high-end steak restaurants.

**Australian Trade (Aus Trade) and various beef importers:** Multiple companies import Australian grass-fed and grain-fed beef for the mid-market restaurant segment. Prices for grain-fed striploin run 600-900 THB/kg, which is more accessible for casual dining steak programs.

**Local pork and chicken:** Thailand produces excellent pork and chicken domestically. Betagro, CP Foods, and S-Pure are the major domestic producers with restaurant distribution networks. CP and Betagro both have food service sales teams that can set up direct accounts with weekly delivery and 30-day payment terms. Domestic chicken breast runs 90-130 THB/kg; premium antibiotic-free options like S-Pure run 140-180 THB/kg.

### Wine, Spirits, and Beverages

**Wine:** Major importers include Wine Connection (also operates their own restaurant chain), BBC (Bangkok Beer & Beverages Company), and several smaller specialists. Wine import duties and excise tax in Thailand are high -- expect a 200-400% markup from FOB price to landed cost. A bottle that costs 8 EUR at the winery in France retails for 800-1,500 THB in Bangkok.

**Spirits:** Bangkok's cocktail bar scene has driven an expansion of specialty spirit importers. Companies like Spirits Co., L&P Distribution, and Diageo's Thai operation supply restaurants and bars. Payment terms for spirits are typically stricter (COD or 15-day terms) because of the high product value and excise tax obligations.

### Organic and Specialty Produce

**Royal Project (โครงการหลวง):** Thai royal initiative that produces high-quality temperate vegetables, fruits, and processed products at farms in northern Thailand. Products are available at Royal Project shops and selected supermarkets. Quality is excellent and prices are fair, though availability fluctuates seasonally.

**Organic farms and cooperatives:** Several organic farms deliver directly to Bangkok restaurants. Rai Ruen Rom, Harmony Life, and the Thai Organic Trade Association network connect farmers with restaurant buyers. Expect to pay 30-50% above conventional prices for certified organic produce. Minimum orders are typically 1,000-2,000 THB with weekly delivery.

**Imported produce:** Bangkok's international community supports a network of importers bringing in Japanese fruits, Korean vegetables, European berries, and other specialty produce. Villa Market and Gourmet Market carry these at retail, but for restaurant volumes, contact the importers directly -- companies like Fresh Produce International and Asia Fruit Co. supply restaurants at wholesale prices, usually 20-30% below retail.

## Direct Farm Relationships

For restaurants with the volume, the planning capability, and the willingness to invest in relationships, buying directly from farms offers the best unit price and the most control over ingredient quality.

### How It Works

You identify a farm (or group of farms) that produces what you need, negotiate a supply agreement covering volume, price, delivery schedule, and quality standards, and commit to purchasing a set quantity regularly. The farm gets a guaranteed buyer; you get below-market pricing and first pick of the harvest.

### Contract Growing

Some restaurants take it further and contract farms to grow specific varieties or quantities. A Thai-Italian restaurant I work with contracts a farm in Kanchanaburi to grow San Marzano tomatoes and Italian basil. The farm dedicates a section of their land to these crops, the restaurant commits to buying the entire output at an agreed price, and both sides benefit from predictability.

**Contract growing works when:**
- Your volume justifies dedicated farm output (you need 50+ kg/week of a specific ingredient)
- The ingredient is central to your concept and quality differentiation
- The variety or specification is not readily available through standard channels
- You can commit to a 6-12 month purchasing agreement

**Contract growing does not work when:**
- Your volume is small (under 20 kg/week for most crops)
- You need 50 different ingredients from 50 different farms (complexity kills)
- You cannot predict your needs 2-3 months in advance (farms need lead time)
- You are not willing to absorb output variation (a bad weather week means less yield, and you still need to source from somewhere)

### Finding Farm Partners

**Provincial agricultural offices:** Every Thai province has an agricultural extension office that connects farmers with buyers. Contact them directly or through the Department of Agriculture's website.

**Farmer cooperatives:** Organized groups of farmers that pool production and sell collectively. Cooperatives handle logistics and quality grading, which simplifies your procurement.

**The Royal Project network:** Beyond their retail presence, Royal Project farms welcome direct restaurant partnerships for consistent volume.

**Your existing market vendors:** The people selling at Talad Thai often have direct farm connections. Ask your regular vendor if they can source specific items directly from their farm network at better prices for committed volume.

**Organic farmer networks:** The Thai Organic Trade Association and the Green Net cooperative connect organic farmers with restaurant and retail buyers.

<Callout type="info">
Direct farm relationships take 3-6 months to establish and test. Start with one or two high-volume ingredients where the price difference makes a meaningful impact on your food cost. For most restaurants, this means their primary protein or their highest-volume produce item. Do not try to go direct on everything at once.
</Callout>

## Online Supplier Platforms

Bangkok's restaurant procurement scene has moved partially online, with several platforms connecting suppliers and restaurant buyers.

**Happy Fresh for Business / Line Man Mart for Business:** Consumer delivery platforms that also offer business ordering for restaurants. Convenient for small, urgent orders but not price-competitive for regular procurement.

**Makro PRO:** Makro's online ordering platform for registered business accounts. Full product catalog, delivery within 1-2 days, and the ability to reorder from purchase history. The most practical online option for regular restaurant procurement. Their app tracks your spending by category, which helps with [food cost analysis](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).

**Direct supplier LINE accounts:** Many Bangkok food suppliers manage orders through LINE Official Accounts. You add them as a contact, send your order via chat, and they deliver. This is how a large portion of restaurant procurement actually works in Thailand -- informal, relationship-based, and conducted through a messaging app. It works, but it leaves no paper trail for accounting and no easy way to compare prices across suppliers.

**Freshket:** A B2B fresh produce platform connecting farms and restaurants. They handle logistics, cold chain, and quality grading. Pricing sits between wholesale market rates and Makro, with the convenience of app-based ordering and next-day delivery. Minimum orders are typically 500-1,000 THB.

## How to Evaluate and Qualify Suppliers

Not every supplier who offers a good price deserves your business. Here is how to evaluate them properly.

### The Trial Order

Before committing to any new supplier, place three trial orders over two weeks. Check for:

- **Quality consistency:** Was the product the same quality all three times, or did it vary?
- **Delivery reliability:** Did they deliver when promised, or were they late? Even once?
- **Order accuracy:** Did you receive exactly what you ordered, or were items substituted, shorted, or wrong?
- **Packaging and cold chain:** Were temperature-sensitive items properly chilled during transport?
- **Communication:** When you had a question or issue, could you reach someone? How fast did they respond?

One bad delivery out of three is a red flag, not a fluke. Suppliers show you their best during the trial period. If they are inconsistent now, it only gets worse once you are a committed account.

### Documentation Requirements

For any supplier you plan to use regularly, collect:

- **Business registration documents:** Verify they are a legitimate registered business
- **Food safety certification:** GMP, HACCP, or relevant Thai FDA certifications for processed food suppliers
- **Product specifications:** Written specifications for every product you order -- size, grade, origin, packaging, shelf life
- **Pricing agreement:** Written (even if informal via LINE) price list with validity period. Verbal pricing changes without notice are a common source of food cost creep
- **Delivery terms:** Agreed delivery days, time windows, minimum orders, and penalty for missed deliveries

### Quality Scoring

Create a simple monthly scorecard for each supplier tracking:

- **Quality score (1-5):** Based on product consistency across all deliveries that month
- **Delivery reliability (%):** Percentage of deliveries that arrived on time and complete
- **Price competitiveness (1-5):** Based on periodic price comparison across channels
- **Communication (1-5):** Responsiveness when issues arise

Review quarterly. Drop suppliers who consistently score below 3 in any category. Reward suppliers who score above 4 with increased volume.

<Callout type="tip">
Keep your supplier scorecard in a shared document that your procurement staff and head chef can both access. The chef cares about quality; the procurement officer cares about price and reliability. When both perspectives inform supplier decisions, you get better outcomes than when either side decides alone.
</Callout>

## Negotiating Payment Terms

Payment terms affect your cash flow as much as ingredient prices affect your food cost. Here is how payment terms work in Bangkok.

### Standard Payment Terms by Channel

**Wholesale markets:** Cash on delivery. No exceptions for new buyers. After 3-6 months of regular purchasing, some vendors extend informal credit (order today, pay on next visit), but this is relationship-based, not contractual.

**Makro:** Cash, card, or corporate account with 30-day terms. The 30-day account requires credit approval and is worth setting up once your monthly spend exceeds 50,000 THB -- it improves your cash position by holding 30 days of inventory cost.

**Specialty importers:** Typically 15-30 day terms for established accounts. New accounts start at COD or prepayment. After 3-6 months of reliable payment, you can negotiate 30-day terms.

**Direct farm suppliers:** Usually COD or weekly settlement. Farms operate on thin margins and need cash flow. Offering faster payment (COD instead of 30 days) is a legitimate negotiation lever for better pricing.

**Domestic producers (CP, Betagro):** 30-day terms standard for accounts with adequate volume. Some offer early-payment discounts (2% discount for payment within 10 days).

### Negotiation Tactics

**Volume commitment:** The most effective negotiation lever. "I will guarantee 200,000 THB/month in orders if you give me 30-day payment terms and a 5% volume discount." Suppliers prefer predictable revenue over higher per-unit margins.

**Multi-product bundling:** "I will buy my chicken, pork, and cooking oil from you exclusively if you give me a better rate across all three." Consolidating spend with fewer suppliers gives you leverage.

**Seasonal prepayment:** For seasonal items (lychee, durian, mango, certain seafood), offering prepayment 1-2 months before peak season can lock in prices before seasonal spikes. This is how experienced Bangkok operators secure [stable pricing during volatile periods](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality).

**Reference accounts:** If you operate multiple restaurants or know other operators, offering referrals in exchange for better terms works. "Give me this rate and I will introduce you to three other restaurant owners I know."

## Quality Control and Food Safety

Bangkok's heat, traffic, and informal supply chain create food safety risks that do not exist in cities with stricter cold chain infrastructure. Your quality control procedures compensate for these systemic gaps.

### Receiving Inspection

Every delivery gets checked before acceptance. Train your receiving staff (usually the prep cook or kitchen helper who arrives first) on this procedure:

**Temperature check:** All refrigerated items should arrive below 5 degrees C, and frozen items below -18 degrees C. A digital probe thermometer (200-500 THB at any kitchen supply store) is mandatory equipment. Check at least 2-3 items per delivery.

**Visual inspection:** Check for damage, discoloration, and off-odors. Produce should be fresh with no wilting, bruising, or mold. Proteins should have proper color and no sliminess. Dry goods packaging should be intact with no pest damage.

**Weight verification:** Weigh random items against the invoice. Consistent shortages of 3-5% are common with some suppliers -- they either over-count intentionally or their scales are off. Either way, you are paying for product you are not receiving.

**Expiry date check:** All packaged goods should have adequate remaining shelf life. Reject anything within 30% of its expiry date unless you will use it within days.

**Reject and document:** If a delivery fails inspection, refuse it immediately and document the issue (photo + written note). Contact the supplier the same day. Do not accept substandard deliveries with the intention of "mentioning it later" -- once you accept, your leverage disappears.

### Storage Standards

**Cold storage capacity:** Your walk-in cooler (or reach-in refrigerators for smaller operations) needs enough capacity for 2-3 days of inventory. Running at 95% capacity with no buffer means you cannot accept an early delivery or a bulk purchase without compromising storage temperatures.

**FIFO (First In, First Out):** Label everything with a received date and use older stock first. This sounds basic, but I have walked into dozens of Bangkok restaurant kitchens where there is no labeling system and product rotation is random. The result is waste, inconsistency, and food safety risk.

**Temperature monitoring:** Keep a temperature log for all cold storage. Check and record twice daily -- morning and evening. A simple clipboard log works, or invest in a digital temperature monitoring system (3,000-8,000 THB) that alerts you if temperatures drift out of range.

<Callout type="warning">
Bangkok's average temperature of 30-35 degrees C means your cold chain has zero margin for error. A delivery truck stuck in traffic for an extra 45 minutes, a cooler door left ajar during prep, or a power outage during the afternoon -- any of these can push temperatures into the danger zone. Build your procedures assuming the worst case, not the best.
</Callout>

## Managing Multiple Suppliers

A typical Bangkok restaurant works with 8-15 suppliers. Managing that many relationships, deliveries, quality checks, and payments requires systems.

### Supplier Map

Create a document listing every supplier with:

- **Supplier name and primary contact** (name, phone, LINE ID)
- **Products supplied** and agreed pricing
- **Delivery schedule** (days and time windows)
- **Payment terms** and payment due dates
- **Backup supplier** for each product category

Post this in the kitchen and update it monthly. When a new prep cook starts, this document is part of their orientation.

### Order Calendar

Map out your weekly ordering schedule so you know which suppliers to contact on which days. A typical pattern:

- **Sunday:** Order dry goods and frozen items from Makro (delivery Monday)
- **Monday/Wednesday/Friday:** Fresh produce from Talad Thai or Freshket (next-day delivery)
- **Tuesday:** Seafood order from Simummuang vendor (delivery Wednesday)
- **Thursday:** Specialty imports and dairy (delivery Friday or Saturday)
- **Daily:** Protein from CP/Betagro distributor (next-day delivery)

Assign one person to own the ordering calendar. In small restaurants, this is the head chef. In medium operations, a dedicated purchasing officer. The [staffing guide](/blog/restaurant-staffing-thailand) covers this role in the team structure section.

### Purchase Order Records

Even if your ordering happens via LINE chat (which it probably does), keep a record of every purchase order with: date, supplier, items ordered, quantities, agreed prices, and delivery date. A simple Google Sheet works. This record lets you:

- Track spending by supplier and category over time
- Catch price increases that were not communicated
- Compare actual spend against budget
- Identify opportunities to consolidate or switch suppliers
- Provide your accountant with clean procurement data for [financial reporting](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand)

## Building Backup Supplier Relationships

Every supplier will fail you at some point. The question is not if, but when. A produce supplier's truck breaks down, a seafood vendor gets shorted by their fisherman, a specialty importer has a customs delay. You need backups.

### The 80/20 Approach

For each major ingredient category, maintain one primary supplier (who gets 80% of your volume) and one backup supplier (who gets 20%). The backup supplier receives enough regular business to stay engaged and prioritize you when you need to scale up their volume.

Do not wait until your primary supplier fails to contact the backup. Place regular small orders so they know your requirements, have your account set up, and can respond quickly when you need emergency supply.

### Emergency Sources

Know your emergency purchasing options for critical ingredients:

- **Makro:** Open until 9 PM, carries most staples. Your emergency fallback for nearly everything.
- **Lotus's / Big C:** Late-night options for basic produce and proteins.
- **Nearby restaurant allies:** Build relationships with neighboring restaurant operators. I have borrowed 10 kg of chicken breast from the restaurant next door during a supplier emergency. Reciprocate when they need something. This informal network is one of the underrated advantages of being part of a restaurant community.
- **Your own inventory buffer:** Keep a 1-2 day safety stock of critical items (your top 5 ingredients by volume). This buffer costs you 3,000-10,000 THB in tied-up inventory, but it prevents the scenario where a single supplier failure forces you to 86 your most popular dish during dinner service.

## Seasonal Sourcing Challenges

Thailand's tropical climate and distinct wet/dry seasons create predictable -- but still challenging -- fluctuations in ingredient availability and price.

### Peak Season Pricing

Certain ingredients spike dramatically during specific periods:

- **Songkran (April):** Pork and chicken prices rise 10-20% as demand for holiday cooking surges. Order early and stock up.
- **Vegetarian Festival (October):** Vegetable prices spike 15-30% due to increased demand. Tofu and mushroom prices can double.
- **Rainy season (June-October):** Leafy green prices fluctuate wildly. A flood in a major growing province can double prices overnight.
- **Mango season (March-June):** Mango prices drop to their lowest. This is when to run mango-heavy specials and desserts.
- **Durian season (April-July):** Durian prices drop from 300-500 THB/kg off-season to 100-200 THB/kg at peak. Plan your durian menu items accordingly.

For a comprehensive breakdown of seasonal pricing patterns, see our [ingredient cost and seasonality guide](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality).

### Menu Flexibility

The best protection against seasonal price swings is a menu that can flex. Design 2-3 "seasonal swap" positions on your menu where the ingredient changes based on what is available and well-priced. A salad that features mango in April and pomelo in December uses the same kitchen workflow but adapts to the market.

Your [food cost control](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok) system should flag when any ingredient exceeds its budgeted cost by more than 15%. When that happens, you either absorb the increase (temporary), adjust portion sizes, adjust prices, or swap to an alternative ingredient. The decision depends on how long the price increase will last and how central the ingredient is to the dish.

## Cold Chain Logistics in Bangkok Heat

This deserves its own section because it kills more product and causes more food safety issues than any other supply chain factor in Bangkok.

### The Problem

Bangkok's average daytime temperature is 33-36 degrees C, traffic means deliveries spend 30-90 minutes in transit, and many suppliers use unrefrigerated trucks or motorcycles for last-mile delivery. A box of shrimp that left the supplier at 2 degrees C can reach 10+ degrees C by the time it arrives at your kitchen -- above the food safety threshold.

### Your Cold Chain Standards

**Insist on refrigerated transport** for all proteins, dairy, and seafood. This is non-negotiable for any supplier you use regularly. If they deliver by motorcycle, require insulated bags with ice packs at minimum.

**Specify delivery time windows.** Early morning deliveries (before 9 AM) expose products to less heat and less traffic time. Avoid midday deliveries for temperature-sensitive items.

**Receive and refrigerate immediately.** Do not leave deliveries sitting on the loading dock or kitchen floor while staff finishes another task. The receiving person's first job is to check temperature, inspect quality, and get everything into cold storage within 15 minutes of arrival.

**Ice is cheap, waste is expensive.** A 25 kg bag of ice costs 30-40 THB. A spoiled delivery of shrimp costs 5,000+ THB. Stock ice and use it generously for any product that might lose cold chain during transit or receiving.

### Minimum Order Quantities and Storage

This is the tension point of Bangkok restaurant procurement: wholesale markets and distributors offer better prices at higher volumes, but you need storage capacity to hold the inventory, and you need to use it before it spoils.

**Dry goods:** Order in bulk. They do not spoil quickly, storage is cheap (shelving in a dry room), and the price advantage of buying 50 kg bags of rice versus 5 kg bags is 20-30%. Make sure you have proper shelving, pest control, and FIFO rotation.

**Frozen goods:** Order in bulk if you have adequate freezer space. Frozen proteins, prepared sauces, and frozen seafood keep for months. The price advantage of buying a full case versus individual packs is 10-20%.

**Fresh produce:** Order frequently in smaller quantities. The price advantage of buying a full crate of tomatoes is real, but if you cannot use 20 kg before they soften, you are paying for waste. Match order quantities to actual consumption, based on your POS data and recipe yield calculations.

**Dairy and deli:** Short shelf life means small, frequent orders. Better to pay 5% more per unit for 3 deliveries per week than to buy in bulk once a week and throw away 20% of it.

## Cost Comparison: Market vs. Distributor vs. Direct

The pricing differences between channels are significant enough to move your food cost percentage by 3-5 points -- which translates directly to profit.

### Scenario: Monthly Ingredient Spend of 300,000 THB

**All from Makro:** 300,000 THB (baseline). Convenient, consistent, properly invoiced for tax purposes.

**70% Makro + 30% wholesale market:** Approximately 270,000-280,000 THB. The 30% you shift to wholesale markets (fresh produce, herbs, proteins) comes at 20-30% below Makro pricing. Monthly savings: 20,000-30,000 THB.

**50% Makro + 30% wholesale market + 20% direct/importer:** Approximately 255,000-270,000 THB. Adding direct farm purchases and importer relationships for high-volume specialty items saves another 10,000-15,000 THB.

**Total potential savings:** 30,000-45,000 THB per month, or 360,000-540,000 THB per year. That is 1-2 months of rent for many Bangkok restaurants. The savings are real, but they come with increased procurement complexity -- more vendors to manage, more deliveries to receive, more relationships to maintain, and more time spent on purchasing.

### When the Savings Are Not Worth It

For restaurants spending under 100,000 THB/month on ingredients, the time and effort of managing 8-10 suppliers for marginal savings may not justify the complexity. At this volume, a Makro-centered strategy with one or two market runs per week is simpler and nearly as cost-effective.

The breakeven point where multi-channel procurement clearly pays off is around 150,000-200,000 THB/month in ingredient spend. Below that, keep it simple. Above that, the savings fund a part-time procurement role.

## Building Your Supplier Network: A 60-Day Plan

If you are [opening a new restaurant](/blog/how-to-open-restaurant-thailand) or rebuilding your procurement from scratch, here is the sequence.

**Week 1-2: Research and visit**
- Visit Talad Thai, Khlong Toei, and Or Tor Kor to understand prices and product quality
- Visit Simummuang if your menu is seafood-heavy
- Register for a Makro HoReCa card
- Identify 3-5 specialty importers relevant to your concept
- List every ingredient your menu requires and categorize by channel

**Week 3-4: Trial orders**
- Place trial orders with 2-3 vendors per major category
- Evaluate quality, delivery reliability, and communication
- Compare pricing across channels for your top 20 ingredients by spend
- Select primary and backup suppliers for each category

**Week 5-6: Setup and negotiation**
- Finalize supplier agreements (pricing, delivery schedule, payment terms)
- Set up your ordering calendar and purchasing records
- Brief your kitchen team on receiving inspection procedures
- Establish cold storage protocols and temperature logging

**Week 7-8: Optimize**
- Run your first month of full operations and track actual versus budgeted food cost
- Identify the 5 ingredients where channel switching would save the most money
- Adjust supplier mix based on real data
- Begin conversations with farms for potential direct relationships on high-volume items

By the end of 60 days, you should have a functioning supplier network with primary and backup sources for every ingredient category, clear procurement procedures, and enough data to start optimizing.

Your supplier network is the foundation your entire menu stands on. Every dish that leaves your kitchen depends on someone, somewhere, having delivered the right ingredient at the right quality at the right time. Get this right and your kitchen runs smoothly, your food cost stays in range, and your customers get the consistency they expect. Get it wrong and you spend every morning scrambling, every service improvising, and every month wondering where your margin went.
