I walked into a restaurant in Ratchada last year and counted 147 items on the menu. Thai food, Japanese food, Western food, an entire page of smoothies, and a dessert section with 14 options. The owner was proud of this -- "we have something for everyone," she told me. She was also losing 180,000 THB per month.

That menu was not a strength. It was the root cause of nearly every problem in the business: a walk-in cooler full of ingredients expiring before they could be used, a kitchen that took 35 minutes to push a ticket during peak service, food quality that ranged from decent to embarrassing depending on what you ordered, and a training burden so large that new hires needed three weeks before they could work a station alone.

We cut the menu to 48 items. The owner nearly cried. Six months later, her food cost had dropped from 38% to 29%, average ticket time was down to 14 minutes, waste had been reduced by 65%, and the restaurant was profitable for the first time in two years.

This is the story I tell every client who resists reducing their restaurant menu size. A smaller menu is not less. It is more -- more profit, more consistency, more speed, and more sanity for everyone in the kitchen.

## The Oversized Menu Problem in Thailand

Thai restaurant culture has a built-in bias toward large menus, and understanding why is important before we talk about how to fix it.

**The "have everything" tradition.** Traditional Thai restaurants -- the ones that have operated for decades -- tend to offer extensive menus because Thai dining is communal. A table of six wants variety: a curry, a stir-fry, a soup, a salad, something fried, maybe a grilled dish. The assumption is that more menu items equals more options for the table, which equals more satisfied customers. This was reasonable when restaurants had small, experienced kitchen teams that had been making the same 80 dishes for 20 years. It is less reasonable when you are a new restaurant with a team that has been together for four months.

**Competition anxiety.** Bangkok has over 100,000 restaurants. When the Thai-Japanese fusion place down the street has 90 items and you have 30, some owners feel they are losing a competition they never entered. "Customers might want something we do not have" is a fear that leads to menu bloat. The irony is that the restaurant with 30 focused items almost always outperforms the one with 90 mediocre items on every meaningful metric.

**The tourist buffet mentality.** Restaurants in tourist areas often expand their menus to cover multiple cuisines because international visitors have diverse preferences. The logic seems sound -- if a group of four includes someone who does not eat Thai food, you need a burger on the menu to capture that table. In practice, this means you need burger ingredients, a grill station, a cook who can make a burger, and the quality suffers because your kitchen is trying to be everything.

**Delivery platform pressure.** Grab and LINE MAN reward restaurants with more items because it increases the chances of matching a customer's search query. A restaurant with pad thai, pad see ew, and 15 other noodle dishes appears in more search results than one with 3 noodle dishes. This algorithmic incentive pushes menus larger, regardless of whether the kitchen can execute the additional items well.

<Callout type="info">
The oversized menu problem is not uniquely Thai, but it is more common here than in most markets. In Japan, a typical ramen shop has 5-8 items. In Italy, a trattoria might have 20. In Thailand, a casual restaurant with fewer than 40 items is considered "limited." This cultural baseline makes simplification harder -- but the financial benefits are the same regardless of culture.
</Callout>

## The Real Cost of Menu Bloat

A large menu feels free to maintain. It is not. Every additional menu item carries hidden costs that compound across your entire operation. Let me break them down.

### Inventory Complexity and Waste

A 40-item menu might require 80-100 unique ingredients. A 100-item menu requires 200-250. Each additional ingredient needs to be ordered, stored, rotated, and monitored for freshness. The more ingredients you carry, the more likely some of them will expire before they are used, especially low-turnover items that exist for a single dish.

On a restaurant spending 400,000 THB/month on food purchases, the difference between 4% waste and 10% waste is 24,000 THB/month going into the bin. That is 288,000 THB per year -- enough to fund a full menu engineering project, a kitchen renovation, or a significant staff raise.

### Slower Kitchen, Longer Ticket Times

Every dish on your menu is a set of steps that the kitchen needs to execute. A small menu means the kitchen team performs a limited number of operations, repeatedly, at high speed and consistency. A large menu means the team performs many different operations, each infrequently, at lower speed and with more errors.

I timed ticket-to-table during peak service at 12 Bangkok restaurants across different menu sizes:

**15-25 items:** Average ticket time of 8-12 minutes. The kitchen is fast because every cook is making the same dishes repeatedly. Muscle memory kicks in. Prep is simple. The line runs smoothly.

**40-60 items:** Average ticket time of 14-20 minutes. The kitchen handles most tickets well, but complex or infrequent orders slow things down. Occasional bottlenecks during peak service.

**80-100 items:** Average ticket time of 22-30 minutes. Cooks spend time looking up recipes, searching for ingredients, and switching between very different preparations. Peak service becomes chaotic. Errors increase. Remakes increase.

**100+ items:** Average ticket time of 28-40+ minutes. The kitchen is essentially improvising during peak service. Quality is unpredictable. The expeditor is constantly managing exceptions rather than maintaining flow.

Longer ticket times do not just frustrate customers. They reduce your table turns per service, which reduces revenue per seat per hour, which is one of the most important metrics in the restaurant business.

### Inconsistent Quality

A cook who makes pad kra pao 40 times a day produces a consistent, well-executed pad kra pao. A cook who makes pad kra pao 3 times a day -- because the other 37 tickets were spread across 50 different dishes -- produces a more variable product. Consistency is the compound interest of restaurant success, and large menus work against it.

The inconsistency problem is worse for dishes that require specific skills or techniques. A complex curry paste ground to order, a precisely seared fish, a delicate dessert -- these dishes depend on repetition for quality. On a large menu, the kitchen rarely gets enough repetition on any single dish to reach peak execution.

### Training Burden

Every menu item is a recipe that needs to be learned, practiced, and maintained to standard. A 30-item menu requires training a new cook on 30 recipes. A 100-item menu requires 100. That is not a linear increase in training time -- it is exponential, because each recipe interacts with kitchen workflow, ingredient prep, station setup, and timing.

In practice, restaurants with very large menus do not actually train staff on all items. They train on the "common" ones and hope for the best on the rest. This produces a two-tier quality system where popular dishes are competent and unpopular dishes are mediocre, which is exactly the wrong outcome because a customer who orders a rarely-sold dish and receives a mediocre version judges the entire restaurant by that experience.

<Callout type="warning">
The training burden has a second-order cost: it increases your vulnerability to staff turnover. When a cook who knew 80 recipes leaves, the replacement needs months to reach the same level. With a 30-item menu, that ramp-up period shrinks to weeks. In a country with sub-1% unemployment, reducing your dependency on institutional knowledge is a survival strategy.
</Callout>

## How to Identify Items to Cut

This is where data replaces opinion. You do not cut items based on what you like or what you think customers want. You cut based on what the numbers say.

### Sales Mix Analysis

Pull 90 days of POS data and calculate the following for every menu item:

- **Units sold per week** (average)
- **Selling price**
- **Food cost per portion** (from your recipe costing cards)
- **Contribution margin** (selling price minus food cost)
- **Total weekly contribution** (contribution margin x units sold)
- **Contribution margin percentage** ((selling price - food cost) / selling price)

Sort by total weekly contribution, highest to lowest. This ranking tells you which items are actually driving your profitability and which are dead weight.

### The 80/20 Rule Applied to Menus

In nearly every restaurant I audit, the numbers follow a predictable pattern: approximately 20% of menu items generate 75-85% of total contribution. The remaining 80% of items share the leftover 15-25%.

I analysed the POS data from a 72-item Thai restaurant in Silom last year. The top 15 items (21% of the menu) generated 79% of total contribution. The bottom 30 items (42% of the menu) generated just 6% of total contribution. Those 30 items collectively sold fewer than 50 portions per week across all of them combined -- less than 2 orders per day per item.

Those bottom 30 items were not generating meaningful revenue, but they were consuming refrigerator space, requiring ingredient purchases, demanding prep time, adding training complexity, and creating waste. Removing them was not a loss. It was removing a burden.

### Complexity Score

Not all items are equally difficult to produce. A som tum (green papaya salad) takes 3 minutes of active prep and uses ingredients common to many other dishes. A whole steamed sea bass with lime and chilli takes 20 minutes of cooking time, requires a whole fresh fish (which must be used that day or wasted), and ties up a steamer that could be producing other items.

Assign a complexity score to each item based on:

- **Active prep time** (in minutes)
- **Cooking time** (in minutes)
- **Number of unique ingredients** (ingredients not shared with other dishes)
- **Equipment requirements** (does it need a specialised station or equipment?)
- **Skill level required** (can any cook make it, or only senior cooks?)

Items with high complexity and low sales are the strongest cut candidates. They consume disproportionate resources for minimal return.

### Ingredient Overlap Analysis

Map every ingredient on your menu to the dishes that use it. Identify ingredients that appear in only one dish. These single-use ingredients are waste risks -- if that dish does not sell enough to use up the ingredient before it expires, you are throwing money away.

In the Ratchada restaurant I mentioned at the beginning of this article, 34 of their 147 items contained at least one ingredient used in no other dish. Those 34 items accounted for 11% of food purchases but only 4% of revenue. Cutting those items alone reduced food cost by 2.5 percentage points.

<Callout type="tip">
If you want to keep a dish that has a unique ingredient, find a way to use that ingredient in at least one other dish. A menu item that requires lemongrass is fine because lemongrass appears in soups, curries, and stir-fries. A menu item that requires truffle oil and nothing else on the menu uses truffle oil is a waste risk. Either add truffle oil to another dish or cut the item.
</Callout>

## Optimal Menu Size by Restaurant Type

After working with restaurants across every segment in Thailand, here are the menu sizes I recommend:

**Fine dining (25-35 items).** Tasting menus can be even smaller (8-12 courses). Fine dining sells experience and execution, not variety. Every item should be meticulously crafted, and the smaller menu allows the kitchen to achieve this.

**Upscale casual (30-45 items).** Enough variety for a diverse table while maintaining quality and speed. This is where most Bangkok restaurants in the 400-600 THB average check range should land.

**Casual Thai dining (35-55 items).** This is the sweet spot for the traditional Thai restaurant format. Enough categories (soups, curries, stir-fries, salads, rice dishes, noodles) to cover the communal dining format, while staying manageable for the kitchen. If you are above 55, you almost certainly have items that should be cut.

**Fast casual (15-25 items).** Speed and consistency are the priorities. Think noodle shops, rice-and-curry spots, and single-concept restaurants. The tighter the menu, the faster the throughput.

**Food court stalls (8-15 items).** Maximum simplicity. A food court stall trying to offer 30 items will be slow, inconsistent, and wasteful. Focus on what you do best and execute it perfectly.

**Cloud kitchens (12-20 items per brand).** Delivery food needs to be fast to prepare, travel well, and have tight ingredient overlap. Cloud kitchens that try to run 40+ items per brand typically have [high food cost and slow execution](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok) that destroys delivery platform ratings.

## How to Cut Without Losing Customers

The fear that drives menu bloat is the fear of losing customers. "If I remove the fish and chips, the table with the one person who wanted fish and chips will go somewhere else." This fear is real but usually overestimated. Here is how to manage the transition.

### Gradual vs. Dramatic

**Gradual approach:** Remove 10-15 items at a time, every 4-6 weeks. Monitor POS data after each round to check for any revenue impact. If a cut item's sales were being redirected to other items (which is what usually happens), continue cutting. This approach is less scary for the owner and gives you data at each stage, but it stretches the process over months.

**Dramatic approach:** Cut 30-50% of the menu in one go, paired with a relaunch event. "New menu, focused on what we do best." This is the approach I prefer for turnaround situations because the financial impact is immediate. It also sends a clear signal to customers that something has changed, which can re-engage lapsed visitors who assumed the restaurant was the same as last time.

For most restaurants that are not in crisis, the gradual approach is lower risk. For restaurants that are [struggling financially](/blog/why-restaurants-fail-thailand), the dramatic approach saves money faster.

### Communicating the Change

How you frame the simplification matters. "We removed items" sounds like you are offering less. "We've refined our menu to focus on the dishes our kitchen executes best" sounds like you are offering more quality.

**On social media:** Announce the new menu as an upgrade. Feature the dishes that remain with fresh photography. Tell the story of why -- "After analysing what our customers love most, we have built a menu that lets our kitchen deliver the best version of every dish, every time."

**In the restaurant:** Brief your servers thoroughly. They will hear "where is the [removed item]?" multiple times. The response should be confident and positive: "We refined our menu this month and that dish is not currently available, but I would recommend [similar dish that stayed] -- our kitchen makes an excellent version." Never say "we had to cut it" or "management decided to remove it."

**For delivery platforms:** Update your Grab and LINE MAN menus simultaneously. Remove discontinued items, update photos, and refresh descriptions. A clean delivery menu with professional photography will outperform a bloated one without it.

## Dealing with "But Regulars Love That Dish"

This is the most common objection I hear, and it deserves a dedicated section because it is the objection that most often prevents necessary cuts.

The reality, backed by data from every menu simplification I have done:

**Most "regular favourites" sell fewer than 5 orders per day.** The owner or chef remembers one particular customer who always orders the crab fried rice, and that memory creates the illusion that the item is popular. Check the POS data. If it sells 3 orders per day, it is not a crowd favourite. It is a niche item with a memorable fan.

**Regulars adapt.** When you remove an item, the regular who used to order it does not leave the restaurant. They order something else. In three months, they have a new regular order and the old item is forgotten. I have tracked this explicitly across 8 menu simplifications: of the regulars who were ordering items that were cut, 92% continued visiting at the same frequency. Of the remaining 8%, most had already been declining in visit frequency before the cut.

**The one exception.** If a single item generates more than 5% of your total revenue and has a genuine following, do not cut it -- even if its margins are mediocre. Some items are traffic drivers. A famous khao mun gai (chicken rice) that people come specifically to eat is worth keeping at a lower margin because it gets people through the door, and once they are in the door, they order other things too. The key is distinguishing between items that actually drive traffic and items that you are emotionally attached to.

<Callout type="tip">
Here is a test: if you removed the item and put a note on the menu saying "This dish has been discontinued," would anyone ask about it? Would they mention it on social media? If the answer is no, the item is not driving traffic -- it is just filling space. If the answer is genuinely yes, keep it and work on improving its margins.
</Callout>

## Simplification and Kitchen Efficiency

The kitchen benefits of a smaller menu go beyond faster ticket times. The entire operation becomes more manageable.

### Prep Time Reduction

A 100-item menu might require 4-5 hours of morning prep to get all stations ready. A 40-item menu might require 2-3 hours. That is 1-2 hours of labour saved daily, which is 30-60 hours per month. At 75-100 THB/hour for a prep cook, that is 2,250-6,000 THB/month in direct labour savings -- small individually but it adds up alongside the other efficiencies.

More importantly, shorter prep means your kitchen is ready on time. I have seen restaurants with massive menus that are still prepping when the first lunch customers sit down, which leads to slow first tickets and a bad start to every service.

### Station Organisation

A smaller menu allows for cleaner station organisation. Each cook has a defined set of dishes, a defined set of ingredients within arm's reach, and a defined workflow. When a ticket comes in, the cook does not need to think about where the ingredients are or what the recipe requires -- they know because they make this dish 30 times a day.

Compare this to a kitchen running 100+ items, where a cook might handle 25 different dishes across a shift, some of which they make once a week. That cook is constantly context-switching, retrieving ingredients from storage, checking recipe references, and slowing down the entire line.

### Speed of Service

Faster kitchens produce happier customers, fewer walkouts, higher table turns, and better delivery platform ratings. The relationship between menu size and speed of service is one of the strongest predictors of [restaurant profitability](/blog/restaurant-profit-margins-thailand) I have found in my data.

A restaurant that reduces average ticket time from 25 minutes to 14 minutes during peak dinner service (6-9 PM, roughly 3 hours) can serve approximately 1.5-2x more table turns in the same window. If average check is 300 THB per person and the restaurant has 40 seats, the difference between 1.5 turns and 2.5 turns per evening is roughly 12,000 THB in additional daily dinner revenue, or 360,000 THB per month. That is not a rounding error.

## Simplified Menu and Food Cost

The food cost benefits of menu simplification come from multiple sources that compound on each other.

### Fewer SKUs, Better Purchasing Power

When you reduce your menu from 100 items to 45, your ingredient list might shrink from 220 unique SKUs to 120. With fewer items to buy, your order volume per item increases. Higher volume per SKU gives you:

**Better prices from suppliers.** Ordering 50 kg of chicken breast per week instead of 20 kg qualifies you for bulk pricing at most Bangkok wholesale markets. The savings are typically 5-12% per ingredient.

**Fewer suppliers needed.** With a shorter ingredient list, you can consolidate orders with fewer vendors, which simplifies logistics and gives each vendor a larger order (and more reason to offer competitive pricing). For a detailed guide on supplier strategy, see [Food Cost Control for Bangkok Restaurants](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).

**More predictable ordering.** With fewer items and more consistent sales per item, your weekly orders become more predictable. You buy what you need, not what you might need. Over-ordering decreases, and emergency runs to Makro mid-week (which always come with premium pricing and lost labour hours) become rare.

### Less Waste

I covered this above, but it is worth quantifying again in the food cost context. A restaurant that moves from 10% waste to 4% waste on 400,000 THB/month in food purchases saves 24,000 THB/month. That 24,000 THB drops straight to the bottom line because it was money already being spent -- you are just no longer throwing it away.

### Higher Contribution Items Get More Orders

When you remove low-margin items from your menu, customers do not leave -- they order something else. And if you have structured your simplified menu correctly (using the [menu engineering framework](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) to position your Stars and promoted Puzzles), the "something else" they order has a higher contribution margin than the item you removed.

This is the menu mix shift effect, and it is one of the most powerful outcomes of simplification. You are not adding revenue by adding items. You are increasing profitability by concentrating customer choices onto your best-margin dishes.

## The Seasonal Rotation Alternative

If cutting your menu permanently feels too drastic, consider a hybrid approach: a smaller core menu supplemented by rotating seasonal specials.

**Core menu (70-80% of items).** These are your permanent fixtures -- the dishes your kitchen executes best, your highest-margin items, and the dishes customers expect to find. This core should be 25-40 items depending on your restaurant type.

**Rotating specials (20-30% of items).** A selection of 8-15 items that change monthly or seasonally. These items use seasonal ingredients (which are cheaper and better quality in season), create novelty and a reason for repeat visits, and allow you to test new dishes without committing to permanent menu changes.

The seasonal rotation model gives you the variety that Thai diners expect while maintaining the operational efficiency of a smaller core menu. Your kitchen preps and executes the core menu on autopilot, while the specials add excitement and freshness.

**How to execute seasonal rotations:**

- **Plan one month ahead.** Decide the next rotation's items 4 weeks before launch, so you can source ingredients, cost recipes, train the kitchen, and photograph the items.
- **Print specials separately.** Use a clip-in sheet, a table tent, or a chalkboard for specials rather than reprinting your entire menu. This keeps costs low (a table tent or insert costs 5-15 THB each to print).
- **Track performance.** If a seasonal special sells exceptionally well, consider promoting it to the core menu (replacing a lower performer). If it sells poorly, it simply disappears at the end of the rotation without anyone noticing.
- **Align with Thai seasons and holidays.** Mango specials in April-May (mango season), seafood features during the Gulf of Thailand's prime season (December-February), vegetarian options during the Vegetarian Festival (October), and festive set menus around Songkran and New Year.

<Callout type="info">
The seasonal rotation model works particularly well on [delivery platforms](/blog/menu-design-increase-revenue-thailand) where "new items" trigger algorithmic boosts. Adding 3-5 new items monthly signals an active restaurant, which improves your visibility in search results. This is a case where menu simplification and delivery platform optimisation align perfectly.
</Callout>

## Thai Cultural Resistance to Smaller Menus

I would be dishonest if I did not address this, because it is a real obstacle -- not just a practical one but a cultural one.

### The "Value" Perception

Many Thai customers equate a large menu with value. A restaurant with 100 items "must be good" because they offer so much. A restaurant with 25 items "must be expensive" because they are being selective. This perception is shifting among younger, urban Thai diners who appreciate focused concepts, but it remains strong among older demographics and in areas outside central Bangkok.

The counter to this perception is execution quality. A restaurant with 30 excellent items builds a reputation for quality that a restaurant with 100 inconsistent items cannot match. The first visit might be influenced by menu size, but the second visit -- and every visit after -- is determined by whether the food was good. Quality wins over time.

### The "Something for Everyone" Expectation

Thai dining is inherently social and communal, which creates pressure to offer variety. A table of eight wants each person to choose something different, and a 25-item menu might not feel like enough options for a large group.

The solution is not a bigger menu but a better-structured one. A 45-item menu with 7 well-designed categories (appetisers, soups, curries, stir-fries, grilled/fried, rice/noodles, seafood) gives a table of eight 7 different categories to order from, with 5-7 choices within each. That is more variety than the table can consume in one sitting. The issue is never actual variety -- it is perceived variety, which is a design problem, not a menu size problem.

### Owner Identity

For many Thai restaurant owners, the menu is a reflection of their culinary identity. Cutting dishes feels like cutting part of themselves. "My grandmother's recipe" or "the dish I learned in culinary school" carry emotional weight that no spreadsheet can override.

I respect this. But I also ask: if your grandmother's recipe sells 4 portions per week and costs you 12,000 THB per month in waste, prep, and inventory carrying costs, is the best way to honour it really by burying it on page 3 of a 100-item menu where nobody orders it? Consider making it a rotating special that gets proper attention, promotion, and customer awareness. A dish that appears as a featured special for one month per year gets more love and attention than a dish that sits permanently on a menu that nobody reads past the first page.

## Case Studies: Successful Simplifications in Bangkok

### Case 1: The Bloated Thai-International Restaurant (Asoke)

**Before:** 92 items across Thai, Japanese, Italian, and Western categories. 24 staff. Food cost 37%. Average ticket time 28 minutes. Monthly revenue 1.4 million THB.

**The cut:** Eliminated all non-Thai items (removed 35 items). Analysed the remaining 57 Thai items by sales mix and cut the bottom 22. Final menu: 35 items, all Thai, all using the same core ingredient base.

**After (90 days):** Food cost 29%. Average ticket time 13 minutes. Monthly revenue 1.5 million THB (higher, despite fewer items, because table turns increased and average check went up 12% from better menu engineering). Waste reduced from 45,000 THB/month to 12,000 THB/month. Staff reduced to 18 through attrition (did not need to replace departures because the kitchen was more efficient).

**Net profit improvement:** approximately 190,000 THB/month -- from a restaurant that was already profitable. The simplification turned a 5% margin business into a 17% margin business.

### Case 2: The Neighbourhood Noodle Shop (Ladprao)

**Before:** 68 items including noodles, rice dishes, salads, and a full drink menu. Owner managed the kitchen alone with two helpers. Food cost 34%. Revenue 280,000 THB/month.

**The cut:** Focused exclusively on noodles and a handful of complementary sides. Cut to 18 noodle items and 6 sides (24 total). Removed all rice dishes and salads.

**After (60 days):** Food cost 26%. Revenue steady at 275,000 THB/month (slight dip from losing a few customers who came specifically for rice dishes, offset by faster service attracting more lunch traffic). The owner's workday shortened by 2 hours because prep was dramatically simpler. Eliminated one helper position.

**Net profit improvement:** approximately 35,000 THB/month on a small restaurant. More importantly, the owner's quality of life improved significantly. She had been working 14-hour days and was ready to close. The simplification made the business sustainable.

### Case 3: The Tourist-Area Thai Restaurant (Khaosan)

**Before:** 110 items including Thai, Western breakfast, burgers, pasta, and smoothies. Targeting backpackers. Food cost 36%. Revenue 900,000 THB/month.

**The cut:** This one required a different approach because the multi-cuisine format was actually appropriate for the location. Instead of eliminating cuisines, we simplified within each: Thai went from 45 to 20 items, Western from 30 to 12, breakfast from 20 to 8, and drinks from 15 to 10. Total: 50 items.

**After (90 days):** Food cost 30%. Revenue 870,000 THB/month (minor decline from smaller menu, but margin improvement more than compensated). Kitchen throughput improved by 40% and the restaurant was able to handle peak tourist-season volume without the chaos that had characterised previous high seasons. Staff training time for new hires dropped from 3 weeks to 10 days.

## Step-by-Step Simplification Process

Here is the process I use with every client, and the one you can follow on your own.

### Phase 1: Data Collection (Week 1)

1. **Pull 90 days of POS data.** You need item-level sales, ideally by day of week and daypart (lunch vs. dinner).
2. **Cost every recipe.** If you do not have recipe costing cards, create them. Every ingredient, every portion size, every cost per portion. This is non-negotiable -- you cannot make margin-based decisions without knowing your margins.
3. **Map ingredient usage.** Create a spreadsheet listing every ingredient and which menu items use it. Identify single-use ingredients.
4. **Assess complexity.** Score each item on prep time, cook time, skill required, and equipment needed.

### Phase 2: Analysis (Week 2)

1. **Run your [menu engineering matrix](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand).** Classify every item as a Star, Plowhorse, Puzzle, or Dog.
2. **Rank by total contribution.** Sort all items by weekly contribution margin x units sold. Identify the top 20% and bottom 40%.
3. **Flag cut candidates.** Any item that is: a Dog, sells fewer than 3 orders per day, has food cost above 40%, uses a single-use ingredient, or has a high complexity score.
4. **Decide what stays.** Keep all Stars, keep Plowhorses (they drive traffic), keep Puzzles you can fix (reprice or reposition). Cut the rest.

### Phase 3: Menu Reconstruction (Week 3)

1. **Build the new menu.** Organise remaining items into clear sections. Apply the [golden triangle and visual hierarchy principles](/blog/menu-design-increase-revenue-thailand) to position high-margin items prominently.
2. **Reprice where needed.** Some items may need price adjustments based on current ingredient costs. Use the simplification as an opportunity to correct any pricing that has drifted from target margins.
3. **Write new descriptions.** Shorter, simpler menus deserve better descriptions per item. Invest the space you have saved into evocative, value-building descriptions for your remaining dishes.
4. **Design and print.** Brief your designer, produce the new menu, print.

### Phase 4: Launch (Week 4)

1. **Train staff.** Every server should know every item on the simplified menu, including flavour profile, allergens, recommended pairings, and margin priority. A 30-minute pre-shift training is the minimum.
2. **Brief the kitchen.** New prep lists, new station assignments, new pars. The kitchen team needs to adjust their workflow to the smaller menu.
3. **Launch with communication.** Social media announcement, table tents in the restaurant, a conversation with regulars who you know will notice changes.
4. **Monitor aggressively for 30 days.** Track daily: revenue, covers, average check, food cost, ticket times, and customer feedback. Compare to the 30 days before the change. If something is not working, adjust.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not backtrack in the first two weeks. You will get complaints. Some regulars will ask about removed items. Some staff will say "customers are unhappy." This is normal transition friction. Unless your revenue drops more than 10% in the first two weeks (which I have never seen happen with a data-driven simplification), stay the course. The complaints will fade. The profits will not.
</Callout>

## When NOT to Simplify

Menu simplification is not always the answer. There are situations where a larger menu is appropriate and reducing it would hurt the business.

**Buffet restaurants.** The entire value proposition is variety. A buffet with fewer items is a worse buffet. Simplify the behind-the-scenes operations (standardise sauces, cross-utilise ingredients, batch cook efficiently), but keep the customer-facing variety.

**Hotel restaurants serving multiple demographics.** A hotel restaurant that needs to serve Japanese business travellers, European tourists, and local Thai families at breakfast, lunch, and dinner legitimately needs a large menu. The solution here is operational efficiency (shared prep, smart ingredient overlap) rather than menu reduction.

**Restaurants with genuinely high demand across all items.** If your POS data shows that every item on your 80-item menu sells at least 5 orders per day, your large menu is working. Do not fix what is not broken. This is rare -- I have seen it once in 15 years -- but it exists.

**New restaurants still finding their audience.** In the first 90 days, a slightly larger menu helps you collect data on what your specific customer base actually wants. Launch with 40-50 items, track everything, then simplify based on data after the first quarter. Starting with 15 items and guessing wrong is more costly than starting with 40 and cutting to 25 once you have evidence.

**Restaurants where the chef IS the menu.** Some chef-driven restaurants thrive on a constantly evolving, expansive menu because the chef's creativity is the draw. If customers come because they never know what will be on the menu, standardisation and simplification work against the brand. These restaurants succeed on culinary talent, not operational efficiency, and they require a different management approach.

## The Bottom Line

A smaller menu is not a compromise. It is a strategy. The restaurants in Bangkok that are consistently profitable, consistently well-reviewed, and consistently full share a common trait: they do fewer things, better.

If you are running a 80+ item menu and struggling with [high food costs](/blog/high-margin-menu-items-thailand), slow kitchen speed, inconsistent quality, or staff training headaches, the diagnosis is right in front of you. Every item on your menu that sells fewer than 3 orders per day is costing you money -- in waste, in complexity, in attention diverted from the dishes that actually matter.

The process is straightforward: collect data, identify what to cut, restructure what remains, and launch with confidence. The results -- lower food cost, faster service, better quality, happier staff -- show up within weeks.
