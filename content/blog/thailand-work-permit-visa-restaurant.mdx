Here is a fact that surprises almost every foreign entrepreneur I work with: owning shares in a Thai company does not give you the right to work in that company. You can be a shareholder, you can be a director on paper, you can have invested millions of baht — but the moment you walk into your restaurant and start managing staff, reviewing invoices, or making operational decisions, you are working. And working in Thailand without a work permit is illegal, punishable by fines of up to 100,000 THB and deportation.

I have seen it happen. A European client of mine opened a beautiful Italian restaurant in Sukhumvit, invested 8 million THB, had the company structure set up perfectly by a top-tier law firm — and got fined 50,000 THB because he was working on an expired work permit while the renewal was being processed. The fine was not the painful part. The 48 hours he spent at the Immigration Detention Centre while his lawyer sorted it out was.

The visa and work permit system in Thailand is not intuitive, and the consequences of getting it wrong range from expensive to catastrophic. This guide covers every step, from choosing the right visa category to maintaining your legal status year after year, specifically for foreign restaurant and F&B business owners.

## Why You Need a Work Permit Even as an Owner

Thai law makes a clear distinction between investment and labour. Under the Working of Aliens Act B.E. 2551 (2008, as amended), "work" is defined as engaging in work by exerting energy or using knowledge, whether or not for remuneration. The definition is intentionally broad.

These activities count as "work" and require a work permit:

- Managing or supervising restaurant staff
- Signing contracts, cheques, or purchase orders
- Making business decisions at the restaurant premises
- Attending business meetings in your capacity as a company officer
- Training staff
- Cooking, preparing food, or performing any operational tasks
- Overseeing renovations or construction at your restaurant

These activities do not require a work permit:

- Attending a board of directors meeting as a shareholder (limited to board meetings only)
- Visiting your investment property without conducting work
- Participating in conferences or seminars (under certain visa types)

The line between "visiting my investment" and "working at my restaurant" is thinner than you might think, and immigration officers interpret it broadly. If you are in your restaurant and directing any activity, you are working.

<Callout type="warning">
"But I am just the owner, I am not an employee." I hear this from foreign restaurant owners constantly. Thai law does not care about the distinction. If you are performing any work activity in Thailand — including unpaid work in your own company — you need a work permit. The only exception is attending formal board meetings, and that exception does not extend to operational management.
</Callout>

## Step 1: Choose the Right Visa Category

Before you can apply for a work permit, you need to be in Thailand on a visa that allows you to apply for one, and not all visas qualify. Here are your options, ranked by practicality for restaurant owners.

### Non-Immigrant B Visa (Non-B) — The Standard Route

The Non-B visa is the standard visa for foreign nationals intending to work or conduct business in Thailand, and it is the visa that 90%+ of foreign restaurant owners use.

**How to obtain it:**

**Option A: Apply at a Thai embassy or consulate abroad (recommended)**

This is the cleanest path. You apply before entering Thailand, with all your documentation prepared in advance.

**Required documents:**
- Passport valid for at least 18 months
- Completed visa application form
- Passport-size photos (4x6 cm, white background)
- Company registration documents (Form Bor.Or.5, memorandum of association, list of shareholders)
- Company's business license or food and beverage license
- Invitation letter from the company, signed by an authorized director
- Company's audited financial statements (or a letter from the auditor if the company is new)
- Company bank statement showing sufficient capital
- Minutes of the board meeting approving your appointment
- Your educational certificates (some embassies require these)
- Employment contract or appointment letter

**Processing time:** 5-10 working days at most Thai embassies. Some embassies offer express processing for an additional fee.

**Cost:** Varies by nationality and embassy. Typical range: 2,000-5,000 THB (or equivalent in local currency). Single-entry Non-B: ~2,000 THB. Multiple-entry Non-B: ~5,000 THB.

**Validity:** Single-entry Non-B gives you 90 days from entry to extend your stay and apply for a work permit. Multiple-entry Non-B gives you one year with 90-day entries (you must exit and re-enter every 90 days until you get your permit and extend).

**Option B: Convert a tourist visa or visa exemption inside Thailand**

It is possible to convert certain visa types to a Non-B at the Immigration Bureau in Bangkok (Chaeng Watthana) without leaving the country. This is more convenient but less reliable — approval depends on the discretion of the immigration officer, and processing can take 2-4 weeks with multiple visits.

<Callout type="tip">
I recommend applying for the Non-B at a Thai embassy abroad whenever possible. The process is more predictable, the approval rate is higher, and you avoid the frustration of converting inside Thailand, which can require 3-5 visits to immigration over several weeks. The embassies in Vientiane (Laos), Kuala Lumpur (Malaysia), and Phnom Penh (Cambodia) are popular choices for foreigners already in the region.
</Callout>

### BOI Visa (Through Board of Investment Promotion)

If your restaurant project has received BOI promotion (see the BOI section below), you can obtain a visa and work permit through the BOI's One-Stop Service Centre. This route is faster and simpler than the standard process, but it requires BOI approval first.

**Advantages:**
- Visa and work permit processed simultaneously
- Faster processing (7-10 working days through BOI One-Stop)
- Work permit allows broader job descriptions
- Multiple-re-entry permit included
- No need to exit Thailand for visa renewal

**Limitations:**
- Only available if your project has BOI promotion
- Most standalone restaurants do not qualify for BOI (see below)

### SMART Visa

The SMART visa is designed for highly skilled professionals, executives, investors, and startup founders. For restaurant owners, the most relevant categories are:

**SMART "E" (Executive):** For senior executives of companies that have BOI promotion or are in targeted industries. Requires a minimum salary of 200,000 THB per month.

**SMART "I" (Investor):** For investors putting at least 20 million THB into a targeted industry. F&B can qualify if it involves significant technology or innovation (e.g., food-tech, automated kitchens, food science).

**SMART "S" (Startup):** For founders of tech startups in targeted industries. A pure restaurant does not qualify, but a food-tech startup with a restaurant component might.

**Advantages:**
- Up to 4-year visa
- No separate work permit required (the SMART visa includes work authorization)
- No 90-day reporting (annual reporting instead)
- Spouse and children can work/study in Thailand

**Limitations:**
- High salary/investment thresholds
- Narrow industry targeting — most traditional restaurants do not qualify
- Application processed by the National Economic and Social Development Council (NESDC), which adds bureaucracy

### LTR Visa (Long-Term Resident Visa)

The LTR visa, introduced in 2022, offers a 10-year visa for four categories of foreigners. The most relevant for restaurant investors:

**Wealthy Global Citizen:** Requires at least $1 million USD in assets, at least $80,000 USD per year in income, and at least $500,000 USD invested in Thai government bonds, property, or Thai investments.

**Wealthy Pensioner:** Requires at least $80,000 USD per year in pension income (or $40,000 USD with $250,000 USD invested in Thailand).

**Work-from-Thailand Professional:** For remote workers earning at least $80,000 USD per year. Not directly relevant for restaurant operators but worth noting.

**Highly-Skilled Professional:** Requires at least $80,000 USD per year in salary and employment by a Thai company in targeted industries.

**Advantages:**
- 10-year visa (5-year renewable)
- Annual reporting instead of 90-day
- Digital work permit (no physical book)
- Flat 17% personal income tax rate (reduced from up to 35% progressive)
- Fast-track at airports

**Limitations:**
- High financial thresholds
- Does not replace work permit entirely (you still need to register with the Department of Employment, but the process is simplified)
- Limited to specific investor/income profiles

<Callout type="info">
For most foreign restaurant owners with an investment under $500,000 USD, the Non-B visa is the only realistic option. The BOI, SMART, and LTR routes are worth exploring if you are investing heavily or building a restaurant group, but they have eligibility thresholds that exclude the majority of independent restaurant operators.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Apply for Your Work Permit

Once you have entered Thailand on a Non-B visa (or other qualifying visa), you apply for a work permit at the Department of Employment under the Ministry of Labour.

### The 4:1 Rule

This is the requirement that catches most people: for every foreign work permit issued, the company must employ at least 4 Thai nationals. The employees must be registered with Social Security and on the company payroll.

**What this means in practice:**
- 1 foreign work permit = minimum 4 Thai employees
- 2 foreign work permits = minimum 8 Thai employees
- Each Thai employee must be paid at least minimum wage (370 THB/day in Bangkok as of 2026) and registered with the Social Security Office

Most restaurants need at least 4 Thai staff anyway — cooks, servers, cleaners — so this requirement aligns naturally with operational needs. But if you are a very small operation (food truck, tiny cafe, two-person cloud kitchen), you may need to hire Thai employees specifically to meet the ratio.

<Callout type="warning">
The 4 Thai employees must be real employees with real work duties, on the payroll and paying Social Security contributions. Hiring "ghost employees" who are on paper but do not actually work is fraud and grounds for work permit revocation. Immigration officials can and do visit restaurants to verify that the Thai employees listed on the work permit application actually work there.
</Callout>

### The 2 Million THB Capital Requirement

For each foreign work permit, the company must have at least 2 million THB in registered capital. This is a one-time capital injection, not an ongoing expense — the money goes into the company's bank account and can be used for business operations.

**What this means in practice:**
- 1 foreign work permit = 2 million THB registered capital
- 2 foreign work permits = 4 million THB registered capital
- The capital must be fully paid up (not just registered — actually transferred to the company bank account)

For most restaurant startups, 2 million THB in registered capital is reasonable — you will need at least this amount to fund renovation, equipment, initial inventory, and working capital anyway. The requirement becomes burdensome when you need multiple foreign work permits, for example a foreign chef in addition to yourself as the owner-manager.

### Required Documents

The work permit application requires a substantial document package. Here is the complete list:

**Company documents:**
- Company registration certificate (DBD)
- List of shareholders (Form Bor.Or.5)
- Memorandum and articles of association
- Minutes of board meeting appointing the foreign employee
- Company's most recent audited financial statements
- Value Added Tax (VAT) registration (Form Por.Por.20)
- Social Security registration and proof of Thai employee enrollment
- Company bank statement (showing registered capital)
- Company map showing office/restaurant location
- Photographs of the company premises (exterior and interior)

**Personal documents:**
- Passport with valid Non-B visa
- Three passport-sized photos (3x4 cm)
- Medical certificate from a Thai-accredited hospital (certifying you are free of prohibited diseases: leprosy, elephantiasis, drug addiction, alcoholism, third-stage syphilis)
- Educational certificates (degree or relevant qualifications)
- Resume or CV
- Employment contract specifying job duties, salary, and working hours

**Job-specific documents:**
- Detailed job description (must match what you actually do — more on this below)
- Justification letter explaining why a foreigner is needed for this position (for restaurant managers/owners, this typically references specialized knowledge, investment management, or culinary expertise)

### The Digital Work Permit (e-Work Permit)

Since 2021, Thailand has been transitioning to a digital work permit system. The traditional "blue book" (physical work permit booklet) is being replaced by a digital record linked to your passport number.

**Current status (2026):** New work permits are issued digitally by default. You receive a digital work permit card and can verify your status through the Department of Employment's online system. Some older work permits still exist as physical books and are valid until renewal, at which point they convert to digital.

**Practical implications:**
- You no longer need to carry a physical work permit booklet
- Immigration and labour officials can verify your status electronically by scanning your passport
- Changes to your work permit (job description, employer, address) can be submitted online
- The digital system has reduced processing times for renewals and modifications

<Callout type="tip">
Even though the physical blue book is being phased out, I recommend keeping a PDF copy of your digital work permit on your phone and a printed copy at your restaurant. If immigration officers visit your premises and your digital record does not load (which happens occasionally with the government system), having a printed copy avoids unnecessary complications.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Extend Your Stay and Maintain Legal Status

Your Non-B visa gets you into Thailand, and your work permit allows you to work. But you also need to manage your immigration stay — the length of time you are permitted to remain in the country before you must leave or extend.

### One-Year Extension of Stay

Once your work permit is issued, you apply for a one-year extension of stay at the Immigration Bureau. This converts your initial 90-day entry into a full one-year stay.

**Required documents:**
- Passport
- Work permit (digital confirmation)
- TM.7 application form
- 4x6 cm photos
- Company registration certificate
- Social Security payment receipts for Thai employees
- Personal income tax filing (Form PND.91) — this is critical; you must show you are paying income tax on your Thai salary
- Company's withholding tax certificates (Form 50 Tawi)
- Company map and photographs
- 1,900 THB fee

**Processing time:** Same day to 2 weeks, depending on the immigration office and workload.

### 90-Day Reporting

Even with a one-year extension, you must report your address to immigration every 90 days. This is separate from your visa extension — it is a notification requirement under Thailand's immigration law.

**How to report:**
- In person at an immigration office (instant)
- By post (send 7-10 days before the due date)
- Online through the immigration bureau's website (when the system is functioning — it has periodic outages)

**Cost:** Free.

**Penalty for late reporting:** 2,000 THB fine. Not catastrophic, but it creates a negative record that can complicate future extensions.

### Annual Renewal Cycle

Every year, you need to renew both your work permit and your extension of stay. The cycle looks like this:

**60-30 days before work permit expiry:** Submit work permit renewal application to the Department of Employment. Same document package as the initial application, plus your previous year's personal income tax return and the company's latest financial statements.

**30 days before stay extension expiry:** Submit extension of stay renewal at Immigration. Bring updated employment and tax documents.

**Work permit renewal cost:** 3,000 THB government fee plus 10,000-25,000 THB in agent fees if using professional assistance.

**Extension of stay renewal cost:** 1,900 THB government fee.

**Critical timing:** Your work permit and extension of stay expiry dates may not align perfectly, and you must never let either document lapse. If your work permit expires before renewal is processed, you are technically working illegally; if your extension of stay expires, you are an overstayer. Both have serious consequences.

<Callout type="warning">
The single most common legal problem I see with foreign restaurant owners is a gap between work permit expiry and renewal. The renewal process can take 30-45 days, and if you submit late, there is a window where your old permit has expired but the new one is not yet issued. During this window, you are technically working without a permit. Submit your renewal application at least 45 days before expiry. Better yet, have your lawyer track all deadlines.
</Callout>

## BOI Promotion for Restaurant Businesses

The Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) offers promotional certificates that provide significant benefits, including easier visa and work permit processes, tax holidays, and the ability to hold 100% foreign ownership.

### Does My Restaurant Qualify?

Standalone restaurants are difficult to get BOI promotion for, because the BOI prioritizes projects that bring technology, innovation, or significant employment to Thailand. A single Thai restaurant serving pad thai — no matter how good — is unlikely to qualify.

**Restaurant projects that can qualify:**
- **Restaurant groups** with 5+ locations and a training program for Thai staff
- **Hotel or resort F&B operations** as part of a larger hospitality investment
- **Central kitchen or food manufacturing** facilities that supply multiple outlets
- **Food-tech projects** involving automated kitchen systems, food science, or supply chain innovation
- **Franchise operations** that involve technology or skills transfer from abroad

**Minimum investment:** 1 million THB (excluding land and working capital) for most BOI categories.

**Benefits if approved:**
- 100% foreign ownership (no need for Thai majority shareholders)
- Corporate income tax exemptions (3-8 years depending on the project type and location)
- Import duty exemptions on machinery and equipment
- Visa and work permit processing through BOI One-Stop Service (faster, simpler)
- Permission to bring in foreign experts and technicians
- Land ownership rights for the project (in some cases)

**Application process:**
1. Prepare a detailed business plan with financial projections
2. Submit application to the BOI (in person or online)
3. BOI reviews the application (60-90 working days for projects under 750 million THB)
4. If approved, receive a promotional certificate with conditions
5. Register the company and apply for visa/work permit through BOI One-Stop

**Cost of BOI application:** The government fee is minimal (a few thousand THB), but preparing the application typically requires a BOI consultant. Consultant fees range from 50,000-200,000 THB depending on the complexity of the project.

<Callout type="info">
If you are investing 10 million THB or more in a restaurant group or food-related business, consult a BOI advisor before setting up your company structure. The tax savings and operational benefits of BOI promotion can significantly outweigh the application cost and effort. I work with several BOI consultants and can make introductions — [reach out for a consultation](/free-consultation).
</Callout>

## SMART Visa: Detailed Eligibility for F&B

The SMART visa is Thailand's attempt to attract high-skill, high-value foreigners. For restaurant owners, the relevant categories are narrow but worth understanding.

### SMART "E" (Executive)

**Eligibility:**
- Senior executive (VP level or above, or equivalent) of a company that has received BOI promotion, holds a BOI certification, or operates in a targeted S-curve industry
- Minimum monthly salary of 200,000 THB
- Minimum 10 years of work experience in the relevant field

**For restaurant owners:** If your restaurant group has BOI promotion and you are the CEO/GM earning 200,000 THB/month or more, you qualify. For most independent restaurant owners, the salary threshold alone disqualifies them.

### SMART "I" (Investor)

**Eligibility:**
- Invest at least 20 million THB in a targeted industry or technology company in Thailand
- The investment must be in technology or innovation-related activities

**For restaurant owners:** A 20 million THB investment in a food-tech venture or an innovative restaurant concept with technology components could qualify. A standard restaurant investment does not meet the "technology" requirement.

### SMART "T" (Talent)

**Eligibility:**
- Experts or specialists with specific qualifications in S-curve industries (includes food technology under "Agriculture and Biotechnology")
- Minimum monthly salary of 100,000 THB
- Relevant degrees or certifications

**For restaurant owners:** If you have formal culinary science or food technology credentials and can position your work as falling within the targeted industry, this category is worth exploring with a SMART visa consultant.

**SMART visa benefits (all categories):**
- Up to 4-year visa
- No separate work permit needed
- Work authorization covers all activities related to your SMART visa endorsement
- 90-day reporting replaced by annual reporting
- Spouse and dependents receive similar benefits

## LTR Visa: The Wealthy Investor Route

The Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa is the most straightforward option for wealthy investors who want long-term residency in Thailand without the annual visa renewal process.

### Wealthy Global Citizen Category

**Requirements:**
- Personal assets of at least $1 million USD
- Annual income of at least $80,000 USD (in the 2 years preceding application)
- Investment of at least $500,000 USD in Thai government bonds, Thai property, or a direct investment in Thailand

To make this concrete: a foreign investor who owns property in Bangkok worth $300,000 USD and has invested $200,000 USD in their restaurant company (registered capital) meets the $500,000 USD Thai investment threshold. Combined with $1 million USD in global assets and $80,000 USD annual income, they qualify.

## What Happens If Your Restaurant Fails

Restaurant failure rates in Thailand are high — roughly 60% of new restaurants close within 3 years (for a detailed analysis, see our guide on [why restaurants fail in Thailand](/blog/why-restaurants-fail-thailand)). If your restaurant fails, your visa and work permit status becomes an immediate concern.

### Immediate Steps

**Work permit:** If you close the company or stop working, you must surrender your work permit to the Department of Employment within 15 days. Continuing to hold a work permit without working — or working at a different company on the same permit — is a violation.

**Extension of stay:** Your extension of stay is tied to your employment, so once your work permit is cancelled, your extension is effectively invalid. You have 7 days to report to immigration and either:
1. Leave Thailand
2. Apply for a different visa type (tourist visa, education visa, retirement visa, etc.)
3. Transfer your work permit to a new employer (if you have secured alternative employment)

**Company winding down:** If you are closing the company entirely, the formal dissolution process takes 3-6 months and involves:
- Passing a dissolution resolution at a shareholder meeting
- Appointing a liquidator
- Publishing the dissolution notice in a newspaper
- Settling all debts, tax obligations, and employee severance
- Filing final tax returns
- Applying for deregistration with the DBD

**Employee severance:** Under Thai labour law, you must pay severance to all employees based on their length of service:
- Under 120 days of service: no severance
- 120 days to 1 year: 30 days' wages
- 1-3 years: 90 days' wages
- 3-6 years: 180 days' wages
- 6-10 years: 240 days' wages
- 10-20 years: 300 days' wages
- Over 20 years: 400 days' wages

This severance obligation exists regardless of the reason for closure. Budget for it.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not simply "walk away" from a failed restaurant and leave Thailand. Abandoning a company without proper dissolution leaves outstanding tax obligations, unpaid employee severance, and potential legal claims. It also creates problems if you ever want to return to Thailand for business — outstanding corporate obligations are flagged in the system and can complicate future visa applications.
</Callout>

### Starting Over

If your first restaurant fails but you want to try again in Thailand, you have two options:

**Use the same company:** If the company is still active and has funds, you can pivot to a new concept without re-registering. This saves time and money.

**Register a new company:** If the old company has debts or legal issues, a clean start may be better. The full company registration process takes 2-4 weeks and costs 30,000-80,000 THB (see our [foreigner restaurant ownership guide](/blog/foreigner-opening-restaurant-thailand) for details).

Your work permit must be updated to reflect the new business activity. If you are using the same company, this is a modification (5,000-10,000 THB, 2-3 weeks). If you register a new company, it is a new work permit application (full process, 30-45 working days).

## Common Violations and Penalties

Thai authorities take work permit violations seriously. Here are the most common violations I see among foreign restaurant owners, and their consequences.

### Working Without a Work Permit

**Violation:** Performing any work in Thailand without a valid work permit. This includes working while your permit is being renewed, working on activities not listed on your permit, or working for a different employer than listed on your permit.

**Penalty:** Fine of up to 100,000 THB, potential imprisonment of up to 5 years (rarely enforced for minor violations), and deportation with possible blacklisting from re-entry.

**How people get caught:** Immigration spot checks at restaurants (increasingly common in tourist areas), complaints from competitors or disgruntled employees, routine document checks at immigration when renewing visas, police checks during other investigations.

### Wrong Job Description

**Violation:** Your work permit says "Restaurant Manager" but you are also cooking, which is a separate job function. Or your permit says "Executive Chef" but you are also handling accounting.

**Penalty:** Your work permit can be revoked, and you face the same penalties as working without a permit.

**Prevention:** List every job function you will perform on your work permit application. "Restaurant Manager" should include: management, supervision, training, menu development, quality control, marketing, financial oversight, and any other duties you actually perform. Be comprehensive.

### Expired Work Permit

**Violation:** Working between the expiry of your old permit and the issuance of your new one. Even a one-day gap counts.

**Penalty:** Same as working without a permit — fines, potential deportation.

**Prevention:** Submit renewal applications at least 45 days before expiry. If your renewal is not processed before your old permit expires, technically you should stop working until the new one is issued. In practice, having a pending renewal application and a receipt from the Department of Employment provides some protection, but it is not foolproof.

### Employing Foreigners Without Permits

**Violation:** As an employer, hiring foreign staff (including foreign chefs) who do not have work permits.

**Penalty for the employer:** Fine of 10,000-100,000 THB per illegal foreign worker. Potential imprisonment.

**Penalty for the employee:** Same as working without a permit.

**Important:** This applies even if you "did not know" the foreign employee lacked a permit. As the employer, it is your responsibility to verify.

## Bringing in a Foreign Chef

Many foreign restaurant owners want to bring in a chef from their home country — an Italian chef for an Italian restaurant, a Japanese sushi master, a French pastry chef. This is possible but requires careful planning.

### Work Permit for a Foreign Chef

The process is identical to your own work permit: Non-B visa, work permit application, 4:1 Thai employee ratio, 2 million THB registered capital requirement.

**Additional requirements for chefs:**
- The chef must have demonstrable expertise that is not available in Thailand. This is the "necessity test" — the Department of Employment will ask why you cannot hire a Thai chef instead.
- Culinary certificates, years of experience, awards, or media recognition help justify the necessity.
- The job description must be specific to the chef's specialty (e.g., "Japanese sushi preparation and menu development" rather than generic "cooking").

**Salary requirements:** There is no formal minimum salary for work permits, but immigration and labour officials expect a foreign employee's salary to be reasonable for the position. A foreign chef earning less than 50,000 THB/month raises questions, and a salary of 60,000-120,000 THB/month for a skilled foreign chef is the normal range.

**Thai employee impact:** Remember the 4:1 rule — one additional foreign work permit means 4 additional Thai employees, so if you (the owner) and a foreign chef both need permits, you need at least 8 Thai employees. This usually aligns with operational needs for a restaurant of any meaningful size.

### Restricted Occupations

Certain occupations are reserved for Thai nationals under Thai law. For the restaurant industry, the key restricted occupations are:

- **Cooking Thai food** — technically restricted, though enforcement varies
- **Waitstaff / service staff** — restricted
- **General labour** — restricted

A foreign chef preparing non-Thai cuisine (Italian, Japanese, French, etc.) generally does not face issues. A foreign chef who wants to cook Thai food may need additional justification — for example, a fusion concept where the chef's international training is integral to the menu.

<Callout type="tip">
When writing the job description for a foreign chef's work permit, focus on specialized skills that Thai chefs typically lack: specific regional cuisine expertise, pastry techniques, fermentation or aging processes, menu development for international concepts, and staff training in foreign culinary traditions. The more specific and specialized the description, the easier the approval.
</Callout>
