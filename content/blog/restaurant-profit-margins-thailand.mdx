I keep a spreadsheet of every restaurant I have consulted with since 2012. It now has 83 entries across Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Phuket, and a handful of other Thai cities. Each entry includes the restaurant type, monthly revenue, food cost percentage, labour cost, rent-to-revenue ratio, and net profit margin.

The range is striking. At the top, a cocktail bar in Thonglor running 22% net margin. At the bottom, a Western casual dining restaurant on Sukhumvit that operated at -8% net margin for 14 months before closing. The median net profit margin across all 83 restaurants -- the ones that survived long enough for me to collect full-year data -- is 6.8%.

That number should temper expectations for anyone thinking about opening a restaurant in Thailand. The majority of restaurants that are profitable at all earn single-digit net margins. The ones that achieve double-digit margins are either in high-margin segments (bars, cafes), exceptionally well-managed, or both.

This article provides detailed margin benchmarks by restaurant type in Thailand, breaks down where the money goes, and identifies the specific levers you can pull to improve margins. The data comes from my client base, supplemented by industry reports from the Thai Restaurant Association and Kasikorn Research Center. If you are running a restaurant and want to know whether your numbers are healthy, or if you are planning to open one and want realistic profit expectations, this is the reference I wish someone had given me when I started consulting.

## Understanding the Three Margins

Before diving into benchmarks, a quick clarification on the three margin levels you will encounter. These get confused constantly, and confusing them leads to bad decisions.

**Gross Margin = (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue x 100**

This tells you how much of each revenue THB is left after paying for raw ingredients and beverages. It measures your menu pricing and purchasing efficiency. A restaurant with 1,000,000 THB in revenue and 310,000 THB in food and beverage COGS has a 69% gross margin. COGS includes only the cost of food and beverage ingredients -- nothing else.

**Operating Margin (EBITDA Margin) = (Revenue - COGS - Labour - Occupancy - Operating Expenses) / Revenue x 100**

This tells you how much is left after paying for everything except depreciation, interest, and tax. It measures the profitability of your core operation. This is the metric investors and lenders care about most because it strips out financing decisions and non-cash charges.

**Net Profit Margin = Net Profit / Revenue x 100**

This is the bottom line -- what is left after everything, including depreciation, interest, and corporate income tax. This is what the owner actually earns (before dividends, which carry an additional 10% withholding tax for individuals).

The relationship is always: Gross Margin > Operating Margin > Net Margin. A restaurant might have a 68% gross margin, 12% operating margin, and 6% net margin. Each level peels away another layer of costs. The gaps between them tell you where the money goes.

For a detailed P&L walkthrough showing how these margins are calculated line by line, see the [restaurant financial statements guide](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand).

Cloud kitchens are where margin expectations collide with reality. The pitch is compelling: low rent, no front-of-house, minimal staff. But the 30-35% platform commission replaces -- and often exceeds -- the savings on rent and labour. I wrote about this in detail in the [cloud kitchen guide](/blog/how-to-start-cloud-kitchen-bangkok).

The cloud kitchens that achieve positive margins share common traits: they run multiple brands from one kitchen (spreading fixed costs across 2-3 menus), their food cost is tightly controlled (simple menus, limited SKUs), and they have negotiated commission rates below the standard 30-35% through volume commitments.

The math only works if every other line item is minimized.

Cafes have the best gross margins in the restaurant industry because coffee is an extraordinarily high-margin product. A latte that sells for 140 THB uses 15-25 THB in coffee, milk, and cup. Even specialty single-origin pour-overs at 180 THB use 30-40 THB in ingredients. The gross margin on beverages is 75-85%.

The constraint is revenue per square meter. A cafe seat generates less revenue per hour than a restaurant seat because customers linger (90-120 minutes versus 45-60 minutes for casual dining). This means the occupancy cost as a percentage of revenue tends to be higher, which partially offsets the gross margin advantage. Bars consistently achieve the highest net margins in Thailand's F&B industry. The economics are simple: spirit cost on a cocktail selling for 380-480 THB is 60-100 THB (including garnish and ice), yielding a 75-85% gross margin. Beer margins are lower (60-70%) but volume is higher. Labour costs are moderate because a well-run bar needs fewer staff than a full-service restaurant of the same revenue.

The catch is regulatory and operational. Thailand's alcohol licensing requirements add complexity (see the [bar licensing guide](/blog/how-to-open-bar-thailand)), entertainment venue rules limit operating hours in some areas, and bars are disproportionately affected by regulatory changes. The recent [alcohol law changes](/blog/thailand-alcohol-law-2026) have implications for bar operators that directly affect revenue patterns.

Still, from a pure margin perspective, a well-run cocktail bar in a reasonable location is the most profitable restaurant format in Thailand.

<Callout type="tip">
If you are running a restaurant with thin food margins, your beverage programme is the highest-leverage opportunity to improve overall profitability. Increasing beverage revenue from 15% to 25% of total revenue -- without changing anything else -- can improve net margin by 2-4 percentage points. That is the difference between a 5% and a 9% net margin. Train your staff to suggest drinks, create a beverage menu that is visible and appealing, and price cocktails where the market supports them (350-500 THB in Bangkok is normal for craft cocktails).
</Callout>

## Food Cost Benchmarks: The Number Everyone Watches

Food cost is the most discussed metric in restaurant finance, and for good reason -- it is the largest single expense and the most directly controllable on a weekly basis.

### Target Ranges by Cuisine Type

**Thai cuisine:** 28-32%. Local sourcing and relatively inexpensive proteins keep costs down. The main risk is fresh seafood, which can swing 15-25% week to week depending on weather and supply.

**Japanese / sushi:** 32-40%. Fish cost volatility is extreme. A sushi restaurant importing salmon, tuna, and uni faces currency risk (JPY/THB), supply chain disruptions, and seasonal price spikes. The best operators lock in prices through forward contracts with importers, while smaller restaurants absorb the swings.

**Italian:** 30-35%. Imported cheese, olive oil, and cured meats are the cost drivers. Restaurants that make their own pasta and source local alternatives where possible (Thai mozzarella has improved significantly in recent years) can stay at the lower end.

**American / burger:** 32-38%. Quality beef is the single biggest cost driver. A restaurant using imported USDA Choice or Australian Wagyu faces food costs at 36-40%, while one using local Thai beef or blended patties can run 30-32%.

**Indian:** 26-30%. Spice-forward cuisines have a structural cost advantage because spices, lentils, and vegetables are cheap relative to the flavor impact they deliver. Indian restaurants in Thailand consistently achieve some of the lowest food costs.

**Korean:** 30-34%. Imported ingredients (gochujang, doenjang, Korean chili flakes) add cost, but proteins are often the more affordable cuts (pork belly, chicken thigh) which helps offset import costs.

### What Drives Food Cost Above Target

When food cost creeps above your target, the cause is almost always one of these five factors:

**Supplier price increases not reflected in menu prices.** Thailand's fresh ingredient prices are volatile. Pork prices rose 18% in the first half of 2025 due to ASF-related supply constraints, and many restaurants absorbed the increase rather than adjusting menus. Check your top 10 ingredient prices quarterly against your menu costing sheets.

**Portion creep.** Cooks gradually add more protein, more sauce, more garnish. It happens in every kitchen, and it happens slowly enough that nobody notices until the monthly food cost report arrives 3 points higher than expected. Re-spec portions quarterly. Weigh plated dishes randomly during service. Post portion guides with photos at every station.

**Waste and spoilage.** A well-managed restaurant kitchen should have food waste of 2-4% of purchases. Above 5% indicates prep inefficiency, over-ordering, or poor storage. Track waste daily -- even a simple clipboard at the bin where cooks note what they throw away changes behavior.

**Menu mix shift.** If your highest-selling item shifts from a 28% food cost dish to a 38% food cost dish, your blended food cost rises even though nothing in your kitchen has changed. POS sales mix reports reveal this. The [menu engineering](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) framework addresses how to steer the menu mix toward high-margin items.

**Theft.** Unfortunately common in restaurant kitchens globally, and Thailand is no exception. Tight receiving procedures (check deliveries against purchase orders, weigh everything), limited storage access, and inventory counts are the standard controls.

## Labour Cost Benchmarks: Thailand vs. Western Countries

One of Thailand's structural advantages for restaurant operators is lower labour cost relative to revenue. Here is how Thailand compares:

**Thailand:** 22-35% of revenue (depending on segment)

**United States:** 30-35% of revenue (and rising with minimum wage increases)

**United Kingdom:** 28-35% of revenue

**Australia:** 32-40% of revenue (highest among major markets)

**Singapore:** 28-35% of revenue (partially offset by service charge)

The gap is real but narrowing. Thailand's minimum wage has increased from 300 THB/day in 2013 to 400 THB/day in 2025 for Bangkok -- a 33% increase. Skilled kitchen staff command 20,000-45,000 THB/month depending on position and experience, and turnover rates in Thai restaurants run 40-60% annually, which creates constant recruitment and training costs.

### Where Labour Dollars Go

For a typical casual dining restaurant in Bangkok with 16 staff:

**Kitchen (7 staff):** 40-45% of total labour cost. Head chef, sous chef, line cooks, prep cooks. This is the team that most directly affects food quality and consistency.

**Front of house (7 staff):** 35-40% of total labour cost. Restaurant manager, servers, host, bartender, runner. This team affects customer experience and average check (through upselling).

**Back office (1-2 staff, often part-time):** 5-10% of total labour cost. Purchasing, admin, receiving.

**Employer costs on top of wages:** Social security (5%, capped at 750 THB/month per employee), Workmen's Compensation Fund (0.2-0.5%), and service charge distribution (if applicable). These add 6-12% on top of base wages.

<Callout type="info">
Service charge distribution is the wildcard in Thai restaurant labour cost analysis. A restaurant collecting 10% service charge on dine-in bills and distributing 90% to staff effectively increases labour cost by 7-9% of dine-in revenue. Some operators include service charge distribution in their labour cost calculation; others treat it as a pass-through. Be consistent in your approach and make sure you are comparing like-with-like when benchmarking against other restaurants. I include it in labour cost because it is a real cost of employing staff, even though it is funded by a specific revenue line.
</Callout>

## Operating Expense Breakdown

Beyond COGS, labour, and rent, a restaurant has a constellation of smaller expenses that collectively account for 8-15% of revenue. Here is where that money typically goes:

**Delivery platform commissions: 0-12% of total revenue.** This depends entirely on your delivery mix. A restaurant doing 30% of revenue through delivery platforms at 32% commission is paying 9.6% of total revenue in commissions alone. A restaurant with no delivery pays 0%. This single line item can be the difference between a profitable and unprofitable restaurant.

**Marketing: 2-5% of revenue.** New restaurants need to spend at the higher end to build awareness, while established restaurants with strong word-of-mouth can maintain at 2-3%. Social media content creation, paid ads (Facebook, Instagram, Google), food blogger collaborations, and printed collateral.

**Utilities: 3-6% of revenue.** Electricity is the big one. A mid-size Bangkok restaurant with a full kitchen, walk-in cooler, and air-conditioning spends 25,000-50,000 THB/month on electricity. Water adds 3,000-8,000 THB. Gas (if not electric kitchen) adds 5,000-15,000 THB.

**Payment processing: 1.5-3% of card and QR revenue.** Credit card processing fees in Thailand are 1.8-2.5% for Visa/Mastercard. QR (PromptPay) is effectively free for small transactions but may have nominal fees for merchant accounts. As cash usage declines (now 30-40% of transactions at most Bangkok restaurants, down from 60%+ five years ago), processing fees are a growing expense.

**Repairs and maintenance: 1-2% of revenue.** Kitchen equipment breaks. Plumbing fails. Air conditioning units need servicing. Budget 1-2% of revenue for ongoing maintenance, and expect to spend more in the first year (equipment issues surface early) and after year 5 (when original equipment starts reaching end of life).

**Accounting and legal: 1-2% of revenue.** Monthly bookkeeping (8,000-25,000 THB), annual audit (20,000-50,000 THB), legal consultation as needed. The [accounting guide](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand) covers these costs in detail.

**Technology and POS: 0.5-1% of revenue.** POS subscription (1,000-4,000 THB/month), reservation system, accounting software, internet, and IT support.

**Everything else: 1-2% of revenue.** Pest control, cleaning supplies, toiletries, uniforms, flowers, staff training, and the inevitable miscellaneous expenses that defy categorization.

## Delivery-Only Margin Differences

The delivery margin question deserves its own analysis because delivery is growing and the margin structure is fundamentally different from dine-in.

### Side-by-Side Comparison: Same Menu Item, Dine-In vs. Delivery

Consider a pad kra pao rice bowl that sells for 220 THB dine-in and 220 THB on Grab:

**Dine-in:**
- Revenue: 220 THB (plus 22 THB service charge, plus 17 THB VAT = 259 THB total to customer)
- Food cost: 66 THB (30%)
- Gross margin per dish: 154 THB (plus service charge income)
- Platform commission: 0 THB
- Packaging: 0 THB
- **Net contribution per dish: ~154 THB**

**Delivery (Grab):**
- Revenue: 220 THB (platform shows price to customer after markup)
- Food cost: 66 THB (30%)
- Platform commission at 32%: 70.40 THB
- Packaging: 8 THB
- **Net contribution per dish: ~75.60 THB**

The delivery dish generates roughly half the margin of the dine-in dish. At scale, this changes your overall profitability materially. A restaurant that shifts from 10% delivery mix to 30% delivery mix -- all else equal -- sees net margin drop by 2-3 percentage points.

### When Delivery Makes Sense Despite Lower Margins

Delivery makes financial sense when:

**Your kitchen has unused capacity.** If your kitchen can produce 200 meals per service but you are only serving 120 dine-in, the incremental delivery orders use capacity that would otherwise sit idle. The marginal cost of those delivery orders is just food cost + packaging + commission -- there is no incremental labour or rent.

**You are building brand awareness.** Delivery platforms are a discovery channel. Some customers order delivery first, then visit the restaurant. The lifetime value of converting a delivery customer to a dine-in regular justifies the lower initial margin.

**You run a cloud kitchen.** Without dine-in revenue to offset delivery margins, the entire business model must work within delivery economics. This means simple menus, tight food cost control, and negotiated commission rates.

**Delivery makes less sense when** your kitchen is already at capacity (delivery orders compete with higher-margin dine-in orders), when your food does not travel well (presentation-dependent dishes lose value in delivery), or when the platform's commission rate exceeds 35%.

## Seasonal Margin Fluctuations

Margins are not constant throughout the year. Thailand's seasonal patterns affect revenue, which directly impacts margins because fixed costs remain constant.

**Peak season (November-January):** Revenue rises 15-25% above average. Fixed costs stay flat, so operating margin expands by 3-7 percentage points. A restaurant running 10% EBITDA margin in an average month might hit 14-17% in December. This is when cash accumulates and reserves build.

**Low season (April-September):** Revenue drops 15-30% below peak. Fixed costs stay flat, so operating margin contracts. A restaurant running 10% EBITDA margin in an average month might drop to 4-6% EBITDA -- or even turn negative in the weakest months. This is when cash reserves get consumed.

**The annual margin is what matters.** A restaurant that runs -2% in June and +17% in December may average 8% for the year, which is healthy. The danger is not the low-season margin itself -- it is not having the cash reserves to survive it. See the [cash flow management guide](/blog/restaurant-cash-flow-management-thailand) for how to plan around seasonal swings.

<Callout type="warning">
Tourist-dependent restaurants face the widest seasonal margin swings. A restaurant in Patong Beach (Phuket) might run 20% EBITDA in January and -15% in July. The annual net margin might be 5%, which sounds fine, but the business needs enough cash reserves to survive 4-5 months of losses. Many tourist-area restaurants close during low season to avoid burning cash -- this is a rational financial decision, not a sign of failure.
</Callout>

## Strategies to Improve Margins by 5-10%

If your net margin is 4% and you want it to be 10%, you need to find 6 percentage points. Here are the strategies that actually work, ranked by typical impact:

### 1. Fix Food Cost (Potential: 2-5 Points)

This is where most margin improvement lives. If your food cost is 35% and the benchmark for your segment is 30%, closing that 5-point gap drops straight to the bottom line.

**Menu engineering.** Analyze your POS sales mix to identify high-sellers with poor margins and low-sellers with high margins. Redesign your menu to steer customers toward the profitable items. A well-executed menu engineering project typically reduces blended food cost by 2-3 percentage points. The [menu engineering guide](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) covers the methodology.

**Supplier renegotiation.** If you have been with the same suppliers for a year or more without reviewing pricing, you are probably overpaying. Get quotes from 2-3 alternative suppliers for your top 20 ingredients by cost. Even a 5% reduction on your top 20 items (which represent 60-70% of total food cost) saves 1-2 percentage points of food cost.

**Waste reduction.** Implement a daily waste log. Track everything that goes in the bin. Challenge the kitchen to reduce waste by 1% of purchases. For a restaurant buying 300,000 THB in food per month, a 1% waste reduction saves 3,000 THB/month -- small individually, but it compounds with other improvements.

**Portion standardization.** Re-spec every dish with precise gram weights for proteins and key ingredients. Post recipe cards with photos at every station. Spot-check plates during service. Portion control is a daily discipline, not a one-time project.

### 2. Grow Beverage Revenue (Potential: 2-4 Points)

Beverages have the highest margin of any menu category. Increasing beverage revenue as a share of total revenue improves your blended margin without changing anything in the kitchen.

**Increase beverage attachment rate.** Train servers to suggest a specific drink with every order, not "would you like a drink?" but "our lime basil soda goes perfectly with the larb -- can I get you one?" The difference between a 40% and a 60% beverage attachment rate on a 100-seat restaurant is 80,000-120,000 THB per month in high-margin revenue.

**Develop a cocktail programme.** If you are only serving beer and wine, you are missing the highest-margin category. A cocktail programme with 8-10 signature drinks at 320-450 THB each, running 78-82% gross margin, can transform your P&L. The initial investment (equipment, training, ingredients) is modest compared to the margin impact.

**Create a visible beverage menu.** Many Thai restaurants have a beverage menu hidden on the last page of the food menu or not at all. Create a separate, well-designed beverage menu. Place it on the table. Feature drinks on table tents and digital boards.

### 3. Optimize Labour Scheduling (Potential: 1-3 Points)

Most restaurants are overstaffed during slow periods and understaffed during peak periods. Better scheduling fixes both problems.

**Analyze hourly revenue patterns.** Your POS shows revenue by hour. Staff to match the curve. If Tuesday lunch generates 40% less revenue than Saturday dinner, you do not need the same staffing level. Use part-time staff and split shifts to match labour to demand.

**Cross-train aggressively.** A server who can also work prep during the afternoon gap between lunch and dinner is more valuable than two separate employees. Cross-training reduces the total headcount needed and improves scheduling flexibility.

**Measure revenue per labour hour.** Target 500-800 THB per labour hour for casual dining. If you are below 500, you are either overstaffed or underperforming on revenue. Track it weekly.

### 4. Reduce Delivery Commission Impact (Potential: 1-3 Points)

If delivery is 20%+ of your revenue, the commission line is a significant margin drag.

**Negotiate rates.** Platforms will negotiate for high-volume merchants, and restaurants doing 200+ orders per month through a single platform often secure commission reductions of 2-5 percentage points. Ask. The standard rate is not a fixed rate.

**Build direct ordering channels.** A LINE Official Account, an Instagram-linked ordering page, or a simple website ordering system (using MHA or similar) eliminates the 30-35% commission entirely. You pay for delivery through your own riders (200-500 THB/order in central Bangkok) or use a delivery-only fleet service. The net saving can be 15-25% of the order value.

**Adjust delivery menu pricing.** Some restaurants charge 10-15% more on delivery platforms than dine-in. This partially offsets the commission. Platforms allow different pricing, and most customers accept it because they are paying for convenience.

### 5. Review Occupancy Costs (Potential: 1-2 Points)

**Renegotiate rent at lease renewal.** Landlords in Bangkok's restaurant scene know that vacancy is expensive. If you have been a reliable tenant for 3+ years, you have leverage. Even a 5% rent reduction on a 120,000 THB/month lease saves 72,000 THB per year -- roughly 0.6% of revenue for a 1 million THB/month restaurant.

**Reduce electricity.** Energy-efficient equipment (inverter air conditioning, LED lighting, energy-rated refrigeration) can cut electricity bills by 15-25%. The payback period on upgrading to inverter AC is typically 12-18 months. Kitchen exhaust system optimization reduces the load on AC by extracting heat more efficiently.

**Sublease unused space or hours.** If you only operate dinner service, can you sublease the kitchen for a lunch-only concept or a cloud kitchen brand? Some restaurants generate 20,000-40,000 THB/month from daytime subleases.

<Callout type="tip">
Margin improvement is cumulative. You do not need one dramatic change -- you need five small improvements that each contribute 1-2 percentage points. Reduce food cost by 2 points, grow beverage revenue by 1 point, optimize scheduling for 1 point, negotiate delivery commission down by 1 point, and reduce electricity by 0.5 points. Combined: 5.5 percentage points of margin improvement. On 1 million THB/month in revenue, that is 55,000 THB more per month hitting the bottom line -- 660,000 THB per year.
</Callout>

## When to Worry: Red Flag Margin Levels

Not every margin level is survivable. Here are the thresholds that signal serious problems:

**Food cost above 38%:** Unless you are running an ultra-premium concept with very high check averages, food cost above 38% means your menu pricing, purchasing, or portion control is broken. Investigate immediately.

**Labour cost above 38%:** Either you are significantly overstaffed, your revenue is too low for your team size, or you are in a segment (fine dining) where high labour cost is the norm. If you are in casual dining at 38%+ labour cost, something is wrong.

**Prime cost above 70%:** At this level, the remaining 30% of revenue cannot cover rent, utilities, operating expenses, and profit. The business model needs structural change, not tweaking. Either revenue must increase significantly, or costs must be fundamentally restructured.

**Rent above 15% of revenue:** The location is too expensive for the revenue the restaurant generates. Either increase revenue (more covers, higher check, longer hours) or negotiate a rent reduction. If neither is possible, relocation may be the only path to profitability.

**Net margin below 0% for 6+ consecutive months:** A restaurant that loses money for six months in a row without a clear turnaround plan (seasonal recovery, pending renovation completion, post-opening ramp-up) is a restaurant that should seriously evaluate whether to continue operating. Sustained losses consume cash reserves and personal savings. The earlier you address structural problems, the more options you have.

**EBITDA negative for 3+ months outside of low season:** If you cannot generate positive operating cash flow during shoulder or peak months, the restaurant is not viable in its current form. Seasonal losses in April-June can be survived with reserves; operating losses in November-January cannot be excused by seasonality.

## How Margins Change as You Scale

Opening a second location does not double your profit. Here is why, and what actually happens to margins as you grow:

### Single Location to Two Locations

**Costs that do NOT double:** Management overhead (you can share a GM across two locations initially), accounting (bulk pricing), marketing (shared social media, shared brand), and potentially some kitchen prep (central prep for two locations).

**Costs that DO double (or more):** Rent, most labour, utilities, insurance, maintenance, and inventory. The second location also requires its own capex and working capital.

**What happens to margin:** Most operators see a temporary margin compression of 3-5 percentage points when opening a second location, recovering over 12-18 months as the new location matures. The net margin on the second location is typically lower than the first for the first 12 months because you are learning a new neighborhood, training a new team, and splitting management attention.

### Three to Five Locations

This is where scale advantages start to appear:

**Supplier pricing improves.** Purchasing 3-5x the volume from suppliers unlocks meaningful discounts -- typically 5-10% on major categories.

**Central kitchen becomes viable.** A shared prep kitchen that serves 3-5 locations reduces labour cost at each location and improves consistency. The central kitchen adds its own cost, but the net effect is usually a 2-3 percentage point improvement in blended food cost.

**Management structure professionalizes.** You hire an operations manager, standardize systems, and reduce dependency on any single location's performance. This adds cost but reduces variance.

**Marketing scales efficiently.** The marketing budget that covered one location now covers five, with minimal incremental spend.

**Net margin at 3-5 locations:** Typically 1-3 percentage points higher than a single-location operation, assuming strong systems and management. A single-location restaurant running 6% net margin might run 8-9% across a 4-location operation.

### The Scaling Trap

Scaling only improves margins if the individual locations are fundamentally profitable. Scaling an unprofitable concept -- opening a second location of a restaurant that is losing money, hoping volume will fix the problem -- is one of the fastest ways to go bankrupt in the restaurant business. Fix the single-location economics first. If your unit economics work at one location, they will work better at five. If they do not work at one, they will not work at five either.
