Every year I get dozens of inquiries from aspiring restaurateurs who assume Bangkok is the only option. They arrive with a concept, a budget, and a conviction that if they are not on Sukhumvit or Silom, they might as well not bother. I understand the impulse. Bangkok is where the money is, where the press is, where the Michelin stars are concentrated. But Bangkok is also where the competition is most brutal, rents are highest, and the margin for error is thinnest.

In 2025, the Department of Business Development recorded over 2,600 new food service company registrations outside of Bangkok. That is not a rounding error. The growth in secondary cities is being driven by real structural forces: tourism diversification, digital nomad migration, the Eastern Economic Corridor, and a Thai middle class that is spending more on dining out in their home provinces.

I have consulted on restaurant openings in all five cities covered in this article. Some of my most successful projects were not in Bangkok at all. This guide will give you the data you need to decide whether your concept belongs in the capital or somewhere with lower rent, less competition, and a customer base that is hungry for quality.

## Why Look Beyond Bangkok

Bangkok is a world-class restaurant city, with 441 Michelin-recognized venues as of the 2026 guide and the highest concentration of food-obsessed consumers in Southeast Asia. But it also has serious structural challenges for new operators.

**Market saturation is real.** Bangkok recorded 1,287 new food service registrations in 2025 alone, which works out to roughly 25 new restaurants opening every week. The closure rate hovers around 60% within the first two years. You are entering one of the most competitive dining markets in Asia.

**Rent eats your margins.** Prime Bangkok locations command 80,000 to 300,000 THB per month or more. In Thonglor, a 150-square-meter space can cost 150,000-250,000 THB monthly before you serve a single plate. That rent burden means you need consistently high revenue just to survive, let alone profit.

**Labor competition is fierce.** Every new restaurant opening in Bangkok is competing for the same pool of experienced kitchen and service staff. Wage inflation in Bangkok's F&B sector has outpaced other cities by 15-20% since 2023.

<Callout type="info">
The Michelin Guide expanded to cover Phuket and Phang Nga in 2024, and their 2026 edition includes 19 recognized venues in Phuket alone. This signals a broader trend: Thailand's culinary reputation is decentralizing, and with it, consumer expectations and spending.
</Callout>

Meanwhile, secondary cities offer advantages that Bangkok cannot match: lower startup costs, less saturated markets, access to tourism spending without Bangkok-level competition, and in several cases, a growing pool of high-spending residents and visitors who are actively looking for quality dining options that do not yet exist.

The question is not whether to open outside Bangkok. The question is which city matches your concept, your budget, and your risk tolerance.

## How I Ranked These Cities

I evaluated each city across four dimensions:

1. **New restaurant registrations (2025).** A proxy for market activity and confidence. High registration numbers indicate a growing market. Very high numbers indicate possible saturation.
2. **Michelin Guide presence.** The guide's expansion signals international dining credibility and attracts quality-conscious consumers. It also raises the bar for everyone.
3. **Tourism growth.** Tourist spending accounts for 30-60% of restaurant revenue in most Thai cities outside Bangkok. Growth trends matter more than absolute numbers.
4. **Rent levels.** The single biggest fixed cost. Lower rent gives you more runway to find your market.

Here is each city in detail.

## City 1: Bangkok — The Benchmark

**New registrations (2025):** 1,287
**Michelin venues (2026 guide):** 441
**Tourism:** 35+ million international arrivals (2025)
**Rent range:** 80,000-300,000+ THB/month for prime dining locations

Bangkok does not need an introduction — it is the undisputed center of Thailand's restaurant industry. But I include it here as the benchmark against which every other city should be measured, because the decision to open elsewhere is really a decision about what trade-offs you are willing to make relative to Bangkok.

### What Bangkok Gives You

**The largest customer pool in Thailand.** Bangkok's metro area has over 10 million residents plus millions of annual tourists. No other Thai city comes close to this volume. For concepts that need raw foot traffic and population density, Bangkok is irreplaceable.

**Infrastructure and supply chain.** Every ingredient, every piece of equipment, every service provider you might need exists in Bangkok: specialty importers, high-end pastry suppliers, commercial kitchen equipment dealers, F&B-specialized architects, experienced restaurant consultants.

**Press and visibility.** Food media, influencers, and international press are concentrated in Bangkok. A single positive review from a major Bangkok food critic or influencer can fill your restaurant for weeks. Getting that kind of organic visibility in Chiang Mai or Pattaya is harder.

**Michelin credibility.** If you are building a fine dining or ambitious casual concept with aspirations for recognition, Bangkok is where the Michelin inspectors dine most frequently. The 441 recognized venues create a competitive ecosystem that pushes everyone to be better.

### What Bangkok Takes From You

**Money.** A casual dining opening in a decent Bangkok location runs 3-6 million THB. The same concept in Chiang Mai costs 1.5-3 million THB. That difference is almost entirely rent and renovation costs.

**Time.** Bangkok's licensing process is slower than most provincial cities, partly because of volume and partly because of more complex municipal bureaucracy. Expect 3-4 months of licensing work in Bangkok versus 4-8 weeks in smaller cities.

**Peace of mind.** Competition is relentless. A new concept opens near you every month. Delivery platforms constantly reshape consumer behavior. Staff get poached by the restaurant across the street. Running a Bangkok restaurant requires a level of operational intensity that can burn out even experienced operators.

<Callout type="tip">
If your concept requires a large, diverse customer base and you have a budget north of 5 million THB, Bangkok remains the strongest choice. But if your concept could thrive with a smaller, more loyal customer base and lower overhead, the cities below deserve serious consideration.
</Callout>

### Best Bangkok Neighborhoods for New Restaurants (2026)

The most interesting openings I am seeing in Bangkok right now are not in Thonglor or Silom. They are in:

- **Charoen Krung/Talad Noi.** Bangkok's creative district. Former warehouses and shophouses converted into restaurants, galleries, and bars. Rent: 200-500 THB/sqm. Strong with design-conscious locals and tourists.
- **Ari/Pradipat.** The neighborhood dining scene for young Thai professionals. Rent: 300-700 THB/sqm. Loyal repeat customer base.
- **Sam Yan/Chula area.** University district with massive foot traffic and a growing creative food scene anchored by Samyan Mitrtown. Rent: 250-500 THB/sqm.
- **Phra Khanong/Udom Suk.** Eastern Sukhumvit's affordable alternative. Rapidly developing with new condos and a mixed Thai-expat population. Rent: 200-500 THB/sqm.

For a deep dive on Bangkok neighborhoods and how to evaluate locations, see my guide on [how to find a restaurant location in Bangkok](/blog/how-to-find-restaurant-location-bangkok).

## City 2: Chonburi/Pattaya — The Eastern Corridor Opportunity

**New registrations (2025):** 481
**Michelin venues (2026 guide):** Not yet covered (expected in future expansion)
**Tourism:** 12+ million visitors (2025), up 22% from 2023
**Rent range:** 25,000-120,000 THB/month for prime dining locations

Pattaya has spent decades fighting its reputation as a nightlife-only destination. That fight is being won. The city has undergone a transformation driven by three forces: the Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC) attracting international business investment, a growing Russian and Chinese residential expat community, and domestic Thai tourism that has discovered Pattaya's improving beachfront and family-friendly developments.

### Why Chonburi/Pattaya Is Worth Considering

**EEC investment is real money.** The Eastern Economic Corridor, the Thai government's flagship industrial development program, has brought over 600 billion THB in investment commitments to the Chonburi/Rayong corridor since 2018. That investment brings engineers, managers, and executives who need to eat. Corporate dining, business lunches, and quality casual restaurants are underserved relative to the growing professional population.

**Expat residential growth.** Pattaya's condo market has expanded significantly, building a growing base of long-term expat residents from Russia, China, India, Scandinavia, and the UK. These residents dine out frequently and are willing to pay for quality.

**Lower rent, high revenue potential.** A restaurant that would cost 200,000 THB/month in Bangkok's Thonglor might cost 50,000-80,000 THB in central Pattaya, and still capture significant tourist and expat spending.

### Emerging Areas in Chonburi/Pattaya

**Jomtien and Na Jomtien.** The new development frontier. Several major hotel and condo projects are underway, the beachfront is less crowded than central Pattaya, and the dining scene is still emerging. This is where I would look for a medium-term play: lower rent today, growing traffic over the next 2-3 years.

**Pratumnak Hill.** The quieter, more upscale area between Central Pattaya and Jomtien. A small but growing collection of quality restaurants. Expat residents with higher spending power. Rent: 30,000-80,000 THB/month.

**Sriracha.** Often overlooked, but Sriracha has the largest Japanese expat community outside of Bangkok, driven by automotive manufacturing. Japanese and fusion restaurants do well here with a loyal customer base.

<Callout type="warning">
Pattaya's restaurant market is highly seasonal and tourism-dependent. High season (November-March) can generate 2-3x the revenue of low season (May-September). Budget your working capital for the low months. If you plan to open in Pattaya, do not sign a lease and open in September expecting immediate returns.
</Callout>

### Pattaya Pros and Cons

**Pros:**
- Second-highest new restaurant registrations in Thailand
- Growing year-round residential customer base (not just tourists)
- EEC brings high-income professionals
- Rent 50-70% lower than equivalent Bangkok locations
- Relatively easy logistics (90 minutes from Bangkok, good highway access)

**Cons:**
- No Michelin Guide coverage yet (less international credibility)
- Reputation still carries nightlife stigma for some demographics
- Heavily tourism-dependent in some areas
- Seasonal revenue swings (plan for 40-50% dip in low season)
- Limited local supply chain for premium ingredients (source from Bangkok)

## City 3: Phuket — Peak Revenue, Peak Competition

**New registrations (2025):** 371
**Michelin venues (2026 guide):** 19
**Tourism:** 14+ million visitors (2025), +66% tourism interest growth (White Lotus effect)
**Rent range:** 40,000-200,000 THB/month for prime dining locations

Phuket is Thailand's most expensive island and its most lucrative restaurant market outside Bangkok. The numbers are striking: some Patong and Bangtao restaurants generate monthly revenues that exceed comparable Bangkok venues, driven by tourist spending at significantly higher average checks. But Phuket is also a market where seasonality can make or break you, and where the recent "White Lotus effect" has supercharged both demand and competition simultaneously.

### The White Lotus Effect

The HBO series White Lotus, which featured luxury Thai hospitality in its third season, created a measurable surge in tourism interest. Google Trends data showed a 66% increase in "Phuket restaurant" searches in the months following the show's airing, luxury hotel bookings on the island spiked, and with those tourists came demand for high-quality dining experiences that matched the aspirational lifestyle depicted on screen.

This is both an opportunity and a trap. The opportunity is that high-spending tourists are actively looking for memorable dining experiences and are willing to pay 2,000-5,000 THB per person for them. The trap is that many operators are rushing to open upscale venues simultaneously, which will inevitably lead to a shakeout in 18-24 months.

### Where to Open in Phuket

**Old Town Phuket.** The cultural heart of the island. Sino-Portuguese architecture, a growing art and cafe scene, and strong foot traffic from both tourists and local residents. Rent: 30,000-80,000 THB/month. This is the most interesting area for concept-driven restaurants that do not depend on beachfront location. Several Michelin-recognized venues are here.

**Bangtao/Cherngtalay.** Phuket's luxury belt. Proximity to Laguna resort complex and multiple high-end hotels. High-spending international tourists. Rent: 60,000-200,000 THB/month. Best for upscale casual and fine dining.

**Kamala/Kata.** More relaxed beach areas with growing food scenes. Lower rent than Bangtao: 40,000-100,000 THB/month. Good for mid-range restaurants targeting European tourists and long-stay visitors.

**Rawai.** Southern Phuket. Strong with expat residents and families. Less tourist-dependent than other areas. Rent: 25,000-60,000 THB/month. Underserved in quality dining.

### Phuket's Michelin Momentum

With 19 Michelin-recognized venues in the 2026 guide, including Bib Gourmand, Michelin Selected, and Starred restaurants, Phuket has become Thailand's second most important culinary destination after Bangkok. The inspectors' presence validates the market and attracts food-focused travelers who plan their trips around restaurant reservations.

<Callout type="info">
The Michelin Guide Phuket expansion has created a halo effect for the entire island's dining scene. Restaurants that are not in the guide but operate at a high level benefit from the increased foot traffic and elevated consumer expectations. If you are opening in Phuket, build your kitchen and service to Michelin-adjacent standards even if you are not pursuing a star. The rising tide lifts all boats.
</Callout>

### Phuket Pros and Cons

**Pros:**
- Highest tourist spending per capita outside Bangkok
- 19 Michelin venues drive food tourism
- White Lotus effect boosting luxury market demand
- Peak season revenue can be extraordinary (some venues do 60-70% of annual revenue in 5 months)
- Growing year-round expat and digital nomad community

**Cons:**
- Extreme seasonality (May-October is dramatically slower)
- Highest island rents in Thailand
- Supply chain challenges (premium ingredients shipped from Bangkok, 15-20% markup)
- Staff housing costs (housing in Phuket is expensive; some operators provide staff accommodation)
- New competition flooding the market post-White Lotus

## City 4: Chiang Mai — The Digital Nomad Kitchen

**New registrations (2025):** 271
**Michelin venues (2026 guide):** 18
**Tourism:** 10+ million visitors (2025), strong digital nomad growth
**Rent range:** 15,000-60,000 THB/month for prime dining locations

Chiang Mai is my sleeper pick for restaurateurs on a tighter budget, because the rent-to-quality ratio is the best in Thailand. You can open a beautiful, well-equipped restaurant on Nimmanhaemin, the city's trendiest dining street, for the same money that gets you a mediocre space in Bangkok's Sukhumvit area. And the customer base, while smaller, is loyal and food-literate.

### Why Chiang Mai Works for Restaurants

**The digital nomad economy is structural, not cyclical.** Chiang Mai has been Southeast Asia's top digital nomad hub for nearly a decade. The community numbers tens of thousands and turns over continuously, which means a steady stream of new customers discovering your restaurant. These are people who work remotely, eat out frequently (often 2-3 meals per day), and share restaurant recommendations on social media and nomad forums.

**Michelin recognition changed the game.** When the guide expanded to Chiang Mai, it validated what locals already knew: the city's food scene is exceptional. The 18 recognized venues span from street food (Bib Gourmand) to fine dining, and the guide's presence has attracted food-focused travelers who specifically visit Chiang Mai to eat.

**Costs are dramatically lower.** Not just rent. Staff wages are 20-30% below Bangkok levels. Local produce from the surrounding agricultural regions is abundant and cheap. Utility costs are lower. A restaurant that needs 500,000 THB/month in revenue to break even in Bangkok might break even at 200,000-300,000 THB/month in Chiang Mai.

### Nimmanhaemin vs. Old City: Two Different Markets

**Nimmanhaemin and surrounding sois** are where the trendy cafes, international restaurants, and nightlife concentrate. The customer mix is young Thais, digital nomads, university students (Chiang Mai University is adjacent), and tourists. This is the higher-rent end of Chiang Mai (30,000-60,000 THB/month for a good space), but it is still a fraction of Bangkok prices. Foot traffic is strong, especially evenings and weekends. Competition is growing but not yet at Bangkok saturation levels.

**The Old City** (within the moat) attracts a different crowd: cultural tourists, budget travelers, and a loyal community of long-stay expats and retirees. Rent is lower (15,000-35,000 THB/month), the atmosphere is more relaxed, and the customer base is less trend-driven but highly loyal. Some of Chiang Mai's best restaurants are tucked into quiet Old City sois with minimal signage.

<Callout type="tip">
Chiang Mai's restaurant market rewards authenticity over trendiness. The most successful venues I have worked with here are not Instagram-optimized concept restaurants. They are places with genuine stories, passionate owners, and food that reflects a real point of view. If you are the kind of operator who cares more about cooking well than marketing well, Chiang Mai might be your city.
</Callout>

### The University Advantage

Chiang Mai University and several other institutions bring over 100,000 students to the city. This creates a massive base of price-sensitive but frequent diners. For quick-service, cafe, and affordable casual concepts, the student market provides reliable volume. And unlike tourist traffic, it is consistent year-round (aside from university holidays in March-April and October).

### Chiang Mai Pros and Cons

**Pros:**
- Lowest rent of any major Thai restaurant market
- 18 Michelin venues validate the food scene
- Steady digital nomad customer base
- Excellent local produce (northern Thai ingredients, organic farms, specialty coffee from Doi Suthep region)
- Relaxed lifestyle (easier to recruit staff who want quality of life)
- Manageable competition relative to market size

**Cons:**
- Smaller total market than Bangkok, Phuket, or Pattaya
- Burning season (February-April) significantly reduces tourism and makes outdoor dining unpleasant
- Lower average checks than Bangkok or Phuket (expect 200-600 THB average, not 500-2,000 THB)
- Limited late-night dining culture compared to Bangkok
- Fewer high-spending domestic Thai tourists (Chiang Mai attracts budget-conscious Thai travelers)

## City 5: Surat Thani/Koh Samui — The Luxury Island Play

**New registrations (2025):** 211
**Michelin venues (2026 guide):** Not yet covered
**Tourism:** Strong recovery, +88% tourism interest growth for Samui (White Lotus halo effect)
**Rent range:** 30,000-150,000 THB/month for prime dining locations (Samui); much lower on mainland

Koh Samui operates in a different universe from mainland Thailand — a luxury island destination where tourists arrive expecting premium experiences and are willing to pay for them. The restaurant market is smaller, but the per-customer spending is among the highest in the country.

### The White Lotus Halo Effect

While the White Lotus effect hit Phuket directly, Samui benefited enormously from the general surge in interest in Thai luxury island experiences. Google Trends data showed an 88% increase in "Koh Samui restaurant" searches in 2025. The island's luxury hotel sector, anchored by properties like Four Seasons, Six Senses, and Ritz-Carlton, generates a steady stream of high-net-worth diners looking for quality restaurants outside their hotel.

### Where to Open on Samui

**Fisherman's Village (Bophut).** The island's dining epicenter. A walkable strip of restaurants, bars, and shops with consistent foot traffic. Rent: 40,000-100,000 THB/month. Best for mid-range to upscale casual restaurants targeting tourists.

**Chaweng.** The busiest tourist area with the highest foot traffic. Rent: 50,000-150,000 THB/month. Higher volume, more competition, and a more transient customer base. Good for high-volume casual dining.

**Maenam/Lamai.** Quieter areas with growing expat communities and boutique hotels. Rent: 30,000-70,000 THB/month. Underserved in quality dining. Strong potential for destination restaurants that can draw diners from across the island.

<Callout type="warning">
Samui's seasonality is more extreme than Phuket's. The Gulf of Thailand monsoon season (October-December) can reduce tourist arrivals by 60-70% compared to peak months. Some Samui restaurants close entirely during the worst months. Your financial model must account for 3-4 months of significantly reduced revenue. Build a cash reserve equal to at least 4 months of fixed costs.
</Callout>

### The Island Premium

Everything costs more on an island. Ingredients shipped from the mainland carry a 15-25% premium. Staff who relocate to Samui expect housing support (budget 5,000-8,000 THB/month per employee for shared accommodation). Equipment delivery and maintenance involve logistical complexity. These costs are manageable if your average check reflects the premium market, but they can destroy margins for mid-range or budget concepts.

### Surat Thani/Koh Samui Pros and Cons

**Pros:**
- Highest average tourist spending per visit among Thai islands
- White Lotus halo effect driving luxury tourism growth
- Less competition than Phuket for comparable quality levels
- Loyal expat community provides year-round base
- Strong luxury hotel sector drives high-end dining demand

**Cons:**
- Extreme seasonality (Gulf monsoon October-December)
- Island logistics premium on all supplies (15-25% above mainland)
- No Michelin Guide coverage yet
- Smaller labor pool (must recruit from mainland or provide housing)
- Infrastructure limitations (power outages, water supply issues during dry season)
- Flight-dependent tourism (fewer direct international flights than Phuket)

## Sleeper Market: Isaan

I want to mention one region that does not fit neatly into a city ranking but deserves attention: Isaan, Thailand's northeastern plateau.

The Michelin Guide's 2026 edition includes 28 Bib Gourmand venues across Isaan, from Khon Kaen to Udon Thani to Nakhon Ratchasima. That is serious recognition for a region that most foreign restaurateurs never consider. Isaan has Thailand's lowest operating costs, a fiercely loyal local dining culture, and a growing middle class driven by agricultural modernization and cross-border trade with Laos and Vietnam.

**Khon Kaen** is the most interesting Isaan city for restaurant investment. It is a university town with over 40,000 students, a growing tech sector, and direct flights to Bangkok. Rent for a quality restaurant space: 10,000-30,000 THB/month. A full restaurant opening in Khon Kaen can cost under 1 million THB.

The caveat is that Isaan's dining market is almost entirely Thai. If your concept requires a significant expat or tourist customer base, Isaan is not the right fit. But if you can build a restaurant that resonates with Thai consumers at Isaan price points, the economics are compelling.

## How to Decide: A Framework

After helping clients choose locations across all five cities, I have boiled the decision down to five questions:

**1. What is your total budget?**
If you have under 2 million THB, Bangkok is very difficult. Chiang Mai and Pattaya offer viable options at that price point. If you have 5 million THB or more, all five cities are in play.

**2. Who is your customer?**
Expats and tourists? Phuket, Samui, or Pattaya. Young professionals and digital nomads? Chiang Mai or Bangkok's emerging neighborhoods. Affluent Thais? Bangkok or Chiang Mai. Corporate and business dining? Bangkok or Pattaya (EEC corridor).

**3. Can you handle seasonality?**
Phuket and Samui have extreme seasonal swings. If you need consistent monthly revenue, Bangkok and Chiang Mai are more stable. Pattaya falls in between.

**4. Do you need Michelin credibility?**
If recognition and press are part of your business strategy, Bangkok (441 venues), Phuket (19 venues), or Chiang Mai (18 venues) are where the inspectors eat. Pattaya and Samui are not yet covered.

**5. Are you a hands-on or absentee operator?**
If you plan to manage the restaurant yourself, lifestyle factors matter. Running a restaurant in Chiang Mai is a fundamentally different daily experience than running one in central Bangkok. If you are an absentee operator, Bangkok's deeper management talent pool is an advantage.

<Callout type="tip">
Before committing to any city, spend at least two weeks there during low season. Eat at 20+ restaurants. Talk to other operators. Walk the neighborhoods at different times of day. The numbers in this article give you a starting framework, but ground truth always wins. Every city looks great during high season. Low season reveals the real market.
</Callout>

## The Foreign Ownership Factor

The legal structure for restaurant ownership is the same across all Thai cities. The [Foreign Business Act applies equally](/blog/foreigner-opening-restaurant-thailand) whether you are in Bangkok or Chiang Mai. You will need a Thai limited company with at least 51% Thai ownership (or an alternative structure like BOI promotion or Treaty of Amity for US citizens).

However, the practical experience of navigating bureaucracy does vary by location. Bangkok has the most law firms and consultants experienced in setting up restaurant companies for foreigners. In smaller cities, you may need to bring in Bangkok-based legal counsel for company registration and then work with a local accountant for ongoing compliance.

For the full guide on foreign ownership structures and legal requirements, see [Can a Foreigner Own a Restaurant in Thailand?](/blog/foreigner-opening-restaurant-thailand)

## Matching Concepts to Cities

Not every concept works in every city. Here are the pairings I see working best in 2026:

**Bangkok:** Fine dining, concept-driven casual, cloud kitchens, Japanese/Korean/Western fusion, cocktail-forward restaurants, multi-brand delivery operations. See [restaurant concepts trending in Bangkok for 2026](/blog/restaurant-concepts-bangkok-2026).

**Pattaya:** International casual dining (Western, Russian, Indian), seafood restaurants, sports bars with quality food, family-friendly dining, business lunch concepts near EEC offices.

**Phuket:** Upscale seafood, Mediterranean-influenced restaurants, wine bars, boutique fine dining, health-conscious and organic concepts, rooftop/sunset dining.

**Chiang Mai:** Specialty cafes, farm-to-table, creative Thai cuisine, wine bars, bistros, vegetarian/vegan (strong demand from digital nomad community), cooking school-restaurant hybrids.

**Koh Samui:** Beachfront dining, luxury casual, wellness-focused menus, Thai fine dining for hotel overflow, brunch concepts.

## Next Steps

If you are seriously considering a location outside Bangkok, here is what I recommend:

1. **Visit during both high and low seasons.** The difference is dramatic and affects your financial projections.
2. **Run a realistic P&L model** using the rent and staffing costs for your target city, not Bangkok benchmarks.
3. **Talk to at least 5 existing restaurant operators** in the city. Ask about their experience with seasonality, staffing, supply chain, and local regulations.
4. **Understand the local customer** before you finalize your concept. What works in Bangkok does not automatically translate to Chiang Mai or Phuket.
5. **Factor in your own lifestyle.** You will be spending years in this city. Make sure you want to live there.
