Restaurant staffing in Thailand is the problem that keeps operators awake at night. Not rent. Not food cost. Not even landlord disputes. It is finding people, training them, and then watching them leave for a hotel job that pays 2,000 THB more per month.

I have managed restaurant teams across Bangkok, Chiang Mai, and Phuket for over a decade. The staffing landscape has changed substantially since COVID — wage expectations are up, experienced cooks are harder to find, and the hospitality sector is competing aggressively for the same labor pool. Hotels, airlines, and international chains are all pulling from the same talent base that independent restaurants depend on.

The average annual turnover rate for restaurant staff in Bangkok sits somewhere around 45-60%, depending on the segment. Fine dining tends to retain better (people like the prestige and tips), while casual dining and QSR operations churn through staff at alarming rates. Some fast-food chains budget for 80%+ annual turnover and build their training systems around it. That is not a model most independent operators can replicate.

This guide covers everything I wish someone had told me when I was hiring my first kitchen brigade: what to pay, what the law requires, where to find people, and how to keep them once you do.

<Callout type="info">
All salary figures in this article are in Thai Baht (THB) and reflect Bangkok market rates as of early 2026. Salaries outside Bangkok are typically 15-30% lower, with the exception of tourist-heavy areas like Phuket and Koh Samui where hospitality wages can match or exceed Bangkok levels. For reference, 1 USD is approximately 35.5 THB.
</Callout>

## Thai Labor Law Essentials

This is the section you cannot afford to skip. Thai labor law under the Labour Protection Act B.E. 2541 (1998, with amendments) is employee-friendly. Violations carry real penalties — fines, back-pay orders, and in some cases criminal liability. I have seen operators get burned badly by ignoring these requirements.

### Minimum Wage

Thailand's minimum wage is set by the National Wage Committee and varies by province. As of January 2026:

- **Bangkok and surrounding provinces (Nonthaburi, Pathum Thani, Samut Prakan, Samut Sakhon, Nakhon Pathom):** 363 THB/day
- **Phuket, Chon Buri:** 361 THB/day
- **Chiang Mai:** 352 THB/day
- **Other provinces:** 337-361 THB/day depending on location

<Callout type="info">
The minimum daily wage translates to roughly 10,500-10,900 THB/month in Bangkok (based on a standard 26 working days per month). In practice, almost every restaurant position except dishwasher and kitchen helper pays above minimum wage. But the minimum wage still matters because it sets the floor for overtime calculations and social security contributions.
</Callout>

### Working Hours and Overtime

Standard working hours under the Labour Protection Act are **8 hours per day, 48 hours per week**. Most restaurants operate on a 6-day work week with one rest day.

Overtime rules are strict:

- **Overtime on regular work days:** 1.5x the hourly rate
- **Work on rest days (regular time):** 1x the hourly rate (on top of base)
- **Overtime on rest days:** 3x the hourly rate
- **Work on public holidays:** 1x (for monthly-salaried) or 2x (for daily-wage) on top of base
- **Overtime on public holidays:** 3x the hourly rate

<Callout type="warning">
Maximum overtime allowed by law is 36 hours per week. This is routinely violated in the restaurant industry, and the Department of Labour Protection and Welfare (DLPW) does enforce it. If an employee files a complaint, you will be audited. Keep accurate time records and pay overtime properly. The penalties for non-compliance include fines up to 100,000 THB and imprisonment up to 6 months.
</Callout>

Restaurants are explicitly listed as an exception to certain rest-day rules under the Ministerial Regulation. Employees can agree to work on rest days, but you must compensate them at the rates above. "Agreeing" means an actual written agreement, not a verbal instruction.

You must register with the Social Security Office within 30 days of hiring your first employee. Contributions are due monthly by the 15th of the following month. Late payments incur a 2% monthly surcharge.

<Callout type="warning">
Failing to register for social security or failing to remit contributions is one of the most common compliance failures I see in small restaurants. The Social Security Office conducts random audits, and back-payment orders can cover the entire period of non-compliance plus penalties. Register from day one.
</Callout>

### Leave Entitlements

Thai labor law mandates:

- **Annual leave:** Minimum 6 days per year after 1 year of continuous service. Most restaurants offer 6-10 days. Unused leave can be carried forward or paid out, depending on company policy.
- **Sick leave:** Up to 30 days per year with pay. Employers can require a medical certificate for absences exceeding 3 consecutive days.
- **Personal business leave:** At least 3 days per year with pay (since the 2019 amendment to the Labour Protection Act).
- **Maternity leave:** 98 days total. The employer pays for 45 days; the Social Security Fund covers the remaining 53 days (at 50% of salary, capped).
- **Military service leave:** Up to 60 days per year with pay.
- **Public holidays:** At least 13 paid public holidays per year. The employer selects which national holidays to observe (there are approximately 19 official public holidays — you must designate at least 13). If a holiday falls on a rest day, the next working day is a substitute holiday.

### Severance Pay

This is the one that catches people off guard. Thai law requires mandatory severance pay upon termination without cause, based on length of service:

- **120 days to 1 year:** 30 days' wages
- **1 to 3 years:** 90 days' wages
- **3 to 6 years:** 180 days' wages
- **6 to 10 years:** 240 days' wages
- **10 to 20 years:** 300 days' wages
- **20+ years:** 400 days' wages

Severance is not required if the employee resigns voluntarily or is terminated for cause (serious misconduct as defined in Section 119 of the Labour Protection Act — dishonesty, intentional damage, criminal offense, gross negligence, etc.). But "the food was not good enough" or "we need to cut costs" is termination without cause, and severance applies.

Additionally, if you do not give 30 days' advance written notice of termination, you owe payment in lieu of notice (one month's wages) on top of severance.

### Probation Periods

Thai law does not specifically define probation, but it is standard practice and recognized by the courts. Typical probation is 120 days, aligned with the severance threshold — if you terminate during the first 119 days, no severance is owed. Many restaurants use a 90-day probation with a performance review.

Get this in writing. The employment contract should clearly state the probation period and performance expectations. Verbal agreements do not hold up at the Labour Court.

## Hiring Foreign Staff

Bringing in a foreign chef, a sommelier, or a restaurant manager requires navigating Thailand's work permit system.

### The 4:1 Rule

For each foreign work permit, you must employ at least 4 Thai nationals and have minimum registered capital of 2 million THB in the company. So if you want 3 foreign work permits, you need 12 Thai employees and 6 million THB in registered capital.

### Restricted Occupations

Certain jobs are legally restricted to Thai nationals under the Foreign Employment Act. Notably, **cooking Thai food** was historically on the restricted list. While enforcement is inconsistent, be aware that a foreign chef cooking exclusively Thai dishes can technically face legal challenges. Most operators structure the foreign chef's role around "international cuisine" or "menu development" to avoid the restriction.

### Work Permit Process

1. **Non-Immigrant B Visa** — The employee applies at a Thai embassy/consulate abroad (or converts from a tourist visa inside Thailand with proper documentation)
2. **Work Permit Application** — Filed at the Department of Employment. Requires company documents, employee qualifications, passport, medical certificate
3. **Processing Time:** 2-4 weeks for the work permit itself, but factor in 4-8 weeks total including visa processing
4. **Cost:** Work permit fees are modest (approximately 3,000-6,000 THB/year), but legal/agent fees add 25,000-50,000 THB per application

### True Cost of a Foreign Hire

Beyond salary, budget for:
- Work permit fees and legal costs: 30,000-50,000 THB/year
- Housing allowance (common for foreign chef packages): 15,000-40,000 THB/month
- Visa renewal and 90-day reporting administration
- Potential relocation costs
- The 4 Thai staff members you must employ as a ratio requirement

A foreign executive chef on a 120,000 THB/month salary actually costs you 180,000-200,000 THB/month when you factor in everything. Make sure the value justifies the cost.

## Where to Find Staff

Recruitment in Thailand's restaurant industry is still surprisingly informal. The platforms and channels that work here are different from what you might expect.

### JobThai and JobsDB

JobThai (jobthai.com) is the dominant job platform in Thailand. It is in Thai, which makes it ideal for reaching Thai candidates. JobsDB covers more bilingual and professional roles. Post on both. A standard job posting costs 1,500-5,000 THB depending on the package.

### Facebook Groups

This is where the real action is. Thai restaurant workers find jobs through Facebook groups more than any other channel. Key groups include dedicated food industry hiring groups, chef communities, and Bangkok service industry groups. Search for groups in Thai using terms like "หางาน ร้านอาหาร" (restaurant job search) or "เชฟหางาน" (chef job search). Many groups have 50,000-200,000 members.

### LINE Groups

LINE is Thailand's dominant messaging app, and industry-specific LINE groups function as informal job boards. Ask your existing staff — they are almost certainly in several. When I need a line cook quickly, asking my current team to share a LINE group post fills the position faster than any formal channel.

### Culinary School Partnerships

Thailand has several culinary schools producing graduates every semester:

- **Dusit Thani College** — Hotel and culinary management, well-regarded
- **Le Cordon Bleu Dusit** — French technique focus, higher-end graduates
- **Suan Dusit University** — Culinary arts program, strong placement network
- **Various vocational colleges (วิทยาลัยอาชีวศึกษา)** — Produce the bulk of entry-level kitchen talent

Contact the placement offices directly. Offer internship programs that convert to full-time positions. This pipeline takes 3-6 months to build but produces loyal staff who grew up in your system.

### Recruitment Agencies

For management and senior chef positions, agencies like Adecco Thailand, Robert Walters, and specialized hospitality recruiters (StaffHero, Hosco) can source candidates. Expect to pay 1-2 months' salary as a placement fee. Worth it for hard-to-fill senior roles, not cost-effective for line staff.

### Referral Programs

The single most effective recruitment channel: your own staff. Implement a formal referral bonus — I recommend 2,000-5,000 THB paid after the referred employee completes probation. Your best people know other good people. This costs a fraction of agency fees and produces higher-quality hires because your staff are putting their reputation on the line.

<Callout type="tip">
Pay the referral bonus in two installments — half when the new hire starts, half after they pass probation. This gives the referring employee an incentive to help the new person succeed and stay.
</Callout>

## The Service Charge Question

The 10% service charge is standard in Thailand's mid-range and upscale restaurants. How you handle it has a direct impact on your ability to attract and retain staff.

### How It Works

Most restaurants add a 10% service charge to the bill. This is separate from VAT (7%). The total bill reads: food + service charge (10%) + VAT (7% on the combined total). So a 1,000 THB food bill becomes 1,177 THB.

### Distribution Models

There is no single mandated approach, but common models include:

**Equal distribution:** The entire service charge pool is divided equally among all staff (kitchen and FOH). This is the simplest and most common model. It promotes teamwork across departments.

**Weighted distribution:** Staff receive shares based on position level. A manager might get 1.5 shares, a server gets 1 share, a dishwasher gets 0.7 shares. More complex but feels fairer to senior staff.

**Department split:** A fixed percentage goes to kitchen (e.g., 40%) and FOH (60%), then divided equally within each department. Recognizes that FOH directly drives the guest experience that generates the charge.

### Tax Implications

Service charge income is taxable as personal income for employees. The employer must withhold income tax on service charge distributions, just as with regular salary. Social security contributions also apply to service charge income if it is considered regular income. Many restaurants structure service charge payments as variable/discretionary to manage the SSF implications, but the Revenue Department's position is that regular service charge distributions are assessable income.

### What Staff Actually Care About

In my experience, the total monthly take-home matters more to staff than the base salary alone. A server earning 14,000 THB base + 4,000-6,000 THB in service charge distribution is effectively making 18,000-20,000 THB. This total number is what they compare to competing offers. When you are recruiting, always discuss compensation in terms of total expected monthly income.

## Staff Retention Strategies

Hiring is expensive. Every time someone leaves, you lose 2-4 weeks of productivity while you recruit and train a replacement. The real cost of turnover — including recruitment, training, reduced team performance, and potential guest experience impact — is estimated at 50-150% of the departing employee's annual salary.

Here is what actually works in Thailand's restaurant industry.

### Career Progression Paths

Thai workers, especially younger ones, are not going to stay somewhere with no upward trajectory. Map out clear promotion paths and share them during onboarding:

- **Kitchen:** Dishwasher/Helper → Prep Cook → Line Cook → Chef de Partie → Sous Chef → Head Chef
- **FOH:** Busser → Server → Captain → Supervisor → Assistant Manager → Restaurant Manager

Attach salary bands to each level. When a line cook knows that becoming a chef de partie means a 5,000 THB/month raise and that the criteria are clear (pass a skills test, complete 6 months at current level, receive a positive review), they have a reason to stay and grow.

### Training Investment

Send your sous chef to a weekend workshop. Pay for your bartender's certification course. Sponsor your manager's food safety training. These investments cost 5,000-20,000 THB per person per year and have an outsized effect on retention. People stay where they are learning and growing.

Conduct formal skills training in-house at least monthly. Cross-train FOH staff on basic kitchen knowledge and vice versa. This builds respect between departments and gives you operational flexibility.

<Callout type="tip">
Create a simple training log for each employee. Track what they have learned, certifications earned, and skills demonstrated. Review it during quarterly check-ins. This makes career progression tangible rather than abstract, and it gives you documentation if you ever need to justify a promotion — or a termination.
</Callout>

### Staff Meals

This seems minor, but it is not. A good staff meal — hot, freshly prepared, served before the shift — costs you 30-50 THB per person per day. For a team of 15, that is 13,500-22,500 THB per month. Compare that to the cost of replacing one person who left because they felt undervalued.

Never serve leftover scraps or yesterday's prep waste. Designate a cook to prepare staff meal with the same care as guest food. I have seen kitchens where staff meal is the highlight of the day and a genuine team-building moment. I have also seen kitchens where staff meal is microwaved rice and sadness. The first type of kitchen retains people. The second does not.

### Accommodation Support

Many restaurant workers in Bangkok come from northeastern Thailand (Isan), the north, or the south. They are living in shared apartments, often spending 4,000-6,000 THB/month on rent. Some operators offer staff housing — either a shared apartment near the restaurant or a housing allowance of 2,000-3,000 THB/month. This is a meaningful differentiator, especially for provincial hires who are choosing between your restaurant and going home.

### Shift Scheduling

Publish the schedule at least 2 weeks in advance. Allow shift swaps with manager approval. Respect days off — canceling someone's rest day without notice is the fastest way to lose good people in Thailand.

Consider offering a split-shift structure for dinner-only staff: come in at 10 AM for prep, off from 2-5 PM, back for dinner service. This is standard in the industry but execute it fairly. Nobody wants a 14-hour day with a 3-hour unpaid gap in the middle. If you use split shifts, make sure the total hours and compensation are reasonable.

### Team Culture

Thai workplace culture is hierarchical but also deeply relational. Monthly staff outings (even a simple dinner together), birthday recognition, and celebrating milestones (1-year anniversary, promotions) cost almost nothing but build real loyalty.

One practice I have seen work well: a monthly "staff voice" meeting where employees can raise concerns anonymously through written notes. Thai culture discourages direct confrontation with management — the anonymous format gives people a way to surface problems before they become resignations.

<Callout type="tip">
Small, consistent gestures outperform grand occasional ones. A weekly fruit delivery for the staff break area (200 THB/week) does more for morale than a single annual party. Consistency signals that you care about your team as a default, not as an event.
</Callout>

## Common Staffing Mistakes

I have made some of these myself. Learn from my experience.

### Underpaying and Losing Good People

The math is simple. If you pay a sous chef 28,000 THB when the market rate is 35,000 THB, they will leave. The cost of recruiting and training a replacement exceeds the 7,000 THB/month difference within 2-3 months. Pay competitive rates from the start. Saving 3,000 THB/month on 10 positions is 30,000 THB/month — until three of them leave in the same week and your restaurant cannot operate.

### No Written Employment Contracts

Thai labor law does not strictly require a written contract, but operating without one is reckless. A written contract protects both parties and sets clear expectations on salary, probation, working hours, leave, and termination conditions. Without one, you are exposed to misunderstandings that end up at the Labour Court, where the burden of proof falls on the employer.

Use a bilingual (Thai/English) contract template reviewed by a Thai labor lawyer. Cost: 5,000-10,000 THB for a template you can reuse.

### Ignoring Social Security Registration

I mentioned this above, but it deserves repeating. Some small restaurants operate informally, paying cash and never registering with the Social Security Office. This works until an employee has a workplace injury, or files a complaint, or you get audited. The back-payment and penalties can be devastating for a small operation.

### Over-Relying on Family Hires

I see this constantly with family-run restaurants. The owner's nephew is the "manager." Their cousin is "handling" the kitchen. Family loyalty is valuable, but unqualified family members in key positions will hold your business back. If a family member fills a role, they should meet the same performance standards as any other employee. If they do not, hire a professional and give the family member a role that matches their skills.

### Micromanaging Thai Kitchen Culture

Thai kitchens have their own rhythm and hierarchy. A head chef who has earned the respect of the kitchen team has real authority, and that authority should be respected. I have watched foreign operators insert themselves into kitchen operations, overriding the head chef's decisions on prep lists, station assignments, and plating — and then wonder why the entire kitchen team resigned within a month.

Set expectations. Define standards. Then let your kitchen leadership execute. Check results, not process.

### Not Budgeting for Service Charge Fluctuation

Your staff's service charge income varies with revenue. In a slow month, your servers might take home 3,000 THB less than usual. Some operators top up service charge during slow periods to maintain staff income stability. This costs you 15,000-30,000 THB/month in a bad month, but it prevents an exodus of your best people during low season. Budget for it.

## Putting It All Together

Restaurant staffing in Thailand is not inherently harder than anywhere else, but it has its own rules. The labor law is specific, the recruitment channels are different, and the retention strategies that work here reflect Thai workplace culture — not Western management textbooks.

The operators who succeed at staffing in Thailand share a few traits. They pay competitive wages without being asked. They invest in training before they need to. They understand that Thai workers value respect and stability, and they build systems around those values instead of fighting them. They document everything — contracts, performance reviews, training records — not because they are bureaucratic, but because good documentation prevents the disputes and misunderstandings that destroy teams.

Get the staffing right and everything else becomes easier. Your kitchen puts out consistent food. Your service team creates regulars. Your costs stay predictable. Get it wrong and no amount of great recipes or Instagram marketing will save you.

Start with the fundamentals: pay fairly, comply with the law, hire through the right channels, and give people a reason to stay.
