I once sat in a meeting with a restaurant owner who had been operating in Bangkok for two years and could not tell me his food cost percentage. Not roughly. Not a ballpark. He had no idea. His accountant filed taxes every month, but nobody -- not the accountant, not the owner -- was using the financial data to actually manage the business.

That restaurant closed eight months later. Not because the food was bad. The food was excellent. It closed because money was leaking out of every seam, and nobody was watching.

Accounting is not glamorous. It does not get Instagram posts or media coverage. But every restaurant that survives past year two in Thailand has one thing in common: somebody is paying close attention to the numbers. If that somebody is not you, it needs to be someone you trust and who understands the Thai system.

This article covers what you need to know about restaurant accounting in Thailand -- the standards, the tax obligations, the filing deadlines, and the practical decisions you will face about software, staff, and outsourcing. I have structured it for restaurant owners, not accountants, so I have skipped the theory where I can and focused on what actually affects your cash and your compliance risk.

## Why Restaurant Accounting in Thailand Is Different

If you have run a restaurant in the US, Europe, or Australia, you probably think you understand accounting. You understand a version of it. Thai accounting has its own standards, its own tax structure, and its own quirks that catch foreign operators off guard.

**Thai Financial Reporting Standards (TFRS):** Thailand has adopted standards based on International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), but with simplifications for small and medium enterprises. Most restaurants qualify as SMEs under the TFRS for NPAEs (Non-Publicly Accountable Entities), which means simpler reporting requirements. You do not need to produce the full IFRS-compliant financial statements that a listed company would. But you still need audited financials if your company is registered as a Thai limited company -- and since most foreign-owned restaurants operate through a Thai limited company, this applies to nearly everyone reading this article.

**Dual-language bookkeeping:** Your books must be maintained in Thai. This is a legal requirement under the Accounting Act B.E. 2543. You can keep parallel English records for your own use, but the official books, the ones the Revenue Department audits, must be in Thai. This alone makes hiring a Thai-speaking accountant non-optional.

**Calendar year fiscal year:** Thai companies use a calendar year (January-December) as their fiscal year by default. You can apply to use a different fiscal year, but most restaurants stick with the calendar year because the Revenue Department expects it and deviation invites scrutiny.

**Cash-basis vs. accrual-basis:** Thai tax law requires accrual-basis accounting for corporate income tax purposes. You recognise revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash changes hands. This matters for restaurants because of timing differences between when you receive cash (at the register) and when you pay suppliers (on 30-day credit terms). Your monthly P&L will not always match your bank balance, and understanding why is part of running the business.

## Setting Up Your Chart of Accounts

Your chart of accounts is the backbone of your financial reporting. A restaurant's chart of accounts differs from a generic business because food and beverage operations have specific cost categories that need their own tracking.

Here is a simplified chart of accounts structure I recommend for Thai restaurants:

**Revenue Accounts:**
- Food revenue (dine-in)
- Beverage revenue (dine-in)
- Delivery revenue (food)
- Delivery revenue (beverages)
- Service charge income
- Other income (event hosting, catering, merchandise)

**Cost of Goods Sold (COGS):**
- Food purchases
- Beverage purchases
- Packaging and consumables (delivery)
- Inventory adjustments (waste, spoilage, staff meals)

**Labour Costs:**
- Salaries and wages (kitchen)
- Salaries and wages (FOH)
- Salaries and wages (admin/management)
- Social security contributions (employer share)
- Workmen's compensation fund
- Service charge distributions
- Overtime
- Staff meals expense
- Training expense
- Recruitment costs

**Occupancy Costs:**
- Rent
- Common area maintenance (CAM) fees
- Utilities -- electricity
- Utilities -- water
- Property insurance

**Operating Expenses:**
- Marketing and advertising
- POS and technology subscriptions
- Repairs and maintenance
- Pest control
- Cleaning supplies
- Legal and professional fees
- Accounting fees
- Bank charges and payment processing fees
- Delivery platform commissions
- Depreciation -- equipment
- Depreciation -- renovation and fit-out
- Licenses and permits

<Callout type="tip">
Separate your delivery revenue and delivery-related costs (platform commissions, packaging) from dine-in revenue. Delivery margins are 15-25 percentage points lower than dine-in margins due to platform commissions. If you lump them together, your blended food cost percentage will mask the fact that delivery is dragging down your overall profitability. I have seen restaurants that looked healthy at 32% blended food cost but were actually running 28% dine-in and 48% delivery. That kind of visibility changes decisions.
</Callout>

The critical distinction in a restaurant chart of accounts is separating food COGS from beverage COGS. Beverages typically run 18-25% cost, while food runs 28-35%. Blending them hides problems. If your combined COGS is 30% but food is actually at 38% and beverages at 20%, you have a food cost problem that the blended number obscures.

## VAT Registration and Compliance

Value Added Tax (VAT) in Thailand is 7% (technically 10% with a temporary reduction to 7% that has been renewed continuously since 1999 -- treat it as 7% for planning purposes).

### When You Must Register

**Mandatory VAT registration** applies when your annual revenue exceeds 1.8 million THB. For a restaurant, this threshold is low -- 1.8 million THB per year is just 150,000 THB per month, or roughly 5,000 THB per day. Even a small cafe will likely cross this threshold within the first few months of operation.

Once you cross (or expect to cross) the threshold, you must register for VAT within 30 days. In practice, most restaurants should register for VAT before opening because the threshold is so low. Failing to register on time triggers penalties: a surcharge of 1.5% per month on unpaid VAT plus a potential fine of up to 2x the VAT owed.

### Voluntary Registration

You can register for VAT voluntarily even if your revenue is below 1.8 million THB. Why would you? Because VAT-registered businesses can claim input tax credits. If you are spending 500,000 THB/month on supplies, equipment, and services that include 7% VAT, that is 35,000 THB/month in input VAT you can offset against the output VAT you collect from customers. For a new restaurant making capital expenditures during the pre-opening phase, voluntary registration lets you reclaim VAT on renovation, equipment, and other startup costs.

**The service charge complication:** The 10% service charge that most mid-range and upscale restaurants add to the bill is subject to VAT. The calculation is: food/beverage price + service charge (10%) = subtotal, and VAT (7%) is applied to the subtotal. So a 1,000 THB bill becomes 1,000 + 100 (service charge) + 77 (VAT on 1,100) = 1,177 THB. Many POS systems handle this automatically, but verify that yours is calculating correctly. I have audited restaurants where the POS was applying VAT only to the food amount, not the service charge, which meant they were under-collecting and under-reporting VAT.

<Callout type="warning">
Keep every VAT tax invoice you receive from suppliers. The Revenue Department requires original tax invoices (not photocopies) as supporting documentation for input tax credits. If you cannot produce the invoice during an audit, the credit is disallowed and you owe the difference plus a 1.5% monthly surcharge. Implement a filing system from day one -- a simple folder-per-month approach works fine for most restaurants.
</Callout>

### VAT Filing

VAT returns (Form PP.30) are due by the 15th of the following month. If your January output VAT is 70,000 THB and your input VAT is 25,000 THB, you owe 45,000 THB by February 15th. Late filing triggers a surcharge of 1.5% per month on the outstanding amount.

Most accounting firms include VAT filing in their monthly service. If you are handling it yourself -- which I do not recommend -- the Revenue Department's e-filing system (rd.go.th) works reasonably well.

## Withholding Tax

Thailand's withholding tax system catches many foreign restaurant owners off guard because it works differently from most Western countries. When you pay certain types of income to vendors or service providers, you are required to withhold tax at source and remit it to the Revenue Department on their behalf.

**Common withholding tax rates for restaurants:**

- **Rent payments:** 5% (withhold from each rent payment to your landlord)
- **Service fees:** 3% (accounting, legal, consulting, cleaning, pest control)
- **Advertising:** 2% (media, social media agencies, printing)
- **Transportation:** 1% (delivery services, logistics)
- **Prize and lucky draw payments:** 5% (if you run customer promotions)

Here is how it works in practice: if your monthly rent is 100,000 THB, you pay the landlord 95,000 THB and remit 5,000 THB to the Revenue Department using Form PND.53 (for corporate payees) or PND.3 (for individual payees). You must issue a withholding tax certificate to the payee.

**Filing deadline:** Withholding tax returns are due by the 7th of the following month (or the 15th if filed electronically). For a restaurant with rent, accounting fees, pest control, and various service contracts, this means preparing and filing withholding tax forms every month for each payee.

<Callout type="info">
Many landlords in Thailand understand the withholding tax system and expect tenants to withhold. Some will even remind you. But smaller vendors -- a freelance designer, a local cleaning company -- may not understand why you are paying them 97% of the invoiced amount. Explain the system proactively and provide the withholding tax certificate promptly. It avoids disputes and builds trust.
</Callout>

## Corporate Income Tax

Thai corporate income tax (CIT) is calculated on net profit at a rate of 20%. However, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) that meet certain criteria enjoy reduced rates:

- **Net profit up to 300,000 THB:** Exempt (0%)
- **Net profit 300,001 to 3,000,000 THB:** 15%
- **Net profit above 3,000,000 THB:** 20%

To qualify for SME rates, your company must have paid-up capital not exceeding 5 million THB and annual revenue not exceeding 30 million THB. Most standalone restaurants easily qualify. Multi-outlet operations may not.

**Filing deadlines:**
- **Mid-year return (PND.51):** Due within 2 months of the end of the first 6 months of the fiscal year (typically by August 31st for calendar-year companies). This is an estimated filing based on projected annual profit.
- **Annual return (PND.50):** Due within 150 days of the fiscal year end (typically by May 31st for calendar-year companies). This is the final return based on audited financial statements.

If your mid-year estimate is off by more than 25% from the actual annual figure, you face a 20% surcharge on the underpaid amount. Your accountant should help you make a reasonable mid-year estimate based on the first six months of actual performance.

### BOI vs. Non-BOI Accounting

The Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) offers promotional privileges for certain food industry investments, including corporate income tax exemptions (3-8 years depending on the category), import duty exemptions on machinery, and permission for foreign majority ownership.

BOI promotion is potentially relevant for restaurant operators who are:
- Investing above 1 million THB in qualifying food industry activities
- Setting up a food processing or production facility (not just a restaurant)
- Developing a restaurant chain with significant capital investment

In practice, standalone restaurants rarely qualify for BOI promotion unless they include a food production component (central kitchen, food manufacturing). But if you are building a multi-outlet operation with a central kitchen, it is worth investigating. BOI-promoted companies have additional accounting requirements -- they must maintain separate books for promoted and non-promoted activities, which adds complexity and cost to your accounting.

For most single-location restaurants, standard non-BOI accounting is the right path. Save the BOI analysis for when you scale.

## Social Security Contributions

As an employer, you are responsible for social security contributions for every employee. I covered this in detail in the [staffing guide](/blog/restaurant-staffing-thailand), but the accounting implications are worth repeating here.

**Employer contribution:** 5% of each employee's salary, capped at 750 THB/month per employee (based on a maximum assessable salary of 15,000 THB/month).

**Employee contribution:** 5%, also capped at 750 THB/month. You deduct this from the employee's salary.

**Filing deadline:** Social security contributions are due by the 15th of the following month, filed using Form SPS 1-10. For a restaurant with 15 employees, each at or above the 15,000 THB cap, you are remitting 11,250 THB/month for the employer share plus 11,250 THB withheld from employees -- 22,500 THB total.

**The Workmen's Compensation Fund** is a separate employer-only contribution, typically 0.2-0.5% of total payroll for the restaurant industry, filed annually.

Late social security payments incur a 2% monthly surcharge. Failure to register or remit is one of the most common compliance failures I see in small restaurants, and the back-payment orders during audits can cover the entire period of non-compliance. Register from day one.

## Monthly and Annual Filing Calendar

Keeping track of every filing deadline is one of the most practical things you can do to avoid penalties. Here is the full calendar for a Thai restaurant:

**Monthly filings:**
- **By the 7th (or 15th for e-filing):** Withholding tax returns (PND.3, PND.53)
- **By the 15th:** VAT return (PP.30)
- **By the 15th:** Social security contributions (SPS 1-10)

**Semi-annual filings:**
- **By August 31st:** Mid-year corporate income tax estimate (PND.51)

**Annual filings:**
- **By February 15th:** Annual withholding tax summary for employees (PND.1 Kor)
- **By May 31st:** Annual corporate income tax return with audited financial statements (PND.50)
- **Within 5 months of fiscal year end:** Audited financial statements filed with the Department of Business Development (DBD)

**Other periodic filings:**
- **Workmen's Compensation Fund:** Annual, due within 30 days of the fiscal year end
- **Annual General Meeting (AGM):** Must be held within 4 months of the fiscal year end, with financial statements approved by shareholders

<Callout type="warning">
The May 31st deadline for annual corporate income tax is non-negotiable and comes with audited financial statements. This means your accountant needs your books closed, reconciled, and ready for the auditor by late March at the latest. If you hand your accountant a box of receipts in May, you will miss the deadline and pay penalties. Start preparing for the annual close in January.
</Callout>

## Choosing an Accounting Firm

Most restaurants in Thailand outsource their accounting. The question is not whether to outsource -- it is which firm to choose and what to pay.

### What an Accounting Firm Does for You

A standard monthly accounting service for a restaurant includes:
- Recording all transactions (sales, purchases, expenses) from source documents
- Preparing monthly financial statements (P&L, balance sheet)
- Filing VAT returns (PP.30)
- Filing withholding tax returns (PND.3, PND.53)
- Filing social security contributions
- Payroll processing and personal income tax withholding for employees
- Maintaining the general ledger in Thai

The annual service adds:
- Preparing financial statements for audit
- Coordinating with the auditor
- Filing corporate income tax returns (PND.50, PND.51)
- Filing annual returns with the Department of Business Development

At the low end, 8,000 THB/month gets you basic compliance -- your taxes get filed on time and you stay legal. At the higher end, 18,000-25,000 THB/month should include management reporting that you can actually use to make decisions: monthly P&L with comparison to budget, food cost tracking, labour cost analysis, and cash flow projections.

### What to Look for in an Accounting Firm

**Restaurant experience.** This matters more than you might think. A firm that handles retail shops and trading companies will not understand inventory valuation for perishable goods, service charge accounting, or delivery platform commission reconciliation. Ask specifically: "How many restaurants do you currently serve?"

**English communication.** If you cannot read Thai financial statements, you need a firm that provides bilingual reporting and can explain your numbers in English. This narrows the field considerably but is non-negotiable for foreign operators.

**POS integration.** The best firms can pull transaction data directly from your POS system (FoodStory, Ocha, StoreHub, or others), which reduces manual entry errors and speeds up monthly close. Ask whether they have experience with your POS platform.

**Responsiveness.** Your accountant should be reachable by LINE (the dominant business communication tool in Thailand) and should respond to questions within 24 hours during business days. If you only hear from your accountant when tax is due, find a new one.

**Audit firm separation.** Under Thai law, your bookkeeping firm and your auditor must be separate entities. Some firms will offer both through related companies, which is common and acceptable, but they should be formally distinct.

I tell every client the same thing: your accounting firm is the second most important professional relationship in your restaurant, right after your landlord. Do not choose based on price alone. The difference between an 8,000 THB/month firm that just files forms and a 15,000 THB/month firm that provides actionable financial intelligence is 84,000 THB per year. That 84,000 THB pays for itself many times over in better financial decisions.

## POS Integration with Accounting

Your POS system is your primary data source. Every transaction -- every dine-in bill, every Grab order, every voided check -- flows through the POS. Getting that data into your accounting system accurately and efficiently is one of the biggest operational improvements you can make.

### The Manual Problem

Without POS integration, your accountant receives daily sales summaries (often handwritten or in a spreadsheet), supplier invoices, bank statements, and cash reports. They manually enter everything into their accounting software. This process introduces errors, creates delays (you might not see February's P&L until late March), and makes it impossible to spot problems in real time.

### Integration Options

**Direct API integration.** Some POS platforms (FoodStory, StoreHub) offer direct integration with accounting software like PEAK, FlowAccount, or even QuickBooks. Daily sales data, payment breakdowns, and void reports flow automatically. This is the gold standard.

**Daily export and import.** If direct integration is not available, most POS systems can export daily sales summaries in CSV or Excel format. Your accountant imports these into their system. Not as seamless, but much better than manual entry.

**Delivery platform reconciliation.** This is the pain point I hear about most. Grab, LINE MAN, and Robinhood each deposit money on different schedules (weekly or bi-weekly), net of commissions, promotions, and adjustments. Reconciling these deposits against your POS delivery orders requires matching individual transactions across systems. Some POS platforms now offer built-in delivery reconciliation tools. If yours does not, budget extra time for your accountant to handle this manually.

<Callout type="tip">
Run a daily "Z report" from your POS at closing and reconcile it against actual cash and card payments. This takes 10 minutes per day and catches discrepancies immediately. Waiting until the end of the month to discover a 15,000 THB cash shortfall means you have lost the ability to investigate what happened. The daily habit is tedious but pays for itself in loss prevention.
</Callout>

The POS investment of 1,000-4,000 THB per month is trivial compared to the cost of inaccurate financial data. If you are still using a cash register and a notebook, upgrading to a proper POS is one of the highest-ROI decisions you can make. ## Revenue Recognition for Prepaid Vouchers and Deposits

This is an area where restaurant accounting differs from most other businesses, and where I see mistakes constantly.

### Gift Vouchers and Prepaid Cards

When a customer buys a 5,000 THB gift voucher, you have received cash but you have not earned revenue yet. Under accrual accounting, you record a liability (unearned revenue) of 5,000 THB. When the voucher is redeemed, you recognise the revenue and reduce the liability.

**The VAT timing question:** VAT is technically triggered at the point of sale (when you issue the voucher). But the practical treatment depends on how the voucher is structured. If it is a specific product voucher (e.g., "Tasting Menu for 2"), VAT is assessed on the stated value at the time of sale. If it is a monetary voucher (e.g., "5,000 THB store credit"), VAT is assessed when it is redeemed and applied to actual purchases. Discuss the structure with your accountant before launching any voucher programme.

**Expiration and breakage:** If vouchers expire unredeemed, the unearned revenue eventually becomes income (called "breakage"). Thai tax law requires you to recognise this income, and VAT applies. Set clear expiration terms and track outstanding vouchers in a liability ledger.

### Event Deposits and Advance Payments

Restaurants that host private events often collect deposits of 30-50% upfront. The same principle applies: the deposit is a liability until the event occurs. Revenue is recognised on the event date, not the deposit date.

For the [P&L analysis](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand) to be meaningful, these timing differences need to be handled correctly. A month with heavy deposit collection looks artificially profitable if you recognise the deposits as revenue immediately. Then the month when you actually deliver the events (and incur the costs) looks unprofitable. Accrual accounting fixes this, but only if your accountant is applying it correctly.

## Inventory Accounting Methods

Restaurants deal with perishable inventory, which creates specific accounting challenges. The method you choose for valuing inventory affects your reported food cost and, by extension, your reported profit.

### Weighted Average Method (Most Common)

This is the standard method for Thai restaurants and the one I recommend for most operations. You calculate the average cost of each inventory item based on all purchases during the period, weighted by quantity.

**Example:** You buy 100 kg of chicken breast at 130 THB/kg on January 5th, and 80 kg at 145 THB/kg on January 15th. Your weighted average cost is: (100 x 130 + 80 x 145) / 180 = 136.67 THB/kg. All chicken used during the period is costed at 136.67 THB/kg.

This method smooths out price fluctuations -- which are common in Thailand, where fresh ingredient prices can swing 10-20% week to week -- and is simpler to implement than alternatives.

### FIFO (First In, First Out)

Under FIFO, you assume the oldest inventory is used first. This is theoretically more accurate for perishable goods (you should be using the oldest stock first), but it is more complex to track and produces food cost figures that fluctuate more with market prices.

### Periodic vs. Perpetual Inventory

**Periodic inventory** means you count your physical inventory at set intervals (weekly, bi-weekly, or monthly) and calculate COGS based on: Beginning Inventory + Purchases - Ending Inventory = COGS. This is the most common approach for restaurants because tracking perpetual inventory for hundreds of perishable items in real time is impractical without serious technology investment.

**Perpetual inventory** means tracking every item in and out of stock continuously. POS systems with inventory modules attempt this, but the reality in a restaurant kitchen -- where cooks grab ingredients by sight, prep generates waste, and staff meals consume stock -- makes perpetual inventory aspirational for most operations.

My recommendation: use periodic inventory with weekly counts on your top 20 cost items (proteins, seafood, dairy, alcohol). These items represent 60-70% of your total food and beverage cost. Monthly counts on everything else. This gives you 80% of the insight for 20% of the effort.

## Common Accounting Mistakes Foreigners Make

After 15 years of consulting with foreign-owned restaurants in Thailand, I have compiled a list of the most expensive and most common accounting mistakes. Avoiding these alone will save you significant money and stress.

### Mistake 1: Treating Thailand Like Your Home Country

Thai tax law is not a variation of US, UK, or Australian tax law. The withholding tax system, the VAT mechanics, the social security structure, and the corporate income tax calculation all have Thailand-specific rules. Foreign operators who assume their home-country knowledge transfers directly end up with non-compliant books and surprise tax bills.

Specific examples of things that work differently:
- **Depreciation rates** are set by the Revenue Department, not chosen by the company. Kitchen equipment: 5 years. Building renovation: 5-20 years depending on the type of improvement. You cannot accelerate depreciation without Revenue Department approval.
- **Entertainment expenses** are deductible but capped at 0.3% of gross revenue or paid-up capital (whichever is higher), with a maximum of 10 million THB. If you host supplier dinners, client events, or team celebrations, track them separately.
- **Donations** to approved charities are deductible up to 2% of net profit. Donations to non-approved entities are not deductible at all.

### Mistake 2: Mixing Personal and Business Finances

This is endemic. The owner uses the company bank account for personal expenses, pays for groceries with the company card, lends money to the company without documentation, or takes cash from the register. Every one of these creates accounting problems.

The company is a separate legal entity. Money moving between you and the company is either salary (subject to personal income tax and social security), a dividend (subject to 10% withholding tax), or a loan (which needs documentation and may require interest at the Revenue Department's deemed rate). Treat the company's money as the company's money and open a separate personal account for everything else.

### Mistake 3: Not Understanding the Audit Requirement

Every Thai limited company must have its annual financial statements audited by a certified public accountant (CPA) registered with the Federation of Accounting Professions (FAP). There is no exemption for small companies. The auditor must be independent -- they cannot be the same person or firm doing your bookkeeping.

The audit costs 20,000-50,000 THB for a typical restaurant. It is not optional. Companies that fail to file audited financials with the Department of Business Development (DBD) face fines and potential director liability.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring Delivery Platform Accounting

Delivery platforms are not simple sales channels. The accounting is genuinely complex:

- Grab pays net of commission (30-35%), promotions (variable), and adjustments
- Payment cycles are weekly or bi-weekly, not daily
- The commission is an expense, not a reduction in revenue -- you should record gross revenue and commission as a separate expense
- Platform promotions where the restaurant contributes (e.g., 50% off, restaurant-funded) reduce your net revenue and need separate tracking
- Refunds for cancelled or disputed orders appear as deductions in later payment cycles

I have seen restaurants where delivery revenue through Grab and LINE MAN was 40% of total revenue, but the owner had no idea what their actual net margin on delivery was because the accounting lumped everything together. Break it out. The answers are often uncomfortable, but you need them to make informed decisions about which platforms to use and what menu items to offer for delivery.

For more on this topic, including how delivery economics affect your overall cost structure, see [How Much Does It Cost to Open a Restaurant in Bangkok?](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok)

### Mistake 5: Failing to Accrue for Severance

Thai labour law requires [severance pay](/blog/restaurant-staffing-thailand) upon termination without cause: 30 days' wages after 120 days of service, scaling up to 400 days' wages after 20 years. This is a real liability that grows every month your employees stay.

Proper accounting requires accruing for this liability -- setting aside a provision each month based on your current headcount and their tenure. Most small restaurants do not do this, which means their financial statements understate their true liabilities. When someone is terminated and you suddenly owe 90 days' wages (3 months of salary for a 1-3 year employee), it hits your cash flow like a truck.

The accrual does not mean you need to put cash in a separate account. It means your financial statements should reflect the obligation so you -- and your bank, if you have a loan -- can see the true picture.

### Mistake 6: No Separate Tracking for Tips

Tips in Thailand have specific tax implications. Cash tips left by customers are generally considered the personal income of the employee who receives them and are not reportable by the restaurant. However, if the restaurant collects tips and distributes them (through a tip pool or service charge system), the amounts become employee income subject to personal income tax withholding.

Many restaurants in Thailand handle tips informally, which is fine for cash tips left at the table. But if you are pooling tips through your POS system, those amounts need to be tracked and reported properly.

## Accounting Software for Thai Restaurants

If you or your in-house accountant are handling some of the bookkeeping, having the right software matters.

**PEAK (peakaccount.com):** Thai cloud accounting software, Revenue Department certified, strong VAT and withholding tax automation, used by many Thai accounting firms. Plans start at 500 THB/month. This is the platform I see most often in practice.

**FlowAccount:** Another popular Thai cloud accounting option, with a more user-friendly interface and good mobile app. Plans from 750 THB/month.

**Express:** Desktop accounting software widely used by Thai accounting firms. It is the industry standard for firms that serve small businesses, though it is less modern than cloud alternatives.

**QuickBooks:** Some foreign operators default to QuickBooks because they know it from home. It works, but Thai VAT invoicing, withholding tax, and social security are not natively supported. You will need customisation, which adds cost and complexity. I generally recommend using a Thai platform unless you have a specific reason to use QuickBooks.

## What Good Financial Data Looks Like

Having the right accounting setup is not the end -- it is the beginning. The point of all this work is to produce financial data that helps you manage the business. Here is what you should be receiving from your accountant or generating from your POS every month:

**Weekly (from POS):**
- Daily revenue by category (food/beverage/delivery)
- Average check per cover
- Customer counts by day and daypart
- Food cost estimate (based on purchases vs. revenue)

**Monthly (from accountant):**
- Full P&L with comparison to prior month and budget
- Food cost percentage and beverage cost percentage (separate)
- Labour cost percentage (including all employer costs)
- Prime cost (food + labour as a percentage of revenue)
- Cash flow statement
- Accounts payable aging (what you owe suppliers)
- VAT summary

**Quarterly:**
- Balance sheet review
- Inventory shrinkage analysis
- Break-even analysis update
- Working capital position

**Annually:**
- Audited financial statements
- Tax planning review
- Budget for the coming year

If you are only getting an annual tax filing from your accountant, you are paying for compliance but not for the intelligence that keeps your restaurant alive. A monthly P&L that arrives 45 days late is useless for operational decisions. Push your accountant to deliver monthly statements within 15 working days of the month end.

For a deeper understanding of how to read and use these statements, see my guide on [understanding restaurant financial statements](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand).

## When to Bring Accounting In-House

Most restaurants start with an outsourced accounting firm and some grow to the point where an in-house accountant makes sense. Here is the decision framework:

**Stay outsourced if:**
- You have a single location
- Revenue is under 2 million THB/month
- Your transaction volume is under 50 per day
- You do not have the management bandwidth to supervise an accountant

**Consider in-house if:**
- You have multiple locations
- Revenue exceeds 3 million THB/month
- You need real-time financial reporting
- You want tighter integration between POS, inventory, and accounting
- You are spending more than 20,000 THB/month on outsourced accounting

An in-house accountant/bookkeeper costs 18,000-30,000 THB/month in salary plus social security. You will still need an external auditor (mandatory) and probably want the outsourced firm to handle tax filings for the first year while your in-house person gets up to speed. The transition period typically takes 3-6 months.

## Getting Started

If you are opening a restaurant in Thailand, here is the accounting setup sequence I recommend:

1. **Before company registration:** Choose an accounting firm. Get their input on your chart of accounts and accounting software.
2. **At company registration:** Register for VAT (voluntary if pre-revenue). Set up your accounting software.
3. **When hiring starts:** Register for social security. Set up payroll.
4. **Pre-opening:** Begin recording all expenses (renovation, equipment, supplies). Claim input VAT on startup costs.
5. **At opening:** Ensure POS is properly configured with tax-correct calculations. Establish daily cash reconciliation procedures.
6. **Month 1:** Produce your first monthly P&L. Compare actual performance against your [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) projections.
7. **Ongoing:** Monthly financial review with your accountant. Weekly KPI tracking from your POS.

The cost of getting accounting right from the start is modest -- 8,000-25,000 THB/month for bookkeeping, 20,000-50,000 THB/year for audit. The cost of getting it wrong is not modest at all: tax penalties, audit back-payments, lost visibility into your own business, and decisions made on bad data.

If your restaurant's financial health is a black box to you right now, that is the first problem to solve. Not the menu, not the marketing, not the decor. The numbers. Everything else depends on them.
