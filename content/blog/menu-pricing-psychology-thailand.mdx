Pricing is not math. If it were math, every restaurant in Bangkok would charge food cost plus a fixed margin and be done with it. But customers do not evaluate prices with a calculator -- they evaluate them with feelings, comparisons, and mental shortcuts they are not even aware of.

This is pricing psychology, and it is the most underused tool in Thai restaurant operations. I have spent 15 years redesigning menus in Thailand, and I can say with confidence that how you present your prices matters as much as what you charge. A 20 THB price increase that customers never notice. A "premium" option that nobody orders but makes everything else look like a bargain. A set meal that costs you less to produce than the individual items but feels like more value. These are not tricks -- they are well-documented cognitive patterns that every serious restaurant operator should understand.

This article covers the pricing psychology principles that work in Thailand specifically, because Thai diners and expat diners process prices differently, the Thai baht denomination creates specific quirks, and the competitive dynamics of Bangkok's 100,000+ restaurant market reward operators who get this right.

## Anchoring: The Most Powerful Pricing Tool on Your Menu

Anchoring is the cognitive bias where the first number a person sees influences their judgment of every number that follows. It was first described by Kahneman and Tversky in the 1970s, and it is one of the most robust findings in behavioural economics -- replicated hundreds of times, across cultures, in contexts ranging from real estate to wine purchases to restaurant menus.

Here is how it works on a Thai restaurant menu.

A customer opens your menu and the first price they see is 890 THB for a whole grilled river fish. They might not order it, but that number is now their reference point. When they see your green curry at 280 THB and your pad kra pao at 190 THB, both feel reasonable -- even cheap -- by comparison. Without that 890 THB anchor, the same 280 THB curry might have felt expensive.

I tested this with a client in Ari in 2024. Their original menu listed items from cheapest to most expensive within each section, with prices ranging from 120 THB to 320 THB. We restructured the menu to lead each section with the highest-priced item and added a premium seafood section at the top of the menu with prices from 490 THB to 890 THB. The average check increased by 14% in the first month, without adding a single new dish.

That 14% on their 1.2 million THB/month revenue was an extra 168,000 THB -- every month, ongoing -- from a menu reprint that cost 8,000 THB.

### How to Use Anchoring on a Thai Menu

**Lead with a premium item in each section.** If your curry section ranges from 220 THB to 380 THB, the 380 THB curry should appear first. Not buried at the bottom. First.

**Add at least one "aspirational" item.** This is an item priced 50-80% above your average price point. A whole grilled fish at 890 THB, a premium wagyu stir-fry at 690 THB, a lobster tom yum at 780 THB. This item does not need to sell well -- its job is to reset the price expectation for everything below it. If it does sell, the margins are typically excellent.

**Never list prices low-to-high.** This is the default in most Thai restaurants, and it actively works against you. When the cheapest item is at the top, customers anchor on the low price and everything below feels like an upsell. Reverse the order, or better yet, arrange items strategically rather than by price at all.

**Use a separate "specials" or "chef's table" section.** Even if you only have two or three premium items, housing them in their own section at the top of the menu creates a strong anchor while also functioning as a [Puzzle promotion strategy](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) -- giving high-margin items the visibility they need.

<Callout type="tip">
The anchor item does not need to be your most expensive ingredient. A whole fish presented dramatically on a wooden board with herbs and sauce costs you 180-250 THB but can be priced at 690-890 THB because the perceived value of "whole fish presentation" is high. Your margin on the anchor can be 70%+ while it simultaneously makes your 280 THB curries feel like a steal.
</Callout>

## Decoy Pricing: The Third Option That Changes Everything

Decoy pricing is the principle that adding a third option can change how people choose between the original two. It works because humans are not good at evaluating options in absolute terms -- we evaluate them relative to each other.

The classic example outside of restaurants: a magazine offers digital-only for $59 or print-plus-digital for $125. Most people choose digital. But add a third option -- print-only for $125 (same price as print-plus-digital) -- and suddenly 84% of people choose print-plus-digital because it looks like a bargain compared to the print-only decoy.

In a Thai restaurant, decoy pricing shows up most naturally in portion sizes, set meals, and drink lists.

### Portion Size Decoys

Suppose you serve tom yum goong in two sizes:

- **Regular** (one person): 220 THB
- **Large** (sharing, 2-3 people): 380 THB

Most customers default to the regular because they are ordering for themselves or cannot evaluate whether "large" is worth the premium. Now add a medium:

- **Regular** (one person): 220 THB
- **Medium** (generous one, light two): 340 THB
- **Large** (sharing, 2-3 people): 380 THB

The medium is the decoy. At 340 THB versus 380 THB for large, the large now looks like a bargain -- only 40 THB more for a much bigger portion. Orders of the large size increase by 25-35% in every restaurant where I have tested this. Your food cost on the large is marginally higher, but the revenue increase far outweighs it.

### Set Meal Decoys

Set meals are the most powerful application of decoy pricing in Thailand because Thai dining culture already revolves around shared meals with multiple dishes.

Consider this setup:

- **Set A** (2 people): 2 dishes + rice + 2 drinks = 550 THB
- **Set B** (2 people): 3 dishes + rice + 2 drinks + dessert = 690 THB
- **Set C** (4 people): 5 dishes + rice + 4 drinks + dessert = 1,290 THB

Set A is priced to make Set B look like an obvious upgrade -- for 140 THB more, you get an extra dish and dessert. Set B is the target option; it is where your margin is best, and the decoy effect from Set A pushes people toward it. Set C exists for larger groups and provides an anchor.

I deployed this exact structure with a client in Silom. Before set meals: average check per table was 480 THB. After: 670 THB. That is a 40% increase in average check, driven not by higher prices but by a different choice architecture.

### Beverage Decoys

This works exceptionally well with Thai beverages:

- **Small** Thai iced tea: 60 THB
- **Regular** Thai iced tea: 85 THB
- **Large** Thai iced tea: 95 THB

The regular is the decoy. The gap between regular and large is only 10 THB, which makes the large feel like a no-brainer. Your cost difference between the regular and large is about 3-4 THB (more ice, slightly more tea), so the extra 10 THB revenue is almost pure profit. Multiply by 80-100 beverage orders per day and this one change adds 24,000-30,000 THB/month.

## Charm Pricing and Left-Digit Bias in Thailand

Charm pricing is the practice of ending prices in 9, 5, or 0 to make them feel lower than they are. The "left-digit bias" is the underlying mechanism: we process prices left-to-right and give disproportionate weight to the leftmost digit. A price of 199 THB feels meaningfully cheaper than 200 THB, even though the difference is 1 THB.

This is well-documented globally, but it works differently in Thailand for three reasons.

### The Thai Baht Factor

Thailand operates in a currency where everyday restaurant prices sit in the 100-500 THB range. This means the left-digit effect triggers at different thresholds than in dollar or euro markets:

- **99 to 100 THB** — crossing from two digits to three. This is the most psychologically significant threshold in Thai casual dining. A dish at 99 THB lives in "under 100 baht" territory, which Thai consumers strongly associate with affordable street food pricing. A dish at 100 THB has crossed into "restaurant pricing." This one-baht gap costs you almost nothing but signals value.

- **199 to 200 THB** — the mid-range threshold. Below 200 THB feels "reasonable" to most Bangkok diners. Above 200 THB starts to feel "upscale casual." If your target dish price is around 200 THB, pricing at 195 or 199 THB keeps it psychologically in the affordable zone.

- **299 to 300 THB** — the premium threshold. For many Thai customers, 300 THB is the point where a single dish starts to feel expensive for casual dining. Pricing at 289 or 295 THB avoids tripping that wire.

- **490 to 500 THB** — the fine dining floor. For premium dishes, the difference between 490 and 500 THB matters less because customers in this range have already accepted they are spending. Here, round numbers (500, 600, 800) actually work better because they signal quality rather than value.

### When Charm Pricing Works in Thailand

**For Thai customers in casual dining:** charm pricing is effective and expected. Thai consumers are accustomed to prices ending in 9 or 5 from 7-Eleven, Makro, and Lotus's. Using 189 THB instead of 190 THB feels natural and does not signal "cheap."

**For tourist-heavy restaurants in mid-range pricing:** charm pricing works well. A pad thai at 179 THB feels meaningfully different from 190 THB to a tourist comparing menus on the street.

**For delivery platforms:** charm pricing is almost mandatory. On GrabFood and LINE MAN, customers are scrolling through dozens of options and comparing prices at a glance. A dish at 149 THB will get more clicks than the identical dish at 150 THB. This is well-documented in platform data.

### When Charm Pricing Hurts

**Fine dining and upscale restaurants:** round numbers signal confidence and quality. A tasting menu at 3,500 THB communicates premium. A tasting menu at 3,499 THB communicates that you are trying to look cheaper than you are. Every Michelin-starred restaurant in Bangkok uses round numbers, and this is not coincidence.

**Wine and cocktail lists:** round numbers dominate in premium beverage pricing globally. A glass of wine at 350 THB reads better than 349 THB. The context signals "I chose this number because this is what the wine is worth," not "I am trying to squeeze under a threshold."

**High-end single items:** a wagyu dish at 1,200 THB reads as premium. At 1,199 THB, it reads as a discount store trying to look premium. The higher the price, the more round numbers communicate confidence.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not mix pricing strategies on the same menu. If your stir-fries end in 9 (179, 189, 199) but your curries use round numbers (220, 280, 350), it feels inconsistent and confusing. Pick one approach per menu section at minimum, or ideally one approach for the entire menu that matches your positioning.
</Callout>

## Price Positioning: Where You Sit on the Menu Page

The physical or digital location of a price on the menu affects whether customers focus on it. This interacts with anchoring -- you want customers to see your anchor prices, but you do not want them to dwell on the prices of items you want them to order on impulse.

### Drop the Currency Symbol

Cornell University's Center for Hospitality Research published research showing that removing the currency symbol increases average spend. "220" instead of "฿220" or "220 THB" makes the price less "price-like" -- it reduces the pain of paying because the number is decoupled from the concept of money.

In Thailand, this is standard practice at upscale restaurants (Le Du, Gaggan, Sorn all do this) but rare at casual dining. Even at the casual level, switching from "฿180" or "180 THB" to just "180" with a small footer note ("All prices in Thai Baht, subject to 7% VAT and 10% service charge") makes the menu feel cleaner and reduces price sensitivity.

### Kill the Dot Leaders

Those dotted lines leading from the dish name to the price:

> Pad Thai Goong ........................ 189

They turn your menu into a price list. The eye follows the dots straight to the number, and the customer starts comparing prices rather than reading descriptions. Instead, nest the price at the end of the description in the same font weight, or right-align prices without a visual trail.

Before:
> Pad Thai Goong ............ 189
> Green Curry Chicken ...... 229
> Tom Yum Goong ............ 259

After:
> **Pad Thai with Tiger Prawns** — Tamarind sauce, crushed peanuts, lime, bean sprouts. 189
> **Green Curry with Free-Range Chicken** — Coconut cream, Thai eggplant, sweet basil. 229
> **Tom Yum Goong** — Hot and sour prawn soup with galangal, lemongrass, kaffir lime. 259

The second format draws attention to the food first and the price second. The descriptions also justify the prices -- "tiger prawns" and "free-range chicken" make 189 and 229 feel like value.

### Box Your Stars, Not Your Prices

Many Thai menus put prices in boxes or highlight them with colour. This is backwards. You should be highlighting your highest-margin items, not their prices. A coloured box around "Chef's Recommendation" with a brief description and no special price emphasis draws orders to that item. A coloured box around "350 THB" draws attention to the cost and triggers the "is it worth it?" calculation.

For a deeper dive on menu layout psychology -- the golden triangle, section ordering, and photography placement -- see the menu design section in [Menu Engineering for Thai Restaurants](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand).

## The Psychology of "Good Value" -- Thai vs. Expat Diners

This is where pricing psychology in Thailand gets interesting, because "good value" means fundamentally different things to Thai customers and expat or tourist customers, and most restaurants get at least one of these segments wrong.

### How Thai Customers Evaluate Value

Thai dining culture has several pricing norms that shape perception:

**Comparison to street food is automatic.** When a Thai customer sees pad kra pao for 180 THB on your menu, they are mentally comparing it to the 50-60 THB version from the street cart outside their office. They accept that a restaurant charges more -- air conditioning, ambiance, service -- but the mental comparison happens instantly. This means your premium over street food prices needs justification that is visible: better ingredients, larger portions, plating, the dining experience.

**Shared dining changes the math.** A Thai family of four does not think in terms of individual dish prices -- they think in terms of total table spend. Four dishes at 200 THB each plus rice and drinks comes to about 1,000 THB for four people, or 250 THB per person. That is the number they are evaluating, not the price of any single dish. This means individual item prices can be higher than you think, as long as the total table spend feels appropriate.

**"Khum" (คุ้ม) -- getting your money's worth.** This is the Thai concept of value and it is more nuanced than "cheap." Khum means you got what you paid for and then some. A 300 THB dish that is generous, well-presented, and delicious is khum. A 150 THB dish that is skimpy and mediocre is not. Thai customers will pay more for khum; they will not return for a bad deal regardless of the price.

**Price sensitivity is category-dependent.** Thai customers are highly price-sensitive on rice dishes and basic stir-fries (these "should" be cheap), moderately sensitive on curries and soups, and relatively insensitive on seafood and premium items (these are "expected" to be expensive). This means you have more pricing flexibility on your seafood and specialty items than on your fried rice.

### How Expat and Tourist Customers Evaluate Value

**They convert to their home currency.** A 280 THB green curry is $8 USD or €7.50 EUR. To a Western tourist, this is absurdly cheap compared to Thai restaurants in London or New York. Many tourists mentally add 20-30% to what they would normally spend in Thailand because everything feels like a bargain. This is why tourist-area restaurants can charge 350-400 THB for the same dish that goes for 200 THB in a Thai-majority neighbourhood -- tourists still perceive it as cheap. The [restaurant concepts currently thriving in Bangkok](/blog/restaurant-concepts-bangkok-2026) are the ones that understand this gap and price into it without alienating local diners.

**They compare to other Bangkok restaurants, not to street food.** Tourists do not have a lifetime of street food reference points. Their anchors are the last three restaurants they ate at in Bangkok, the prices they saw on TripAdvisor reviews, and the rates at their hotel restaurant. If those reference points were all 300-500 THB per dish, your 280 THB curry looks like a find.

**Ambiance matters more than portion size.** Western diners place higher value on the dining environment -- lighting, music, decor, service quality -- relative to portion size. A beautiful restaurant that serves slightly smaller portions at slightly higher prices will score higher with expat and tourist customers than a plain room with massive plates.

**Beverage spend is higher.** Tourists and expats are more likely to order cocktails, wine, and imported beer, where your margins are 75-85%. A restaurant that attracts a mixed Thai-expat crowd should invest in its beverage program because the expat segment subsidises your overall margin.

### Pricing Strategy for Mixed Clientele

If your customer base is 50/50 Thai and non-Thai (common in Sukhumvit, Silom, Ari, Thonglor), the optimal approach is:

1. **Price your base menu for Thai value perception.** Your stir-fries and rice dishes should feel fair to Thai customers -- 160-220 THB range for casual dining.
2. **Build margin through premium options and beverages.** Offer upgrade options (river prawns instead of regular, +80 THB), premium dishes that appeal to adventurous expat diners, and a curated drink menu.
3. **Use set meals to capture tourist spend.** A "Taste of Thailand" set for 890 THB that includes five dishes, rice, and a drink is perceived as a great deal by tourists (who would pay $25 for Thai food back home) while generating a blended 68-72% margin for you.

This is not dual pricing -- everyone sees the same menu and the same prices. You are designing a menu architecture that naturally captures value from both segments.

## Bundling and Set Meals: Thailand's Secret Weapon

Bundling works better in Thailand than almost any other market I have worked in. There are structural reasons for this.

Thai dining is communal. A table orders multiple dishes to share, plus rice, plus drinks. This shared format is inherently a bundle -- customers are already thinking in terms of "what should we get for the table" rather than "what should I order for myself." Set meals formalise this behaviour and steer it toward your highest-margin items.

### How to Build a Profitable Set Meal

The key to profitable bundling is this: the customer must perceive the bundle as a discount, while you achieve a higher total margin than if they had ordered individually.

Here is an example from a client in Sathorn:

**Individual pricing:**
- Tom yum goong: 260 THB (food cost 78 THB, margin 70%)
- Pad kra pao: 180 THB (food cost 38 THB, margin 79%)
- Green curry: 240 THB (food cost 52 THB, margin 78%)
- Steamed rice x3: 60 THB (food cost 12 THB)
- Thai iced tea x2: 140 THB (food cost 20 THB)

If ordered individually: 880 THB, total food cost 200 THB, margin 77%.

**Set meal pricing:**
"Classic Thai Set for 2" -- includes all of the above for 790 THB.

The customer perceives a 90 THB savings (10% discount). But your food cost is identical at 200 THB, so your margin drops only from 77% to 75%. Meanwhile, the set meal steers customers toward your chosen items (all Stars or promoted Puzzles), increases the average order value for customers who might have ordered only 2 dishes, and reduces order time because the decision is pre-made.

In practice, this client saw 35% of tables ordering the set meal within the first month. Average table spend increased from 720 THB to 830 THB because the set meal pulled up the spending of customers who would have ordered less.

### Set Meal Tiers

Offer two or three tiers. The psychological principle at work is compromise effect -- when given three options, people disproportionately choose the middle one.

Set B should account for 50-60% of set meal orders if priced correctly. Set A exists to make Set B look generous. Set C exists for groups and as a price anchor.

### Bundling on Delivery Platforms

Set meals are even more effective on GrabFood and LINE MAN, where:

- Customers are browsing quickly and appreciate pre-made decisions
- The platform commission (30-35%) makes individual low-priced items unprofitable
- Bundles increase average order value, which improves your platform ranking
- You control which items go into the bundle (your highest-margin dishes)

A "Delivery Combo" at 299 THB that includes pad kra pao + rice + Thai iced tea costs you roughly 58 THB in food cost. After 35% platform commission (105 THB), your net revenue is 194 THB with a food cost of 58 THB -- a 70% margin on the net. The same items ordered individually at full price with commission would net you a similar margin but generate a lower average order value.

## How Michelin-Starred Restaurants Price Differently

Michelin-starred restaurants in Bangkok operate on a completely different pricing psychology, and understanding it helps even if you are running casual dining, because the principles scale down.

### Tasting Menu as Price Architecture

Most starred restaurants in Bangkok (Le Du, Sorn, Gaggan, Nusara) use tasting menus as their primary format. A tasting menu is the ultimate pricing psychology tool:

**It eliminates comparison.** There is no a la carte menu to compare against, so customers cannot calculate what each course "should" cost. A 12-course tasting menu at 5,800 THB is a single price for a complete experience. Is the amuse-bouche worth 200 THB? The question does not arise.

**It controls the narrative.** The chef decides the food cost mix -- alternating high-cost courses (wagyu, caviar) with low-cost courses (vegetables, broths, bread) to hit an overall food cost of 35-45% while creating the impression that every course is premium.

**It maximises beverage revenue.** A wine or juice pairing at 2,800-4,500 THB on top of the food menu is where Michelin restaurants make their real money. The pairing margin is typically 78-85%, and uptake rates run 50-70% of covers. A restaurant doing 40 covers at 5,800 THB food + 3,500 THB pairing on 60% uptake generates 316,000 THB per evening -- roughly 200,000 THB from food and 116,000 THB from beverages.

**Round numbers signal premium.** Every starred restaurant in Bangkok prices in round numbers: 3,800, 4,500, 5,800. Never 3,799. The round number communicates "this is the price, and it is worth it." Any hint of charm pricing would undermine the positioning.

### What Casual Restaurants Can Learn from Michelin Pricing

You do not need a Michelin star to apply these principles:

**Create a fixed-price option alongside a la carte.** Even a casual Thai restaurant can offer a "Chef's Tasting" for two at 1,490 THB that includes five courses chosen by the kitchen. This targets the growing segment of Thai and expat diners who want a curated experience and are willing to pay for the convenience of not choosing. Your food cost is lower because you control the menu, and the perceived value is higher because "the chef chose it."

**Use beverage pairing suggestions.** You do not need a sommelier. A simple "Recommended pairing: Lemongrass Cooler (90 THB)" next to your star dishes is enough to increase beverage attachment by 10-15%.

**Price with confidence.** If your food is good and your experience is worth it, price accordingly. The restaurants that fail in Bangkok are not the ones that charge too much -- they are the ones that [charge too little and bleed out on thin margins](/blog/why-restaurants-fail-thailand).

## Seasonal Pricing Strategies

Thailand has three seasons: hot (March-May), rainy (June-October), and cool (November-February). Each creates pricing opportunities.

### Cool Season (November-February): Peak Tourist Season

Bangkok's tourist population roughly doubles during these months, with European and North American visitors escaping winter. This creates pricing leverage:

**Add seasonal specials at premium prices.** A "Winter Market Set" or "Cool Season Tasting" priced 15-20% above your regular menu feels justified because it is positioned as seasonal and limited. Tourist customers are primed to spend more during their holiday.

**Do not discount.** Many restaurants run promotions during peak season to "attract more customers." You do not need more customers when demand is already high. Protect your margins and focus on maximising per-cover revenue.

**Extend evening service.** Tourist customers eat later than Thai customers and are more likely to order a full meal plus drinks at 9 PM. If your kitchen closes at 9:30 PM, you are missing revenue. Consider extending to 10:30 or 11 PM during peak months.

### Hot Season (March-May): The Local Opportunity

Tourist traffic dips, but Bangkok residents still eat out. This is when smart pricing focuses on the Thai customer:

**Lunch sets at value pricing.** Office workers in Bangkok eat out daily. A 149-179 THB lunch set that includes a main dish, rice, and a drink hits the right price point for daily spend. Your margin on these is 68-75% because the components are your lowest-cost items (stir-fries, fried rice, simple soups).

**Beverage promotions drive traffic.** "Beat the Heat" promotions on cold drinks and desserts work well because they are seasonally relevant and beverages carry 80%+ margins. Buy-one-get-one on Thai iced tea during 2-5 PM costs you 8-12 THB per free drink but brings in customers who will also order food.

**Introduce seasonal items.** Mango sticky rice from March to June is a Star-level item: food cost of 35-45 THB, sold at 150-220 THB, ordered by 30-40% of tables during peak mango season. If you are not running seasonal mango dishes during this period, you are leaving significant revenue on the table.

### Rainy Season (June-October): The Retention Period

Traffic is lowest during the rainy season, and this is when many restaurants panic and discount aggressively. That is the wrong response.

**Bundle more, discount less.** Instead of dropping prices, add value. A rainy-season set meal that includes a free soup or dessert costs you 15-25 THB in food cost but preserves your menu prices while giving customers a reason to visit.

**Comfort food repricing.** Soups, hot pots, and warm curries sell better in the rain. If these items are already Stars, test a 10-20 THB price increase during these months. Demand is higher, and customers will pay.

**Loyalty incentives over discounts.** A stamp card ("Visit 5 times during rainy season, get a free dessert") drives repeat visits without training customers to expect lower prices.

For more on how seasonal menu changes interact with [menu engineering categories](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand), the key principle is: your Stars may shift seasonally, so track your matrix quarterly at minimum.

## Common Pricing Psychology Mistakes in Thai Restaurants

I see these mistakes repeatedly across the restaurants I audit.

### 1. Pricing Everything at the Same Level

A menu where every item is 180-220 THB gives customers no reference points. There are no anchors, no premium options to make the mid-range feel like value, and no entry-level items for price-sensitive customers. The result is a flat menu where nothing stands out.

**Fix:** Create a price spread. Your lowest items should be 30-40% below your average, your mid-range should cluster around the average, and your highest items should be 80-120% above the average. For a casual Thai restaurant with a 220 THB average, that means items ranging from 130 THB to 450 THB.

### 2. Changing Prices Too Often -- or Never

Some restaurants reprice monthly in response to ingredient fluctuations, which confuses regulars and erodes trust. Others set prices on opening day and never touch them for three years while food costs rise 20%.

**Fix:** Reprice annually for your core menu, with a quarterly cost review to flag items where the food cost has shifted by more than 3 percentage points. Use seasonal specials to test new price points before committing to a permanent change. For more on tracking your food costs against menu prices, see [Food Cost Control for Bangkok Restaurants](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).

### 3. Discounting to Compete

When a new competitor opens nearby, the instinct is to drop prices. This is almost always wrong. A price war in Bangkok's restaurant market is a race to the bottom that nobody wins, because your costs do not decrease when your prices do.

**Fix:** Compete on value, not price. Better food, better service, better ambiance, better Instagram presence. If your 220 THB green curry is genuinely better than the competitor's 180 THB version, your customers will stay. If it is not, your problem is the product, not the price.

### 4. Ignoring Beverage Pricing

Many operators spend weeks engineering their food menu and give their beverage prices zero thought, just rounding to the nearest 10 THB based on gut feel. This is an enormous missed opportunity.

Beverages carry 78-88% margins. A well-priced beverage menu with strategic upselling and pairing suggestions can add 50,000-100,000 THB/month in profit to a mid-sized restaurant with no additional kitchen capacity needed.

**Fix:** Apply the same Stars/Plowhorses/Puzzles/Dogs analysis to your beverage menu that you apply to food. Price your signature drinks based on perceived value, not just cost-plus. A house-made lemongrass cooler that costs 12 THB to produce can sell for 110-130 THB if the presentation and description justify it.

### 5. Pricing Delivery the Same as Dine-In

GrabFood and LINE MAN take 30-35% commission. If you charge the same prices on delivery as dine-in, you are giving away a third of your revenue and your margins collapse. I have seen restaurants running 5% net margin on delivery because they never adjusted their platform prices -- and 5% is before the occasional refund or cancelled order pushes it negative.

**Fix:** Raise delivery prices by 15-20% or create delivery-specific bundles that achieve the target margin after commission. Most customers expect delivery prices to be slightly higher and do not compare them to dine-in menus. For a full breakdown, read: [How Much Does It Cost to Open a Restaurant in Bangkok?](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok) -- specifically the cloud kitchen section, which covers the margin math for delivery-only operations.

## Putting It Into Practice: A Pricing Psychology Audit

Here is a 7-step audit you can run on your own menu this week:

1. **Check your anchors.** What is the first price a customer sees in each section? If it is your cheapest item, move it. Put your highest-priced item first.

2. **Look for decoy opportunities.** Where can you add a third size or tier that makes your target option look like a better deal?

3. **Evaluate your charm pricing.** Are your prices consistent? Do they match your positioning? Casual dining should use charm pricing (ending in 9 or 5). Upscale should use round numbers.

4. **Check the currency symbol.** Are you using ฿ or THB on the menu? Try removing it and using a footer note instead.

5. **Kill the dot leaders.** If your menu has dotted lines from item name to price, remove them and add descriptions instead.

6. **Design a set meal.** Build two or three tiers using the compromise effect, with your target option in the middle and your highest-margin items featured.

7. **Compare dine-in and delivery pricing.** If they are the same, your delivery margin is probably underwater. Adjust.

This audit takes one afternoon and a menu reprint. The impact on your revenue starts the day the new menu goes live.
