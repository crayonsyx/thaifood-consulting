A client of mine lost 340,000 THB in a single week last October. Not because of a bad review, a staff walkout, or a kitchen fire. Because the flooding in Nakhon Ratchasima knocked out the province's vegetable farms for two weeks, his sole produce supplier could not fulfill orders, and he had no backup sourcing plan. He spent the week scrambling through Makro, paying 40-60% above his normal costs for whatever was left on the shelves, while three of his signature dishes came off the menu entirely.

That week was entirely preventable. Not the flood -- nobody controls weather -- but the 340,000 THB in damage to his business. A proper supply chain management system would have had dual sourcing in place, safety stock for critical ingredients, and a crisis protocol that activated the moment his primary supplier flagged a disruption. Instead, he had what most Thai restaurant operators have: a phone full of LINE contacts and a vague assumption that ingredients will show up when ordered.

Supply chain management sounds like a concept for factories and logistics companies, not restaurants. But every restaurant is a manufacturing operation. You take raw materials, transform them through labor and equipment, and deliver a finished product to a customer -- dozens or hundreds of times a day. The supply chain is everything between the farm and the plate, and managing it well is the difference between a restaurant that runs smoothly and one that lurches from crisis to crisis wondering why margins keep shrinking.

This article is the operational framework I use with consulting clients to build supply chains that are efficient, resilient, and adapted to Thailand's specific risks. If you already have your [supplier network in place](/blog/food-suppliers-bangkok-restaurants), this is the next level: making that network function as a system rather than a collection of ad hoc relationships.

## What Supply Chain Management Actually Means for Restaurants

When restaurant operators hear "supply chain management," most think it means "buying ingredients." That is procurement -- one piece of a much larger system. True supply chain management covers every step from the moment an ingredient is harvested or produced until it reaches a customer's plate, plus the planning and risk management that keeps the whole system running.

**Demand planning:** Predicting how much of each ingredient you will need, when you will need it, and adjusting for seasonal patterns, events, and trends.

**Sourcing and procurement:** Selecting suppliers, negotiating terms, placing orders, and managing supplier relationships. This is the part most operators already do, though often informally.

**Receiving and quality control:** Inspecting deliveries, verifying quantities and quality, rejecting substandard product, and documenting everything.

**Storage and inventory management:** Maintaining proper storage conditions, rotating stock (FIFO), tracking inventory levels, and minimizing waste. For a deeper look at the inventory side, see our [inventory management guide](/blog/restaurant-inventory-management-thailand).

**Production planning:** Coordinating between what the kitchen needs, what inventory is available, and what needs to be ordered -- across daily prep, special events, and menu changes.

**Distribution:** For multi-unit operators, moving prepared or semi-prepared items from a central kitchen to satellite locations.

**Waste management:** Tracking and reducing waste at every stage -- over-ordering, spoilage, prep waste, plate waste -- to control cost and improve sustainability.

**Risk management:** Identifying what can go wrong, building contingencies, and having plans that activate when disruptions happen.

Most restaurants do some of these things intuitively. Very few do all of them systematically. The gap between intuitive and systematic is where money leaks out of your business.

<Callout type="info">
In my experience with Bangkok restaurants, operators who implement formal supply chain management reduce their food cost by 3-7 percentage points within the first six months. On a restaurant doing 1 million THB in monthly revenue, that is 30,000-70,000 THB per month in recovered margin -- without changing the menu, raising prices, or cutting portion sizes.
</Callout>

## Mapping Your Supply Chain: Every Touchpoint from Farm to Plate

You cannot manage what you have not mapped. The first step in building a supply chain management system is documenting exactly how ingredients move through your operation.

### The Supply Chain Map Exercise

Sit down with your head chef and purchasing person (often the same person in smaller operations) and trace the journey of your five highest-volume ingredients from origin to plate. For each ingredient, document:

**Origin:** Where is this ingredient produced? Which province, which farm or factory, which country if imported?

**Intermediaries:** How many hands does it pass through between origin and your kitchen? A head of lettuce from Kanchanaburi might go: farm to provincial aggregator to Talad Thai wholesaler to your restaurant. That is three intermediaries, each adding cost and time, and each representing a point where quality or cold chain can break down.

**Transportation:** How does it travel at each stage? Refrigerated truck, open pickup, motorcycle delivery? How long is each transit leg? What temperature controls exist, if any?

**Receiving:** Who receives it at your restaurant? What do they check? How long does it sit before going into storage?

**Storage:** Where does it go after receiving? How long does it typically sit in storage before being used? What temperature is maintained?

**Prep:** How is it transformed before service? Washing, cutting, marinating, pre-cooking?

**Service:** How does it reach the plate? What is the final cooking and plating process?

**Waste points:** Where does waste occur along this chain? Trim waste during prep, spoilage during storage, plate waste after service?

This map sounds tedious, but it reveals things you did not know about your own operation. I have done this exercise with over 30 restaurants and it always surfaces surprises: ingredients sitting at room temperature for 45 minutes during receiving because the kitchen is busy with prep, produce spoiling because it is stored next to ethylene-producing fruits, frozen proteins thawing and refreezing due to poorly maintained equipment.

### Common Supply Chain Blind Spots in Thai Restaurants

**The last-mile problem.** Your supplier may have a proper cold chain from the farm to their warehouse, but the delivery to your restaurant happens on a motorcycle with a styrofoam box in 35-degree heat. The last mile is often the weakest link.

**Receiving gaps.** In many Thai restaurants, deliveries arrive during the morning prep rush, and whoever is closest to the door signs for them without checking temperature, weight, or quality. I have seen restaurants accept deliveries of partially thawed seafood and warm dairy simply because the receiving person was too busy or too inexperienced to reject them.

**Storage creep.** Your walk-in starts at the right temperature, but over the course of a busy day -- doors opening constantly, warm prep items going in, the compressor struggling in afternoon heat -- the temperature can drift up by 2-4 degrees. Without monitoring, you do not know until product quality tells you.

**The single-source trap.** Many operators buy a critical ingredient from one supplier for years because the relationship works. Until it does not. A single-source dependency on any ingredient that appears on more than 30% of your menu items is a supply chain risk you need to address.

<Callout type="warning">
Map your supply chain on paper or in a spreadsheet before investing in any technology. Software is useful for managing an already-understood supply chain, but it cannot fix a supply chain you have not mapped. Start analog, understand the system, then digitize.
</Callout>

## Supply Chain Risks Specific to Thailand

Thailand's geography, climate, infrastructure, and political dynamics create supply chain risks that are different from what operators face in more temperate, infrastructure-heavy countries. Understanding these risks is the foundation of building a resilient system.

### Natural Disasters

**Flooding.** Thailand floods regularly. The 2011 mega-flood was catastrophic, but smaller regional floods happen every monsoon season (June-October) and disrupt agricultural production, transportation, and distribution. Provinces that produce the majority of Thailand's vegetables -- Nakhon Pathom, Kanchanaburi, Nakhon Ratchasima, Chiang Mai -- are all flood-prone during heavy monsoon years.

**Impact on restaurants:** When a major growing province floods, the supply of affected produce drops sharply within 3-5 days, while prices spike 30-80%. The disruption typically lasts 2-6 weeks depending on severity. Recovery is slow because damaged crops need replanting and regrowth cycles.

**Drought.** Thailand's dry season (November-April) creates water stress for farms, particularly in the northeast (Isan) and central plains. Prolonged drought reduces crop yields, raises prices, and can wipe out seasonal crops entirely. The 2024 drought cut rice yields by 15-20% in affected provinces and sent jasmine rice prices to five-year highs.

**Tropical storms.** Less frequent than flooding but more sudden. Tropical storms can close airports (disrupting imported ingredient shipments), damage coastal fishing operations (seafood supply), and knock out roads for days.

### Transportation Disruptions

**Bangkok traffic.** This is the most mundane but most consistent supply chain risk. A delivery scheduled for 7 AM arrives at 9:30 AM because of an accident on the expressway, and your morning prep is scrambled because critical ingredients are not there. Average delivery delays of 30-90 minutes are normal in Bangkok, and your operations need to absorb this variability.

**Highway closures.** Political protests, accidents, and construction regularly close major highways between Bangkok and the provinces. Your produce from Chiang Mai travels roughly 700 km on Highway 1 -- a single closure can delay delivery by a full day.

**Port delays.** Imported ingredients (cheese, wine, specialty products) enter through Laem Chabang port. Customs clearance delays, port congestion, and container shortages create unpredictable lead times. What should take 3-5 days from vessel arrival to your importer's warehouse sometimes takes 2-3 weeks.

### Seasonal Price Volatility

Thailand's ingredient prices are far more volatile than in countries with heavier agricultural subsidies and cold storage infrastructure. The [ingredient cost seasonality guide](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality) covers specific price patterns, but in general:

- Fresh produce prices can swing 30-80% between low and peak season
- Protein prices spike 10-25% around major holidays (Songkran, Chinese New Year, Vegetarian Festival)
- Seafood prices are heavily weather-dependent -- a single bad storm in the Gulf of Thailand can double shrimp prices overnight
- Imported ingredient prices are exposed to currency fluctuations (THB/USD and THB/EUR) on top of base price changes

### Supplier-Specific Risks

**Financial instability.** Small Thai suppliers, particularly market vendors and local farms, operate on thin margins. A bad season, a major debtor defaulting, or a personal crisis can shut them down with little warning.

**Quality drift.** A supplier who was excellent when you started may gradually decline in quality as they cut costs, lose experienced staff, or take on more customers than they can serve. Without regular monitoring, you will not notice until customers do.

**Regulatory changes.** Thai food safety regulations are evolving. New pesticide restrictions, import requirements, or labeling rules can suddenly make a product unavailable or significantly more expensive. The 2025 changes to pesticide residue limits on imported produce caught several importers off-guard and disrupted supply for weeks.

## Building a Resilient Supply Chain

Resilience means your supply chain can absorb disruptions without severely impacting your restaurant operations. It does not mean nothing ever goes wrong -- that is impossible. It means that when something goes wrong, your system handles it gracefully.

### Dual Sourcing

The most effective resilience strategy is dual sourcing: maintaining two qualified suppliers for every critical ingredient category.

**How it works in practice:**
- For each ingredient category (produce, protein, seafood, dry goods, dairy, specialty imports), qualify two suppliers
- Give 70-80% of your volume to the primary supplier and 20-30% to the secondary
- The secondary supplier gets enough regular business to keep your account active and your requirements understood
- When the primary fails, you shift volume to the secondary immediately -- they already know your specifications, your delivery requirements, and your quality standards

**What dual sourcing costs.** You may pay slightly more per unit on the 20-30% going to your secondary supplier, since lower volume means less negotiating leverage. In my experience, the premium is 3-8% on those items. On a monthly ingredient spend of 300,000 THB, allocating 20% to secondary suppliers at an average 5% premium costs you about 3,000 THB extra per month. That is cheap insurance against a supplier failure that could cost you ten times that in a single week.

**Where dual sourcing is non-negotiable:**
- Your primary protein (chicken, pork, beef, seafood -- whatever drives the most menu items)
- Fresh produce and herbs used in more than 30% of dishes
- Any imported specialty ingredient that defines your concept
- Cooking oils and basic staples where running out stops the kitchen

<Callout type="tip">
When setting up dual sourcing, do not tell your secondary supplier they are "the backup." Position them as a growing partner: "I'm currently splitting my purchasing across two suppliers and looking to increase volume with the one who performs best over the next six months." This keeps them motivated to deliver quality and service, rather than treating your account as an afterthought.
</Callout>

### Safety Stock

Safety stock is a buffer of critical ingredients maintained above your normal operating inventory to absorb supply disruptions.

**Calculating safety stock.** For each critical ingredient, determine:
- Average daily usage (from your POS data and recipe yields)
- Maximum lead time for restocking (longest realistic time from order to delivery, including disruption scenarios)
- Normal lead time for restocking

**Safety stock formula:** (Maximum lead time - Normal lead time) x Average daily usage

**Example:** You use 15 kg of chicken breast per day. Normal restocking lead time is 1 day (order today, delivered tomorrow). Maximum lead time during a disruption is 3 days (supplier issues, need to switch to backup). Safety stock = (3 - 1) x 15 = 30 kg of chicken breast maintained as buffer.

**What this looks like in practice.** For a mid-sized Bangkok restaurant, safety stock for critical ingredients typically represents 30,000-60,000 THB in additional inventory investment. This is money tied up in frozen proteins, dry goods, and shelf-stable items -- not a cost, because you rotate through it using FIFO, but capital that stays committed.

**What can be safety-stocked:**
- Frozen proteins (chicken, pork, seafood): 3-5 day buffer
- Rice and dry goods: 7-14 day buffer (long shelf life, low storage cost)
- Cooking oils and sauces: 7-14 day buffer
- Canned and preserved ingredients: 14-30 day buffer

**What cannot be easily safety-stocked:**
- Fresh herbs and leafy greens (2-3 day shelf life)
- Fresh seafood (1-2 day shelf life)
- Dairy products (limited refrigerated shelf life)

For perishable items, dual sourcing is your primary resilience strategy since safety stock is impractical.

### Local Alternatives

For every imported or specialty ingredient, identify a local alternative that can substitute in an emergency.

**Examples from client operations:**
- Japanese panko breadcrumbs replaced with Thai-made breadcrumbs (quality difference exists but acceptable for 1-2 weeks)
- Italian San Marzano tomatoes replaced with Thai Roma tomatoes from Royal Project farms
- Australian wagyu replaced with Thai premium beef from Betagro's Angus program (different quality tier but keeps the dish available)
- French butter replaced with New Zealand butter (widely available at Makro)
- Imported mozzarella replaced with Dairy Home's domestic buffalo mozzarella

The goal is not permanent substitution. It is keeping your restaurant operational during a supply disruption of 1-3 weeks while your primary supply chain recovers. Document these alternatives in your supply chain manual so the kitchen team knows what to switch to and how to adjust the recipe.

## Demand Forecasting for Restaurants

Demand forecasting is predicting how many covers you will serve and which dishes they will order, so you can calculate exactly what ingredients to buy. Most restaurant operators forecast based on gut feeling. Systematic forecasting improves accuracy by 20-40%, which directly reduces both waste (buying too much) and stockouts (buying too little).

### Data You Need

**POS sales history.** At minimum, 3 months of daily sales data broken down by dish. Twelve months is ideal because it captures seasonal patterns. Every modern POS system exports this data.

**Reservation data.** For restaurants that take reservations, your booking system tells you future demand 1-7 days in advance. A restaurant at 40% reservation rate can forecast 40% of tomorrow's covers with reasonable confidence today.

**Event calendar.** Public holidays, local events, competitor openings/closings, weather forecasts, and your own promotions all influence demand. Keep a calendar that flags known demand drivers.

**Historical waste data.** If you track what you throw away (and you should), waste patterns reveal where your forecasts consistently over-estimate demand.

### Forecasting Methods

**Moving average.** The simplest approach: average your sales for the same day of the week over the past 4-6 weeks. Monday's forecast is the average of the last 4-6 Mondays. This smooths out random variation and captures weekly patterns.

**Seasonal adjustment.** Multiply the moving average by a seasonal factor derived from the past year's data. If September historically generates 85% of your annual average revenue, adjust September forecasts down by 15%.

**Event-based adjustment.** For known events (Songkran, Christmas, a nearby concert, a large corporate booking), adjust the forecast based on historical performance during similar events. If Songkran week typically produces 130% of normal revenue, increase your ingredient orders accordingly.

**The 80/20 shortcut.** If building a full forecasting model feels overwhelming, start with this: identify your top 10 dishes by volume (they probably represent 60-80% of your orders), forecast just those 10 dishes using the moving average method, and use those forecasts to drive your ingredient purchasing for the items that matter most. The remaining 20-40% of your menu can be managed with simpler par-level ordering.

<Callout type="tip">
Run your forecasting on a spreadsheet for the first 3 months before investing in software. You need to understand the logic, see where your predictions deviate from reality, and learn which adjustment factors matter for your specific restaurant. Forecasting software is only as good as the judgment behind the inputs, and you build that judgment by doing it manually first.
</Callout>

### Seasonal Demand Patterns in Thailand

Thai restaurants face predictable demand fluctuations that your forecasting should account for:

**High season (November-February):** Tourism peaks, corporate events increase, outdoor dining becomes comfortable. Expect 10-30% above annual average revenue, depending on your location and customer mix. Order ingredients accordingly -- this is not the time to run lean on inventory.

**Songkran (April 13-15):** Most restaurants experience a dip during the actual holiday (staff take leave, locals travel), followed by a spike in the week after when corporate events resume. Tourist-area restaurants may see the opposite pattern.

**Rainy season (June-October):** Foot traffic drops 10-20% at restaurants that depend on walk-in customers. Delivery orders tend to increase during heavy rain periods, so restaurants with a strong delivery channel may see a shift in volume rather than a drop.

**Vegetarian Festival (October):** Restaurants offering vegetarian options see a significant spike. Those that do not offer vegetarian food see a slight dip as a portion of the population temporarily changes eating habits.

## Cold Chain Management in Bangkok's Heat

Bangkok's climate is the enemy of every ingredient that needs to stay cold. Average daytime temperatures of 33-36 degrees C, combined with high humidity and traffic-related delivery delays, mean that your cold chain has no margin for error. This section covers the standards that keep your ingredients safe and your food cost under control.

### Receiving Standards

Every delivery of temperature-sensitive product gets a temperature check before acceptance. This is non-negotiable.

**Temperature thresholds:**
- Refrigerated items: Must arrive at 5 degrees C or below
- Frozen items: Must arrive at -18 degrees C or below
- Hot prepared items (from central kitchen): Must arrive at 60 degrees C or above

**Rejection protocol:** If a delivery arrives above threshold temperature, reject it entirely. Do not accept it "because it is still mostly cold" or "because we will use it today anyway." A delivery of chicken breast that arrives at 8 degrees C has been in the temperature danger zone (5-60 degrees C) for an unknown duration. The food safety risk is not worth the convenience of accepting it.

**Documentation:** Log every delivery with: date, time, supplier, items, temperatures checked, and pass/fail. This log serves three purposes: it gives you data to evaluate supplier cold chain reliability, it protects you in a food safety investigation, and it keeps your receiving staff accountable to the standard.

### Storage Monitoring

**Temperature logging.** Check and record cold storage temperatures twice daily -- once at opening (before the kitchen starts generating heat and opening doors) and once before closing. Post the log on each cold storage unit.

**Digital monitoring.** For the investment of 5,000-15,000 THB per unit, IoT temperature sensors provide continuous monitoring with alerts when temperatures drift out of range. This catches problems overnight, during power outages, or when a compressor starts failing -- long before a twice-daily manual check would.

**Storage organization.** Raw proteins on the bottom shelves (to prevent drip contamination), ready-to-eat items on top shelves, produce in the middle. Separate storage for strong-smelling items (garlic, shrimp paste, durian) that can contaminate other products with off-flavors.

**Capacity management.** A walk-in cooler at 90%+ capacity cannot maintain consistent temperature because air cannot circulate. Keep cold storage at 70-80% capacity maximum. If you consistently exceed this, you are either over-ordering (reduce quantities, increase frequency) or under-equipped (invest in additional cold storage).

### Transportation Requirements

If you are running market purchases (Talad Thai, Simummuang, Khlong Toei), your cold chain responsibility starts the moment you buy the product.

**For market runs:**
- Bring insulated cooler boxes with ice packs for proteins and seafood
- Separate containers for raw proteins and produce (cross-contamination prevention)
- Drive directly back to the restaurant -- no stops for errands
- Receive and store within 30 minutes of leaving the market

**For supplier deliveries:**
- Require refrigerated transport for all proteins, dairy, and seafood in your supplier agreements
- Specify delivery windows (before 9 AM when possible) to minimize heat exposure
- If a supplier delivers by motorcycle, require insulated bags with ice packs as a minimum

**For central kitchen distribution (multi-unit):**
- Dedicated refrigerated vehicles or high-quality insulated containers with gel packs
- Temperature log at dispatch and at receiving
- Maximum transit time standards (typically 45-90 minutes within Bangkok, depending on distance)
- Receiving staff at satellite locations trained to the same temperature check standards as the central kitchen

## Supplier Relationship Management

Your suppliers are partners in your supply chain. How you manage those relationships affects the quality, reliability, and cost of your ingredients.

### Tiering Your Suppliers

Not all supplier relationships deserve equal attention. Categorize your suppliers into tiers based on their importance to your operation:

**Tier 1 (Strategic).** Suppliers of ingredients that define your concept and cannot be easily substituted. Your wagyu importer if you are a steakhouse. Your seafood vendor if you are a seafood restaurant. Your organic farm if organic sourcing is core to your brand. These suppliers get personal relationship investment: face-to-face meetings, joint planning, volume commitments, and priority when allocating your time.

**Tier 2 (Important).** Suppliers of high-volume staples where quality and reliability matter but alternatives exist. Your Makro account, your primary produce vendor, your domestic protein distributor. These suppliers get regular performance reviews and competitive benchmarking, but the relationship is more transactional than strategic.

**Tier 3 (Transactional).** Suppliers of commodity items, disposables, cleaning products, and other non-food items where price is the primary consideration. These suppliers get managed by price comparison and switched when a better option appears.

### Performance Tracking

Track every supplier monthly on four dimensions:

**Quality (1-5 score).** Based on receiving inspection data. How many deliveries met quality standards? Were there any rejections? Was product consistency maintained?

**Reliability (%).** Percentage of deliveries that arrived on time, complete, and as ordered. A supplier who delivers late 20% of the time or shorts your orders regularly is costing you money in scrambled operations, even if their prices are good.

**Responsiveness (1-5 score).** When problems arise -- and they will -- how fast does the supplier respond? Can you reach someone within an hour during business hours? Do they resolve issues proactively, or do you have to chase?

**Cost competitiveness (1-5 score).** Based on quarterly price comparison across 2-3 channels. Is this supplier still offering competitive value, or have better options emerged?

Review these scores quarterly. Share the results with your Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers -- good performers appreciate recognition, and underperformers deserve the chance to improve before you shift volume away from them.

### Regular Reviews

**Monthly:** Quick review of delivery performance data and any open issues with Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers. This can be a 15-minute phone call or LINE message.

**Quarterly:** Sit-down meeting with Tier 1 suppliers to review performance, discuss upcoming needs (seasonal menu changes, event planning, volume projections), and negotiate any pricing adjustments.

**Annually:** Comprehensive supplier review across all tiers. Benchmark pricing against market alternatives, evaluate whether your tier classifications are still correct, and make strategic decisions about consolidating or diversifying your supplier base.

For guidance on the negotiation aspects of supplier management, see our [supplier negotiation guide](/blog/negotiate-suppliers-thailand).

<Callout type="info">
The quarterly review meeting is the most underutilized tool in restaurant supply chain management. I have seen a single quarterly conversation with a protein supplier result in a 5% volume discount that saved a restaurant 8,000 THB per month, simply because the operator demonstrated organized purchasing data and a willingness to discuss committed volume. Suppliers reward organized buyers because organized buyers are reliable revenue.
</Callout>

## Technology for Supply Chain Management

Technology can dramatically improve supply chain visibility, efficiency, and control. But it is a tool, not a solution -- it automates and improves a system that you have already designed.

### Inventory Management Systems

**What they do:** Track ingredient quantities in real time, generate purchase orders based on par levels, alert you when stock is low, calculate food cost by dish, and provide waste and usage analytics.

**Options for Thai restaurants:**
- **FoodStorm / MarketMan:** Cloud-based inventory platforms with Thai restaurant adoption. 2,000-5,000 THB/month. Integrate with some Thai POS systems.
- **POSist / Lightspeed Restaurant:** Full POS systems with built-in inventory modules. 3,000-8,000 THB/month.
- **Google Sheets / Airtable:** Free to low-cost. Surprisingly effective for small-to-medium operations if you set up the templates correctly. The disadvantage is manual data entry.

**When to invest:** If you are spending more than 200,000 THB/month on ingredients, a dedicated inventory system pays for itself through waste reduction and purchasing optimization within 2-3 months. Below that threshold, spreadsheets work fine.

### Ordering Platforms

**Makro PRO app:** Order from Makro online with delivery. Tracks your purchase history by category. Free to use.

**Freshket:** B2B fresh produce platform connecting farms to restaurants. App-based ordering with next-day delivery and cold chain. Useful for produce if your operation cannot justify daily market runs.

**Direct LINE ordering:** Most Thai suppliers manage orders through LINE. This works but creates zero documentation unless you screenshot every conversation. If you use LINE for ordering, at minimum keep a parallel log (even a simple notebook) recording what you ordered, the agreed price, and the delivery date.

### Temperature Monitoring IoT

Wireless temperature sensors placed in walk-in coolers, reach-in refrigerators, and freezers that transmit data continuously to a cloud platform. You get alerts on your phone when temperatures drift out of range.

**Brands available in Thailand:** SensorPush, Testo, and several Thai-made options from Lazada/Shopee. Cost: 3,000-15,000 THB per sensor, no ongoing subscription for most brands.

**ROI justification:** A single spoiled delivery of premium protein can cost 10,000-30,000 THB. A temperature monitoring system that catches one compressor failure or one left-open door per year pays for itself immediately.

### Central Kitchen Management Software

For multi-unit operators using a central kitchen (commissary), dedicated production planning software coordinates ingredient ordering, production scheduling, and distribution to satellite locations.

**Options:** Apicbase, Kitchens United platform, or custom builds on Notion/Airtable for smaller operations. Costs range from 5,000 to 30,000 THB/month depending on complexity.

## Central Kitchen as Supply Chain Hub

If you operate multiple restaurant locations, a central kitchen (commissary) becomes the hub of your supply chain. Rather than each location managing its own procurement, receiving, and prep independently, the central kitchen consolidates these functions.

### Advantages

**Purchasing power.** One entity buying for 3-5 locations has significantly more negotiating leverage than 3-5 separate buyers. Volume discounts of 10-20% above what individual locations can negotiate are common.

**Quality consistency.** Sauces, marinades, bases, and prep items produced in one location by a trained team taste the same at every outlet. This solves the consistency problem that plagues multi-unit operators where each chef interprets recipes slightly differently.

**Reduced waste.** Centralized purchasing means better demand aggregation and less over-ordering. Centralized prep means trim and by-products from one location's needs can be used for another's.

**Simplified receiving.** One receiving point with trained staff and proper equipment (scales, thermometers, inspection protocols) replaces multiple ad hoc receiving processes at individual locations.

**Keep local:** Final cooking (obviously), delicate items that do not transport well (leafy salads, tempura, anything that degrades in 30 minutes), and daily fresh produce that each location uses in small quantities.

## Sustainability in the Supply Chain

Sustainability in restaurant supply chains is not just a marketing angle. It intersects directly with cost management, risk reduction, and long-term supply stability.

### Food Miles and Local Sourcing

Sourcing locally reduces transportation costs, delivery lead times, and the carbon footprint of your supply chain while supporting Thai agricultural communities. It also reduces your exposure to import disruptions (port delays, currency fluctuations, trade policy changes).

**Practical local sourcing wins:**
- Thai herbs and aromatics instead of imported (lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime -- Thailand grows the best in the world)
- Domestic proteins (CP, Betagro, S-Pure) instead of imported for everyday menu items
- Royal Project and local organic farm produce instead of imported organic
- Thai-made dairy (Dairy Home, Chokchai Farm) where quality meets your standards

**Where importing makes sense:** Specialty products that Thailand does not produce at the required quality level -- certain cheeses, specific wine regions, Japanese seafood, European cured meats. Import these strategically for dishes where the imported product is the point, and source locally for everything else.

### Waste Reduction

Food waste in Thai restaurants averages 15-25% of purchased ingredients, based on data from my client base. Reducing this to 8-12% is achievable with systematic effort, and the savings go directly to your bottom line.

**Procurement waste.** Over-ordering because of inaccurate forecasting or lack of inventory visibility. Fix: demand forecasting + real-time inventory tracking.

**Storage waste.** Spoilage due to improper storage conditions, poor FIFO rotation, or excess inventory. Fix: cold chain standards + FIFO enforcement + par-level ordering.

**Prep waste.** Trim, peels, bones, and off-cuts discarded during preparation. Fix: cross-utilization menus (chicken bones become stock, vegetable trim becomes staff meal, fruit peels become garnish or infusions).

**Plate waste.** Food left on the plate by customers. Fix: portion size calibration based on actual consumption data (weigh plate waste for a week to identify which dishes are consistently over-portioned).

### Packaging

For delivery-heavy restaurants, packaging is a significant supply chain cost and sustainability consideration.

**Sustainable options available in Thailand:** Bagasse (sugarcane fiber) containers, paper bags, PLA-lined cups, and bamboo utensils are all available from Thai manufacturers at competitive prices. They cost 15-30% more than standard plastic packaging but increasingly influence customer choice, particularly among younger Bangkok diners and the expat community.

**Packaging as supply chain item:** Treat packaging suppliers with the same rigor as food suppliers -- dual sourcing, quality standards, regular price review. I have seen restaurants run out of delivery containers during a weekend rush because they did not treat packaging as a critical inventory item. Running out of boxes is as damaging as running out of chicken.

## Managing Imported vs. Local Supply Chains

Most Bangkok restaurants of any sophistication run two parallel supply chains: a domestic one for Thai ingredients and an international one for imported products. These two chains have very different characteristics.

### Domestic Supply Chain

**Lead times:** 1-3 days for most items. Overnight for fresh produce from major markets.

**Price behavior:** Volatile, especially for fresh produce. Prices change weekly based on weather, season, and demand. You manage this through flexible purchasing, menu adaptability, and strong market relationships.

**Quality control:** Highly variable. Your receiving inspection standards compensate for inconsistent grading and handling. The relationship with your specific vendor matters more than the market they operate in.

**Payment:** Cash-heavy, relationship-based, informal credit after establishing trust.

### International Supply Chain

**Lead times:** 2-8 weeks from order to delivery for most imported products. Container shipping, customs clearance, and last-mile distribution all add time and variability.

**Price behavior:** More stable in local currency terms (supplier price lists change quarterly rather than weekly), but exposed to currency fluctuation. A 5% move in THB/USD changes your import costs by 5%.

**Quality control:** Generally more consistent (branded products with specifications), but damage during shipping and cold chain breaks during transit are risks. Inspect imported products at receiving just as rigorously as domestic ones.

**Payment:** More formal. Letters of credit, bank transfers, and structured payment terms. Importers typically require 30-50% prepayment for new accounts.

### Managing Both Simultaneously

**Separate ordering calendars.** Imported ingredients need to be ordered weeks in advance; domestic ingredients can be ordered daily. Do not mix these timelines in your planning.

**Currency hedging (for large operators).** If your monthly import spend exceeds 200,000 THB, consider forward contracts or multi-month pricing agreements with importers to lock in exchange rates. Your bank's treasury desk can advise on options.

**Buffer stock for imports.** Maintain 2-4 weeks of buffer stock for critical imported ingredients. Domestic items can be restocked in 1-3 days; imported items cannot. Running out of your signature imported cheese because of a customs delay is avoidable with proper buffer stock.

## Crisis Management: When Things Go Wrong

Every supply chain will experience disruptions. The question is whether you handle them reactively (scrambling) or proactively (executing a plan). Here is the framework.

### Immediate Response: Supplier Failure

When a supplier cannot deliver, your response depends on the severity and expected duration:

**Same-day fix (supplier is late or shorts your order):**
1. Contact the supplier immediately to understand the issue and get an estimated resolution time
2. Check your safety stock for affected ingredients
3. If safety stock is insufficient, call your secondary supplier for emergency delivery
4. If secondary is unavailable, send a team member to Makro or the nearest market for emergency purchasing
5. Evaluate menu impact -- if an ingredient is unavailable, 86 the affected dishes before service rather than serving substandard substitutions

**Multi-day disruption (supplier shut down, transportation blocked, natural disaster):**
1. Activate secondary suppliers for full volume shift
2. Implement menu contingency plan (seasonal swaps, local alternatives, reduced menu)
3. Communicate with your team about which items are affected and what the alternatives are
4. Adjust purchasing forecasts for the expected disruption period
5. Document costs and impacts for post-incident review

**Extended disruption (2+ weeks, major natural disaster, pandemic):**
1. Full supply chain reassessment -- which suppliers are still operational, which routes are open
2. Menu redesign around available ingredients
3. Explore new sourcing channels you have not used before (different provinces, different importers, different markets)
4. Negotiate with landlord and creditors if revenue impact is severe
5. Communicate transparently with customers about menu changes

### Natural Disaster Preparedness

Thailand's flood season (June-October) warrants specific preparation:

**Before monsoon season:**
- Increase safety stock of shelf-stable and frozen ingredients by 50%
- Confirm backup supplier availability and capacity
- Review insurance coverage for business interruption
- Document your menu contingency plan (which dishes can flex, which alternatives exist)
- Test your emergency communication chain (can you reach all suppliers within 1 hour?)

**During a flood event:**
- Monitor provincial flood reports daily (Thai Meteorological Department)
- Contact affected suppliers proactively -- do not wait for them to tell you there is a problem
- Shift orders to unaffected regions/suppliers immediately
- Adjust purchasing volumes based on expected price spikes and availability constraints

<Callout type="warning">
The biggest crisis management mistake I see is waiting. Restaurant operators hear about a flood in a growing province and assume it will not affect them because their supplier has not mentioned it yet. By the time the supplier confirms a problem, the backup options are already strained by every other restaurant making the same shift. Move early. A false alarm costs you nothing. A late response costs you money and menu items.
</Callout>

## Building SOPs for Receiving and Storage

Standard Operating Procedures transform supply chain management from something that depends on individual knowledge into a system that works regardless of who is on shift.

### Receiving SOP

Document this procedure, train every person who might receive deliveries, and post it at the receiving area:

1. **Verify the delivery against the purchase order.** Check every item and quantity against what was ordered. Do not sign anything until this is confirmed.
2. **Temperature check.** Probe at least 3 items per delivery (all categories -- protein, dairy, produce). Log temperatures on the receiving sheet. Reject anything above 5 degrees C (refrigerated) or above -18 degrees C (frozen).
3. **Visual and sensory inspection.** Check for damage, discoloration, off-odors, pest evidence, and packaging integrity. Apply the specific criteria for each product category (fresh fish: clear eyes, red gills, firm flesh; produce: no wilting, bruising, or mold; dry goods: intact packaging, no pest damage).
4. **Weight verification.** Weigh at least 2 items per delivery against invoice weights. Flag variances above 3%.
5. **Expiry date check.** All packaged goods must have at least 60% of their shelf life remaining at delivery.
6. **Store immediately.** Move temperature-sensitive items to appropriate cold storage within 15 minutes of acceptance. Label with received date.
7. **Document and file.** Complete the receiving log (date, supplier, items, quantities, temperatures, pass/fail, receiver name). File the delivery note. Report any rejections to the purchasing manager the same day.

### Storage SOP

1. **FIFO enforcement.** All items labeled with received date. New stock goes behind old stock. No exceptions.
2. **Temperature checks.** Record cold storage temperatures at opening and before closing. Post log on each unit.
3. **Organization.** Raw proteins bottom shelf, ready-to-eat items top shelf, produce middle shelves. No items stored on the floor. Minimum 15 cm clearance from walls for air circulation.
4. **Capacity limit.** Cold storage units must not exceed 80% capacity. If capacity is consistently exceeded, increase delivery frequency to reduce per-delivery quantities, or invest in additional storage.
5. **Weekly inventory count.** Physical count of all items in storage, compared against system or log records. Investigate and document any variances above 5%.
6. **Monthly deep clean.** Full clean and sanitize of all storage areas, including shelving, walls, and floors. Check for pest evidence. Inspect seals on cooler and freezer doors.

## Supply Chain for Delivery-Heavy Restaurants

Restaurants that generate 30%+ of revenue from delivery platforms face additional supply chain considerations that dine-in-focused operations do not.

### Packaging as a Supply Chain Category

Delivery packaging is a recurring cost that many operators underestimate. For a restaurant doing 100 deliveries per day at an average packaging cost of 15-25 THB per order (container, lid, bag, utensils, napkin, stickers), monthly packaging spend runs 45,000-75,000 THB. That is a significant supply chain category that deserves the same management rigor as food ingredients.

**Dual-source your packaging.** Run out of containers on a Friday night and your delivery revenue stops. Maintain a primary packaging supplier and a backup, just as you would for critical ingredients.

**Negotiate volume pricing.** Packaging suppliers offer significant discounts for committed volume. A restaurant ordering 3,000 containers per month at bulk pricing versus buying 500 at a time from a packaging shop saves 20-35% on packaging cost.

**Quality matters.** Cheap containers that leak, warp in the microwave, or make food soggy destroy your delivery rating faster than slow cooking times. Test packaging with your actual dishes before committing to bulk purchases.

### Last-Mile Logistics

For restaurants using their own delivery fleet (rather than relying solely on Grab/LINE MAN riders), last-mile logistics become a supply chain responsibility.

**Insulated delivery bags:** 300-800 THB each. Every delivery rider needs one. Replace them every 3-6 months as insulation degrades.

**Delivery radius:** Define your maximum delivery radius based on food quality preservation. Most Bangkok restaurants cap at 5-7 km because beyond that, food temperature and presentation degrade unacceptably.

**Rider training:** Delivery riders are the final link in your supply chain. If they handle food carelessly, stack containers incorrectly, or take inefficient routes that add 15 minutes of transit time, your cold chain fails at the very end. Train riders on proper handling, and inspect returned bags periodically.

## Putting It All Together: The Monthly Supply Chain Review

Supply chain management is not a one-time setup. It is an ongoing discipline that requires regular review and adjustment.

### Monthly Review Agenda (1-2 Hours)

1. **Food cost analysis.** Actual food cost percentage versus target. Which categories exceeded budget and why?
2. **Supplier performance review.** Delivery reliability, quality scores, and any open issues from the past month.
3. **Waste analysis.** Total waste by category (spoilage, prep, plate, over-production). Trend versus prior months.
4. **Inventory accuracy.** Physical count versus system records. Investigate significant variances.
5. **Price benchmarking.** Spot-check prices from your top 10 suppliers against at least one alternative source. Flag any items where you are paying more than 10% above market.
6. **Upcoming demand drivers.** Review the next month's event calendar, holiday schedule, and seasonal menu plans. Adjust purchasing forecasts accordingly.
7. **Supply chain risk check.** Any emerging risks (weather forecasts, supplier financial issues, regulatory changes) that need contingency planning?

This review should involve the head chef, the purchasing person, and the general manager (or owner). Supply chain decisions affect the kitchen, the finances, and the customer experience -- all three perspectives need to be in the room.

Your supply chain is the operating system of your restaurant. Every plate that reaches a customer, every food cost number you report, and every quality standard you maintain runs on the supply chain infrastructure you have built. Restaurants that manage their supply chain systematically -- with dual sourcing, proper cold chain, demand forecasting, and crisis plans -- operate with lower costs, more consistent quality, and the ability to absorb disruptions that would cripple less-prepared operators.
