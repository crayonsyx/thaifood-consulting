The question behind every restaurant dream in Thailand is the same: where does the money come from?

I have watched hundreds of people work through this question over 15 years. Some arrive with a full budget in savings and the freedom to build exactly what they want. Most do not. Most have a concept, a location in mind, a rough idea of costs, and a gap between what they have and what they need that ranges from a few hundred thousand baht to several million.

That gap is where this article lives. Thailand has more financing options for restaurant startups than most people realise -- bank loans, government-backed programmes, investor arrangements, equipment leasing, franchisor financing, and creative bootstrapping strategies that can stretch a limited budget further than you might think. But each option comes with conditions, costs, and risks that are specific to Thailand's financial and legal system, and picking the wrong one can be as damaging as being underfunded in the first place.

This is a practical guide to every financing path available, with real numbers, real requirements, and honest assessments of what actually works.

</Callout>

## Bank Loans for Restaurants in Thailand

Bank lending to restaurants in Thailand is possible but not easy. Banks view restaurants as high-risk borrowers because the failure rate is high (50% within 6 months, 80% within 3 years) and the assets are hard to liquidate. A bank that forecloses on a restaurant is left with used kitchen equipment worth 20-30% of its purchase price and a lease they cannot use.

That said, Thai banks do lend to the F&B sector, and the terms have improved in recent years as the government has pushed financial institutions to support SMEs.

### Major Bank Options

**Kasikorn Bank (KBank)**

KBank is arguably the most restaurant-friendly of the major Thai banks. Their SME lending programme has specific products for F&B businesses, and their underwriting team has more experience evaluating restaurant business plans than most competitors.

- **Loan size:** 500,000 - 20,000,000 THB
- **Interest rate:** 6.5-9.0% per annum (MLR-based, variable)
- **Loan term:** 3-7 years
- **Collateral required:** Yes, typically property or fixed deposits worth 80-100% of loan value
- **Processing time:** 3-6 weeks

**Siam Commercial Bank (SCB)**

SCB offers SME loans through their business banking division. Less specialised in F&B than KBank, but competitive on rates for borrowers with strong collateral.

- **Loan size:** 1,000,000 - 30,000,000 THB
- **Interest rate:** 6.0-8.5% per annum
- **Loan term:** 3-7 years
- **Collateral required:** Yes, property preferred
- **Processing time:** 4-8 weeks

**Bangkok Bank**

Bangkok Bank is the largest commercial bank in Thailand by assets. Their SME programme is broad rather than specialised, but they are willing to lend to restaurants with strong financials and collateral.

- **Loan size:** 500,000 - 50,000,000 THB
- **Interest rate:** 6.0-8.0% per annum
- **Loan term:** 3-10 years
- **Collateral required:** Yes, property or fixed deposits
- **Processing time:** 4-8 weeks

<Callout type="warning">
Interest rates listed above are indicative ranges for 2026. Thai bank lending rates are tied to the Minimum Lending Rate (MLR), which fluctuates with the Bank of Thailand's policy rate. As of Q1 2026, the policy rate is 2.25%. MLR across major banks ranges from 6.15% to 7.05%. Your actual rate will depend on your credit profile, collateral, and the bank's assessment of your business plan.
</Callout>

### What Banks Want to See

I have helped clients prepare loan applications for restaurants dozens of times. Here is what gets approved and what gets rejected.

**What gets you approved:**

1. **Collateral.** This is the single biggest factor. A Thai bank will lend against property (land or condo with title deed), fixed deposits, or government bonds. If you own property in Thailand valued at 2x+ the loan amount, your approval odds are high regardless of the business type. Without collateral, restaurant loan approval rates drop below 20%.

2. **A detailed business plan.** Not a vague concept document -- a plan with market analysis, a 3-year financial projection, cash flow forecasts, a break-even analysis, and a clear explanation of how you will repay the loan. Banks in Thailand are used to receiving terrible business plans from restaurant applicants. A good one stands out immediately.

3. **Personal financial track record.** Thai banks check your credit score (via the National Credit Bureau) and bank statement history. If you have maintained steady income, no bad debts, and a history of financial responsibility, that helps. Foreign applicants need at least 2 years of Thai bank account history.

4. **Industry experience.** A first-time restaurant owner with no F&B background is a red flag. If you have restaurant experience -- even as a manager or chef rather than an owner -- include it. If you are hiring an experienced operator, include their CV and their commitment to the project.

5. **Thai co-borrower or guarantor.** For foreign applicants, having a Thai co-borrower with assets and income dramatically improves approval odds. This is often the Thai business partner in a majority-Thai company structure.

**Common rejection reasons:**

- **No collateral.** The business plan can be perfect, but without assets backing the loan, most Thai banks will decline.
- **Insufficient equity contribution.** Banks want to see that you have skin in the game. If you are asking the bank to fund 80% or more of the project, you are probably getting rejected.
- **Unrealistic financial projections.** A business plan projecting 90% occupancy in month one and 40% net profit margins raises immediate red flags. Banks have seen thousands of restaurant projections. They know what realistic numbers look like.
- **No existing relationship with the bank.** Thai banking is relationship-driven. Walking into a branch cold with a loan application is less effective than working through a relationship manager you have banked with for 2+ years.
- **Cash-heavy personal finances.** If your tax returns show minimal income but you claim to have millions in savings, the bank's compliance department will flag this immediately.

<Callout type="tip">
Start your banking relationship 12-18 months before you need the loan. Open a business savings account, route your personal income through the bank, and build a transaction history. When you apply for the loan, the relationship manager already knows your financial behaviour and can advocate for your application internally. This single piece of planning improves approval odds more than any other factor.
</Callout>

## SME Development Bank (Government-Backed)

The SME Development Bank (SME D Bank) is a government-owned financial institution specifically designed to serve small and medium enterprises. It is more willing to lend to restaurants than commercial banks, and its terms are more favourable -- but the process is slower and more bureaucratic.

- **Loan size:** 200,000 - 15,000,000 THB
- **Interest rate:** 3.0-7.0% per annum (significantly below commercial bank rates)
- **Loan term:** Up to 10 years
- **Collateral requirements:** More flexible than commercial banks; can use equipment, business assets, or a Credit Guarantee Corporation (TCG) guarantee as partial collateral
- **Processing time:** 6-12 weeks (government pace)

The SME D Bank runs rotating programmes with promotional rates. In 2025-2026, their "SME Transformation Loan" offered rates starting at 3% for the first two years, rising to MLR-based rates thereafter. These programmes change every 6-12 months, so check their website or visit a branch for current offerings.

**The Thai Credit Guarantee Corporation (TCG)** is worth understanding in this context. TCG provides credit guarantees that substitute for collateral. If you do not have property to pledge, a TCG guarantee can cover 30-40% of the collateral requirement, making the remaining gap manageable. The guarantee fee is 1.5-2.0% of the guaranteed amount per year, added to your effective interest rate.

**Who should consider SME D Bank:**
- Thai nationals opening their first restaurant
- Existing restaurant operators expanding or renovating
- Operators who lack the collateral for a commercial bank loan
- Anyone willing to accept a slower approval process in exchange for significantly better rates

**Who should look elsewhere:**
- Foreigners (SME D Bank strongly prefers Thai nationals as primary borrowers)
- Applicants needing funds within 30 days (the process takes 6-12 weeks minimum)
- Large projects above 15M THB

## BOI Investment Promotion for Food Businesses

The Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) offers incentives for food industry investments that meet certain criteria. BOI promotion is not a loan -- it is a set of tax and operational incentives that reduce your effective cost of doing business.

**What BOI promotion offers:**
- Corporate income tax exemption for 3-8 years (depending on the activity and location)
- Import duty exemption on machinery and equipment
- Permission for foreign majority ownership (up to 100%, bypassing the Foreign Business Act)
- Permission to bring in foreign workers and experts
- Permission to own land for the promoted activity

**BOI-eligible food activities include:**
- Food manufacturing and processing (not just restaurants)
- "Creative economy" food businesses (emphasis on Thai culinary arts)
- Food technology and innovation centres

**The catch:** Most standard restaurants do not qualify for BOI promotion. The BOI targets manufacturing, processing, and technology rather than retail food service. However, there are paths:

- A restaurant with an attached food production facility (commissary, central kitchen, or food manufacturing) may qualify under the food processing category
- Restaurant groups operating 5+ outlets with a central kitchen may qualify as food manufacturing
- "Thai Select" premium restaurants that promote Thai cuisine internationally have received BOI support through the Ministry of Commerce programme

If your total investment exceeds 1 million THB and includes a production or manufacturing component, it is worth applying. The application costs nothing and the potential tax savings over 3-8 years can be hundreds of thousands or millions of baht.

## Angel Investors and F&B Venture Capital

The private investment scene for restaurants in Thailand is small but growing. Bangkok has a handful of angel investors and micro-VCs who specifically focus on food and beverage, and the deals they do typically follow one of two models.

### Model 1: Silent Investor (Most Common)

An individual investor puts up 50-80% of the startup capital in exchange for an equity stake and a share of profits. The operator runs the business. The investor receives monthly or quarterly distributions once the restaurant reaches profitability.

**Typical terms in Thailand:**
- **Investor contribution:** 1-10M THB
- **Equity stake:** 40-60% (proportional to capital contribution)
- **Management:** Investor is passive; operator has full operational control
- **Return structure:** Salary for operator + profit sharing after breakeven
- **Exit expectation:** 3-5 year hold with sale of the business or buyback by operator

**Where to find F&B angel investors in Thailand:**
- Bangkok's expat business networks (BCCT, Amcham, AustCham)
- Thai F&B industry events and conferences (Food & Hotel Thailand, Restaurant & Bar Thailand)
- Private wealth managers who serve high-net-worth Thai families with F&B interests
- Referrals from F&B lawyers and accountants who work with restaurant groups

**What investors want to see:**
- An experienced operator (not a first-timer with a dream)
- A proven concept, ideally one that has been tested via pop-ups or a smaller first location
- Realistic financial projections showing breakeven within 12-18 months
- A clear path to return on investment within 3-5 years
- A shareholder agreement drafted by a reputable law firm

### Model 2: Strategic / Operator-Investor (Less Common)

An experienced F&B professional invests capital and operational expertise. This is the "smart money" model -- the investor is not just writing a cheque but bringing industry connections, supplier relationships, and operational knowledge.

These deals are harder to find but more valuable. Thai restaurant groups like Minor International, Central Restaurants Group, and Dusit Thani's food division occasionally invest in independent concepts that align with their portfolio strategy. Smaller independent operators with multiple successful restaurants sometimes back promising new concepts in exchange for equity.

**Typical terms:**
- **Equity stake:** 30-50%
- **Contribution:** Capital + operational support (menu development, supplier introductions, staff training)
- **Management structure:** Joint, with clearly defined roles
- **Return structure:** Profit sharing with performance milestones

<Callout type="warning">
The most common disaster I see in restaurant investor arrangements is the handshake deal. Two people agree verbally on contributions and profit sharing, open the restaurant, and then discover they have completely different expectations about everything from salary levels to reinvestment decisions. A shareholders' agreement costs 15,000-40,000 THB from a Thai business lawyer. The disputes it prevents can cost millions. Get it in writing before a single baht changes hands.
</Callout>

## Thai Partner Financing Arrangements

Under the Foreign Business Act, most foreigners need Thai nationals holding at least 51% of the company's shares. This legal requirement has created a range of financing arrangements specific to the Thai market.

### The Common Patterns

**Pattern 1: Thai partner provides capital + shares, foreigner provides expertise**

The Thai partner invests the majority of capital (matching their majority shareholding) and the foreign partner contributes operational expertise, recipes, brand concept, and management. This is the cleanest structure legally and financially -- everyone's contribution matches their ownership stake.

In practice, finding a Thai partner who has both the capital and the willingness to invest in a restaurant run by someone else is difficult. It happens most often when the foreign partner has a strong track record and the Thai partner is a passive investor seeking diversification.

**Pattern 2: Foreigner provides most capital, Thai partner provides shares**

The foreigner funds 70-90% of the project but the Thai partner holds 51% of shares. The foreigner's excess capital contribution is structured as a shareholder loan from the foreigner to the company, repayable with interest before profit distributions.

This is the most common structure and also the one that creates the most disputes. The foreigner has the financial risk but minority legal ownership. Protecting the foreigner's position requires a well-drafted shareholders' agreement covering:

- Loan repayment priority (before any profit distribution)
- Board composition and voting rights (can differ from share ownership)
- Veto rights on major decisions (property transactions, additional borrowing, hiring/firing key staff)
- Exit mechanisms (buyout pricing formula, forced sale triggers)
- Non-compete clauses

**Pattern 3: Thai partner arranges local bank financing**

The Thai partner uses their personal assets (property, savings) as collateral to secure a bank loan for the restaurant, with the foreigner contributing cash equity and operational management. This can work well because Thai nationals have access to better bank loan terms and their collateral is more straightforward for Thai banks to evaluate.

The risk for the Thai partner is significant -- they are personally guaranteeing a business loan for a restaurant they may not be operating day-to-day. This arrangement requires deep trust and clear legal protections for both parties.

**No matter which pattern you use**, the shareholders' agreement is the document that protects everyone. I recommend the law firms at Siam Legal, Tilleke & Gibbins, or Baker McKenzie Thailand for restaurant-related corporate work. Expect to pay 30,000-80,000 THB for a comprehensive shareholders' agreement.

## Equipment Leasing vs. Buying

For restaurants that need to preserve cash, equipment leasing is an underutilised tool in Thailand. Instead of spending 500,000-1,500,000 THB upfront on kitchen equipment, you can lease the major items and spread the cost over 3-5 years.

**Leasing options in Thailand:**

- **Bangkok Bank Equipment Leasing:** Available for commercial kitchen equipment valued above 200,000 THB. Terms of 3-5 years, implicit interest rate of 5-8% per annum.
- **KBank Leasing:** Similar terms, with faster approval for existing KBank business account holders.
- **Vendor financing:** Some equipment suppliers (particularly for espresso machines, commercial ovens, and cold storage) offer in-house financing with 12-36 month payment plans. Interest rates vary widely -- 0% for 12 months is common as a promotional tool; longer terms may carry 6-10% implicit interest.
- **Operational leasing:** Available through specialised lessors for high-value items. Monthly payments, equipment returned at end of term or purchased at residual value.

For a restaurant with 3M THB in total capital where 1M goes to equipment, buying outright leaves only 2M for everything else -- rent, renovation, working capital. <Callout type="tip">
My recommendation for most restaurant startups in Thailand: buy small items outright (under 50,000 THB each) and lease or finance major equipment (commercial ovens, refrigeration, espresso machines, hood systems). This preserves cash for the working capital buffer that saves more restaurants than any equipment upgrade. Used equipment from restaurant liquidation sales is another option -- see our guide to [startup costs in Bangkok](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok) for tips on the secondary market.

## Personal Loans vs. Business Loans

Many restaurant owners in Thailand, particularly first-timers, fund their restaurant through personal borrowing rather than business loans. This is often the path of least resistance -- personal loans require less documentation, process faster, and do not require a business plan. But the trade-offs are significant.

**Personal loan options:**

- **Credit card cash advances:** 18-28% interest per annum. Fast access but cripplingly expensive. Never use this to fund a restaurant.
- **Personal loans from Thai banks:** 6-15% interest, 1-5 year terms, based on salary and credit score. Maximum amounts typically limited to 5-10x monthly income.
- **Home equity loans / refinancing:** 5-7% interest, 10-30 year terms, based on property value. This is the cheapest personal borrowing option.
- **Informal loans (family, friends):** 0% interest (usually) but relationship risk. The "loan" often becomes a "gift" or a "dispute" depending on the restaurant's success.

**Business loans:**

- **SME bank loans:** 3-9% interest, 3-10 year terms, requires business plan and collateral
- **BOI-promoted financing:** Below-market rates, restricted eligibility
- **Equipment financing:** 5-10% effective rate, secured against equipment

The key difference: personal loans put your personal assets at risk regardless of the business structure. If the restaurant fails and you borrowed against your condo, you lose the condo. A business loan to a Thai limited company, properly structured, limits the lender's recourse to the company's assets (with the notable exception of personal guarantees, which banks almost always require).

**My advice:** Use business loans when possible, even if the interest rate is slightly higher, because the liability structure is cleaner. If you must use personal borrowing, keep it to home equity (lowest rate) and limit the amount to what you can repay from your salary or other income if the restaurant fails entirely. Never fund a restaurant with credit card debt or unsecured personal loans at 15%+ interest -- the cost of capital alone will consume your margins.

## Crowdfunding and Alternative Finance

Crowdfunding is not yet a mainstream restaurant financing tool in Thailand, but it is growing. Two approaches are emerging:

**Reward-based crowdfunding.** Pre-selling dining credits, memberships, or experiences in exchange for upfront capital. A few Bangkok restaurants have raised 500,000-2,000,000 THB through platforms like Indiegogo or through their own pre-sale campaigns on social media. This works best for concepts with a strong existing following -- a popular pop-up chef launching a permanent restaurant, or an established operator opening a second location.

The advantage is that the "investors" receive dining credits rather than equity, so you retain full ownership. The disadvantage is that you are essentially pre-selling revenue, and if the restaurant opens to strong demand, those credits represent foregone full-price revenue.

**Equity crowdfunding.** Thailand's SEC regulated equity crowdfunding in 2019, and platforms like Sinwattana and LiVE are operational. Restaurants can raise up to 20 million THB from public investors through these platforms. However, the compliance costs (legal, accounting, disclosure requirements) are significant -- 200,000-500,000 THB in preparation costs -- making this practical only for larger projects.

**Revenue-based financing.** A newer model where a fintech lender advances capital in exchange for a fixed percentage of daily revenue until the advance (plus a fee) is repaid. This is appearing in Thailand through companies linked to GrabFood and LINE MAN merchant lending programmes. Terms are typically 5-15% of daily delivery revenue until 1.1-1.3x the advance is repaid (effectively 10-30% annualised cost of capital). Fast access, no collateral required, but expensive and only available to established restaurants with delivery revenue history.

- Repayment is structured as a higher ongoing royalty rate or a loan repaid through operations
- The franchisor retains ownership of the equipment until the loan is repaid

## Bootstrapping Strategies That Actually Work

Not everyone has collateral for a bank loan or connections to investors. Bootstrapping -- starting with limited capital and growing through revenue -- is a valid path, and it is how a surprising number of successful Bangkok restaurants began.

### Strategy 1: Start With a Cloud Kitchen

The lowest-capital entry point into the restaurant business. A station in a shared cloud kitchen costs 15,000-40,000 THB/month, and your total startup investment can be under 500,000 THB. Build your brand, refine your menu, develop a customer base on delivery platforms, and save the revenue to fund a physical location later.

I know three restaurants in Bangkok that started as cloud kitchens, built to 500,000+ THB/month in delivery revenue within 12 months, and used that revenue history and cash flow to secure bank financing for their first dine-in location. The cloud kitchen was not the final destination -- it was the proof of concept that unlocked traditional financing.

For a deeper dive, see: [How to Start a Cloud Kitchen in Bangkok](/blog/cloud-kitchen-bangkok)

### Strategy 2: Pop-Up to Permanent

Run a pop-up at markets, events, or inside existing bars and restaurants. Weekly pop-ups at Bangkok's night markets (Artbox, Jodd Fairs, Tha Phra Chan) or weekend markets cost 2,000-10,000 THB per day for a stall and require minimal equipment investment.

A successful pop-up generates three things that are more valuable than money: customer data, brand recognition, and operational proof. When you are ready to approach a bank or investor for a permanent location, you can show actual sales numbers, customer reviews, and media coverage rather than a hypothetical business plan.

### Strategy 3: Take Over an Existing Restaurant

Buying a failing or closing restaurant's assets and lease is dramatically cheaper than building from scratch. When a restaurant closes in Bangkok, the owner typically wants to recover as much of their build-out investment as possible, but they have limited leverage because the alternative is forfeiting the deposit and abandoning the equipment.

A typical "takeover" scenario: the departing tenant spent 3M THB on renovation and equipment. They are asking 1.5M THB for the lease transfer and all fixtures. You negotiate down to 800,000-1,000,000 THB because they have a lease expiry deadline. You are now in a renovated, equipped space for a third of the original build-out cost.

The risks are real -- the equipment may be worn, the layout may not suit your concept, and the location may have failed the previous operator for good reason. Inspect everything carefully and understand why the previous restaurant closed before committing.

### Strategy 4: Partner With a Chef

If you have capital but no operational experience, find a chef who has skills but no money. The capital partner funds the build-out; the chef partner contributes expertise, recipes, and labour. Equity is split based on contributions (often 60/40 or 70/30 capital-to-sweat equity).

This model works when both parties bring something the other genuinely needs and when the partnership terms are documented in a shareholders' agreement. It fails when the capital partner believes money alone entitles them to full control, or when the chef partner underestimates the financial realities of running a business.

### Strategy 5: Phase the Build-Out

Open with a smaller, simpler version of your concept and expand as revenue allows. A 40-seat restaurant does not need to be fully built out on day one. Open with 25 seats, a focused menu, and minimal decoration. Use the first 6-12 months of revenue to fund the second phase -- additional seating, the full bar, the upgraded lighting, the custom interior.

The benefit is obvious: less upfront capital required. The risk is that your opening impression may be underwhelming. Manage expectations through marketing -- position the initial phase as "intimate" rather than "incomplete" -- and have a clear timeline for the full build-out.

<Callout type="info">
Bootstrapping is not free -- it trades money for time and sometimes for image. A cloud kitchen startup takes 12-18 months to build enough revenue and credibility to transition to a physical space. A pop-up path might take 6-12 months of weekend markets before you have enough data and savings to commit to a lease. If speed matters, bootstrapping is the wrong approach. If capital preservation matters more, it is the right one.
</Callout>

## What a Good Business Plan Contains

Whether you are approaching a bank, an investor, or a wealthy uncle, the quality of your business plan directly determines whether you get funded and on what terms.

A restaurant business plan that gets taken seriously in Thailand includes:

1. **Executive summary** (1 page) -- concept, location, total capital required, how much you are seeking, projected breakeven timeline

2. **Market analysis** (2-3 pages) -- target customer demographics, competitive environment (who else is within 1km and what they charge), foot traffic data, demand validation (pop-up results, surveys, delivery platform data)

3. **Concept and menu** (2-3 pages) -- cuisine, pricing, sample menu with food costs, the story behind the concept, why this concept in this location

4. **Management team** (1 page) -- who is running the restaurant, their F&B experience, their role and commitment level

5. **Financial projections** (3-5 pages) -- three scenarios (conservative, base, optimistic) for the first 3 years. Include monthly cash flow for year 1, quarterly for years 2-3. Specify assumptions clearly: covers per day, average check, food cost percentage, rent, staffing costs.

6. **Capital structure and use of funds** (1 page) -- total budget, your equity contribution, amount sought from lender/investor, and exactly how every baht will be spent

7. **Risk analysis** (1 page) -- what could go wrong, how you plan to mitigate each risk

The single most important section is the financial projection, and the single most important quality is honesty. Projecting 80% seat occupancy in month one and 20% net profit margins marks you as someone who has never run a restaurant. Projecting 30% occupancy in month one, growing to 55% by month six, with a 5-8% net margin stabilising by year two marks you as someone who understands reality. Banks and experienced investors can tell the difference in 30 seconds.

## A Decision Framework: Which Financing Path Is Right for You?

The "best" financing option depends on four things: how much you need, how much collateral you have, how much control you want to retain, and how fast you need the money.

**You have property or assets in Thailand → Bank loan (KBank, SCB, or Bangkok Bank)**

This is the cheapest money available (6-9% interest) if you qualify. Use your property as collateral, prepare a solid business plan, and start the process 3-6 months before you need the funds. Add a TCG guarantee if your collateral does not fully cover the loan amount.

**You have limited assets but are a Thai national → SME Development Bank**

Lower rates (3-7%), more flexible collateral requirements, and government-backed guarantee options. Accept the slower timeline and more paperwork. This is the best deal available for qualifying Thai entrepreneurs.

**You have a strong concept but limited capital → Find an investor**

Offer equity in exchange for capital. Be realistic about the terms -- a 40-50% equity stake for an investor providing 60-70% of capital is fair. Invest heavily in a proper shareholders' agreement.

**You have minimal capital and no collateral → Bootstrap**

Start with a cloud kitchen, pop-up, or food truck. Build revenue and a track record. Use that track record to secure financing for a physical restaurant in 12-18 months. This is the slowest path but it preserves optionality and minimises financial risk.

**You are opening a franchise → Explore franchisor financing first**

If the franchisor offers financing, evaluate it against bank loan terms. Franchisor financing is often more expensive (implicit rates of 8-12%) but comes with operational support that independent financing does not.

**You need money fast → Equipment leasing + personal savings**

Lease the big equipment (frees up 500,000-1,000,000 THB of cash), fund the rest from savings, and keep the total project size within what you can afford without external debt. This limits your concept size but eliminates lender dependency.

Whatever financing path you choose, the foundation is the same: a clear concept, honest financial projections, and a realistic understanding of what it costs to operate a restaurant in Thailand.
