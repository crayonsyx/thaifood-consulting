A few years ago I was brought in to consult on a restaurant in Ari that was "doing great." The owner told me revenue was strong, customers were happy, and the dining room was full on weekends. He could not understand why there was never enough cash in the bank account to pay suppliers on time.

I asked to see his P&L. He did not have one. His accountant filed taxes every quarter, but the owner had never seen a profit and loss statement. He was running a business with 18 employees and 900,000 THB/month in revenue based entirely on gut feeling and the balance in his bank account.

Thirty minutes with his data told the whole story. Food cost was at 41% (target: 30-35%), labour was at 38% (target: 25-32%), and his prime cost -- food plus labour -- was 79% of revenue. That left 21% to cover rent, utilities, marketing, accounting, and everything else. His rent alone was 12% of revenue. He was losing money every month and did not know it.

This is not unusual. The majority of independent restaurant owners I work with in Thailand -- Thai and foreign alike -- do not understand their financial statements. They see them once a year when the auditor produces them, glance at the bottom-line number, and file them away. The statements exist for compliance, not for management.

That is a waste. Your financial statements are the most powerful management tools you have. They tell you exactly where your money goes, which parts of your operation are healthy, and where you are bleeding cash. But only if you know how to read them.

This guide covers the three financial statements every restaurant produces, with specific attention to Thai market benchmarks, the ratios that matter most for restaurants, and a complete P&L walkthrough for a mid-range Bangkok restaurant.

<Callout type="info">
All figures in this article are in Thai Baht (THB). At the time of writing, 1 USD is approximately 35.5 THB. The benchmark percentages I cite are based on my experience consulting with 80+ restaurants across Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Phuket, and Pattaya. Your specific numbers will vary based on concept, location, and scale, but these benchmarks provide a useful starting point for comparison.
</Callout>

## The Three Financial Statements

Every Thai limited company must produce three financial statements as part of its annual audit:

1. **Profit and Loss Statement (P&L)** -- also called the income statement. Shows revenue, costs, and profit over a period (monthly, quarterly, or annually).
2. **Balance Sheet** -- also called the statement of financial position. Shows what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the residual equity at a specific point in time.
3. **Cash Flow Statement** -- shows how cash moved in and out of the business during the period.

For day-to-day restaurant management, the P&L is the statement you will use most. The balance sheet and cash flow statement matter more for financing decisions, lease negotiations, and year-end planning. I will spend the most time on the P&L, but cover all three.

## The Restaurant P&L: Line by Line

A restaurant P&L has a specific structure that differs from a generic business. The categories and sequence matter because they reveal the health of different parts of your operation.

Here is the standard structure I use with clients:

### Revenue

**Gross Revenue:** Total sales before any deductions. For a restaurant, break this into:
- Food revenue (dine-in)
- Beverage revenue (dine-in)
- Delivery revenue
- Service charge income
- Other income (catering, events, merchandise)

**Net Revenue:** Gross revenue minus discounts, promotions, void/cancellation, and staff meals charged through the POS. This is the number everything else is calculated against.

Why the breakdown matters: food and beverage have different cost structures. Dine-in and delivery have different margin profiles. Service charge income has its own distribution rules. If you lump everything into a single "revenue" line, you lose the ability to diagnose problems in specific channels.

<Callout type="tip">
A common mistake is recording delivery revenue net of platform commissions. Do not do this. Record gross delivery revenue (the full amount the customer pays for food) and record the platform commission (30-35%) as a separate operating expense. This gives you visibility into your true delivery volume and the true cost of that channel. If you net the commission out of revenue, your food cost percentage against delivery will look artificially high and you cannot compare dine-in and delivery performance accurately.
</Callout>

### Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

COGS is the direct cost of the food and beverages you sell. Nothing else belongs here -- not labour, not rent, not packaging. Just the raw ingredients and beverages.

**Food COGS:** The cost of all food ingredients consumed during the period. Calculated as: Beginning Food Inventory + Food Purchases - Ending Food Inventory = Food COGS.

**Beverage COGS:** Same calculation for beverages. Includes all alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, mixers, garnishes, and ice.

**Gross Profit = Net Revenue - Total COGS**

**Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit / Net Revenue x 100**

If your gross profit margin is below the low end of your segment's range, your food cost or beverage cost is too high. Common causes: over-portioning, theft, waste, supplier price increases that were not reflected in menu prices, or a menu mix that is skewed toward low-margin items. Menu engineering can address all of these -- I covered that process in detail in my [menu engineering guide](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand).

### Labour Costs

Labour is the second largest expense in a restaurant, after COGS. Include everything employee-related:

- Salaries and wages (kitchen, FOH, management, back office)
- Social security contributions (employer share -- 5% of salary, capped at 750 THB/month per employee)
- Workmen's Compensation Fund
- Service charge distributions (the portion the restaurant passes to staff)
- Overtime payments
- Staff meals
- Training costs
- Recruitment costs
- Uniforms

**Labour Cost Percentage = Total Labour Costs / Net Revenue x 100**

Thai benchmarks by segment:

- **Quick service / fast casual:** 20-25%
- **Casual dining:** 25-32%
- **Fine dining:** 30-38%
- **Cafe:** 22-28%
- **Cloud kitchen:** 18-25%

Labour cost above 35% in a casual dining restaurant is a red flag. Either you are overstaffed, overpaying relative to your revenue level, or your revenue is too low for your current team size. The fix is usually not cutting staff -- it is increasing revenue per labour hour through better scheduling, cross-training, or driving more covers during existing shifts.

For a deeper dive into Thai labour costs, including 2026 salary benchmarks by position, see the [restaurant staffing guide](/blog/restaurant-staffing-thailand).

### Prime Cost

**Prime Cost = COGS + Labour Costs**

This is the single most important number in restaurant finance. Prime cost represents the two largest variable costs in your operation, and it is the number that determines whether your restaurant can be profitable.

**Target prime cost: 55-65% of net revenue.**

If your prime cost is above 65%, profitability becomes extremely difficult because the remaining 35% of revenue must cover rent (typically 8-15%), utilities (3-5%), marketing (2-5%), accounting (1-2%), insurance, depreciation, maintenance, and your profit. There is simply not enough margin left.

If your prime cost is below 55%, you are either in a high-margin segment (bar, cafe) or you may be underinvesting in food quality or underpaying staff, both of which have consequences.

<Callout type="warning">
Prime cost is the number I look at first when I review any restaurant's financials. If prime cost is under 65%, there is usually a path to profitability even if other costs need adjustment. If prime cost is above 70%, the business model itself needs to change -- no amount of operational tweaking on the other line items will make up the gap.
</Callout>

### Occupancy Costs

Everything related to your physical space:

- Rent
- Common area maintenance (CAM) fees
- Property insurance
- Utilities (electricity, water, gas)

**Occupancy Cost Ratio = Total Occupancy Costs / Net Revenue x 100**

**Target: 12-18% of net revenue**

Rent alone should be 8-12% of revenue. If your rent exceeds 15% of revenue, you are either in a location that is too expensive for your concept, or your revenue is too low for the space you are occupying. This is one of the numbers I check first in a [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) -- if the projected rent-to-revenue ratio exceeds 12%, we need to either find a cheaper location or redesign the concept to generate higher revenue per square meter.

Utilities are the surprise line item. Electricity in Bangkok for a restaurant with a full kitchen, walk-in cooler, and air conditioning easily runs 25,000-50,000 THB/month. Water is typically 3,000-8,000 THB/month. Gas (if piped) adds 5,000-15,000 THB/month. Together, utilities add 3-6% of revenue on top of rent.

### Operating Expenses

Everything else that keeps the restaurant running:

- Marketing and advertising (2-5% of revenue)
- Delivery platform commissions (this is where Grab/LINE MAN's 30-35% cut lives -- and it is a large number for delivery-heavy restaurants)
- Repairs and maintenance (1-2% of revenue)
- Supplies and consumables (cleaning, paper goods, toiletries: 1-2%)
- Accounting and legal fees (1-2%)
- Technology and POS subscriptions (0.5-1%)
- Pest control
- Bank charges and payment processing fees (1.5-3% of card/QR transactions)
- Insurance (beyond property)
- Miscellaneous

### EBITDA

**EBITDA = Revenue - COGS - Labour - Occupancy - Operating Expenses**

EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation) is the closest thing to a measure of your restaurant's operational profitability before non-cash charges and financing decisions.

**Target EBITDA: 10-18% of net revenue for a healthy restaurant**

- Below 8%: The restaurant is barely viable and has no cushion for unexpected costs
- 8-12%: Thin but survivable if well-managed
- 12-18%: Healthy -- the sweet spot for most casual dining operations
- Above 18%: Excellent, typically seen in high-margin concepts (bars, cafes) or very efficient operations

### Depreciation and Amortisation

Non-cash charges that spread the cost of capital assets over their useful life:

- Kitchen equipment: 5-year depreciation (20% per year)
- Renovation and fit-out: 5-20 years depending on the type (leasehold improvements often depreciated over the lease term)
- Furniture and fixtures: 5 years
- Computer equipment and POS: 3-5 years
- Pre-opening costs: Amortised over 5 years (or the lease term, whichever is shorter)

Depreciation does not consume cash, but it does reduce your taxable profit and it represents the real economic consumption of your assets. A restaurant with 5 million THB in equipment and renovation depreciating over 5 years has 83,333 THB/month in depreciation, which is real economic cost even though no cash leaves the bank account that month.

### Net Profit

**Net Profit = EBITDA - Depreciation - Interest - Tax**

For corporate income tax purposes, Thai SME rates apply (0% on the first 300,000 THB of net profit, 15% on 300,001-3,000,000 THB, and 20% above 3,000,000 THB). See the [accounting basics guide](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand) for full details on CIT.

**Target net profit: 5-12% of net revenue**

Most restaurants in Thailand that are profitable at all run 5-10% net profit margins. A restaurant doing 1 million THB/month in revenue and generating 8% net profit is putting 80,000 THB/month to the bottom line -- 960,000 THB/year. On a 5 million THB initial investment, that is a 19% annual return, which is respectable for any business. But it takes most restaurants 6-12 months to reach this level.

## Balance Sheet Basics for Restaurants

The balance sheet is a snapshot of your financial position at a specific moment. For restaurants, the key items are:

### Assets

**Current Assets:**
- Cash and bank balances
- Accounts receivable (credit card settlements pending, delivery platform payments pending, corporate event receivables)
- Inventory (food, beverages, supplies)
- Prepaid expenses (advance rent, insurance prepayments)

**Non-Current Assets:**
- Kitchen equipment (net of depreciation)
- Renovation and leasehold improvements (net of amortisation)
- Furniture and fixtures (net of depreciation)
- Security deposits (rental deposit, utility deposits)

### Liabilities

**Current Liabilities:**
- Accounts payable (supplier invoices due)
- Accrued expenses (utilities, salaries earned but not yet paid)
- VAT payable
- Withholding tax payable
- Social security payable
- Unearned revenue (gift vouchers, event deposits)
- Current portion of long-term loans

**Non-Current Liabilities:**
- Long-term loans
- Severance pay provision (the accrued liability for statutory severance under Thai labour law)
- Deferred rent (if your lease has escalating rent but you are recognising expense evenly)

### Equity

- Registered capital (paid-up)
- Retained earnings (or accumulated losses)

**The key balance sheet ratio for restaurants:**

**Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities**

A current ratio above 1.0 means you can cover your short-term obligations. Below 1.0 means you may struggle to pay suppliers and staff. Most healthy restaurants maintain a current ratio between 1.2 and 2.0. Below 1.0 is a cash crisis warning.

<Callout type="info">
Many restaurant owners ignore the balance sheet because it feels abstract compared to the P&L. But your balance sheet tells you things the P&L cannot: how much working capital you have, whether your cash position is improving or deteriorating, whether your inventory is growing faster than your revenue (a sign of waste or over-ordering), and how much you owe versus how much you own. Review it quarterly at minimum.
</Callout>

## Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement explains why your bank balance changed between two dates. It reconciles the profit on your P&L (which uses accrual accounting) with the actual cash in your bank.

Three sections:

**Operating Activities:** Cash generated or consumed by running the restaurant. Starts with net profit and adjusts for non-cash items (depreciation) and working capital changes (inventory, payables, receivables). This should be positive for a healthy restaurant.

**Investing Activities:** Cash spent on or received from capital assets. Buying new equipment, renovating, or selling old equipment. This is typically negative (cash outflow) for a growing or maintaining restaurant.

**Financing Activities:** Cash from or to owners and lenders. Loan proceeds, loan repayments, capital injections, and dividend payments.

**The key insight for restaurant owners:** You can be profitable on the P&L and still run out of cash. This happens when:

- You are carrying too much inventory (cash tied up in stock you have not sold)
- Delivery platforms pay you bi-weekly but you pay suppliers weekly (timing mismatch)
- You made a large equipment purchase that did not show up on the P&L (it is capitalised, not expensed)
- You are paying down a loan (principal repayment does not appear on the P&L)

The cash flow statement solves the "where did my money go?" problem that the P&L alone cannot answer.

## Key Financial Ratios for Thai Restaurants

Beyond the percentages discussed in the P&L section, here are the ratios I track for every restaurant client:

### Food Cost Percentage

**Formula:** Food COGS / Food Revenue x 100

**Target:** 28-35% for most restaurants

Track this weekly, not just monthly. A weekly food cost above 35% for two consecutive weeks means something has changed: supplier prices increased, portions are creeping up, waste is growing, or theft is occurring. Weekly tracking catches problems before they become expensive.

### Beverage Cost Percentage

**Formula:** Beverage COGS / Beverage Revenue x 100

**Target:** 18-25% for spirits-heavy programmes, 25-30% for wine-heavy programmes, 20-30% for beer

Beverage margins are your profit accelerator. A restaurant running 30% food cost and 22% beverage cost with a 40/60 food-to-beverage revenue split has a blended COGS of 25.2%, which is outstanding. Pushing your beverage attachment rate (the percentage of tables that order drinks) from 50% to 70% can improve your bottom line by 3-5 percentage points of revenue without serving a single additional food item.

### Prime Cost Ratio

**Formula:** (Food COGS + Beverage COGS + Total Labour) / Net Revenue x 100

**Target:** 55-65%

Already discussed above, but worth emphasising: this is the metric. If you only track one number, track this one.

### Occupancy Cost Ratio

**Formula:** (Rent + CAM + Utilities + Property Insurance) / Net Revenue x 100

**Target:** 12-18%

### RevPASH (Revenue Per Available Seat Hour)

**Formula:** Total Revenue / (Number of Seats x Operating Hours)

This is the restaurant equivalent of a hotel's RevPAR. It measures how efficiently you are converting your physical capacity into revenue.

**Example:** A 60-seat restaurant open 10 hours per day generates 35,000 THB in revenue. RevPASH = 35,000 / (60 x 10) = 58.33 THB per seat-hour.

Why this matters: RevPASH tells you whether you have a traffic problem, a check-size problem, or a table-turn problem. Compare RevPASH across lunch and dinner to see which daypart is underperforming. Compare across days of the week to identify your weakest days.

**Thai benchmarks for RevPASH:**
- Casual Thai dining: 40-70 THB/seat-hour
- Western casual dining: 50-90 THB/seat-hour
- Fine dining: 100-200 THB/seat-hour
- Cafe: 30-50 THB/seat-hour

### Break-Even Revenue

**Formula:** Fixed Costs / (1 - Variable Cost Percentage)

Where variable costs include COGS and variable labour (part-time staff, overtime), and fixed costs include rent, salaried staff, utilities, insurance, accounting, and other fixed obligations.

**Simplified example:** If your monthly fixed costs are 400,000 THB and your variable costs are 40% of revenue, your break-even revenue is: 400,000 / (1 - 0.40) = 666,667 THB/month. You need roughly 22,000 THB/day in revenue to break even.

<Callout type="tip">
Calculate your break-even revenue before you open and update it quarterly. Knowing your break-even number gives you a daily target that everyone in the restaurant can understand. "We need 25,000 THB in revenue today to cover our costs" is more motivating and actionable than "our EBITDA target is 12%." Post the daily revenue target in the staff area and update the actual number every day.
</Callout>

### Table Turnover Rate

**Formula:** Total Covers Served / Total Number of Seats (per meal period)

A 60-seat restaurant that serves 90 lunch covers has a 1.5x lunch turn. If the same restaurant serves 120 dinner covers, that is a 2.0x dinner turn.

**Thai casual dining benchmarks:**
- Lunch: 1.0-1.5 turns
- Dinner: 1.5-2.5 turns
- Total daily: 2.5-4.0 turns

Low table turnover means either not enough customers (marketing problem), slow service (operational problem), or tables lingering too long after finishing (floor management problem). Each requires a different solution.

### Average Check Per Cover

**Formula:** Total Revenue / Total Covers

Track this separately for food and beverage, and separately for dine-in and delivery. Trends in average check tell you about pricing power, upselling effectiveness, and menu mix.

## How to Spot Problems Early

Your monthly P&L is a diagnostic tool. Here are the patterns that signal trouble before they become crises:

### Food Cost Creeping Up

If food cost percentage rises by more than 2 points over 2-3 months without a menu change, investigate immediately. Common causes:

- **Supplier price increases** that were not reflected in menu prices (check your purchase invoices against your menu costing spreadsheet)
- **Portion creep** (cooks gradually increasing portions -- re-weigh everything against your spec)
- **Waste** (check your waste log -- if you do not have one, start one)
- **Theft** (unfortunately common -- tighten receiving procedures, require dual sign-off on deliveries)
- **Menu mix shift** (customers are ordering more low-margin items -- check your POS sales mix report)

### Labour Cost Spiking

Labour cost as a percentage of revenue can spike for two reasons: labour costs increased (new hires, overtime, wage increases) or revenue decreased (same labour, less sales). Check which factor is driving the change before making decisions.

Cutting staff when the problem is actually declining revenue makes both problems worse -- your service quality drops, which drives revenue down further.

### Cash Shrinking While P&L Looks Fine

If your P&L shows profit but your bank balance is declining, check:
- **Accounts receivable growing** (delivery platforms or corporate clients paying slower)
- **Inventory building** (over-ordering, especially for promotions)
- **Capital expenditures** (equipment purchases that are capitalised, not expensed)
- **Loan repayments** (principal is not a P&L expense but it consumes cash)
- **Owner withdrawals** (drawings or informal personal expenses through the company)

### Revenue Declining

Revenue decline in a restaurant follows a pattern. First, weekday lunch drops. Then weekday dinner softens. Weekend dinner holds longest because habits and social commitments maintain it. If your weekend dinner revenue starts declining, you have a serious problem.

Track revenue by daypart (lunch vs. dinner) and by day of week. The patterns tell you where to focus. A restaurant losing weekday lunch traffic needs a lunch promotion or menu adjustment. A restaurant losing dinner across all days likely has a reputation or concept problem.

## Break-Even Analysis: How Much Revenue Do You Need?

Let me walk through a break-even calculation for Khao restaurant using real numbers from the P&L above.

**Fixed costs (costs that do not change much with revenue):**
- Rent: 95,000 THB
- CAM: 8,000 THB
- Insurance: 2,500 THB
- Base salaries (salaried staff): 236,000 THB
- Social security: 11,250 THB
- Accounting: 12,000 THB
- POS/tech: 4,500 THB
- Pest control: 3,500 THB
- Minimum marketing: 15,000 THB
- **Total fixed costs: 387,750 THB/month**

**Variable cost percentage (costs that scale with revenue):**
- Food and beverage COGS: 28.2%
- Variable labour (part-time, overtime, SC distribution): 11.3%
- Delivery commissions: 4.1% (of total revenue)
- Bank/processing fees: 0.6%
- Consumables: 0.7%
- **Total variable cost: 44.9% of revenue**

**Break-even revenue = 387,750 / (1 - 0.449) = 703,721 THB/month**

That is approximately 23,457 THB/day. With a 65-seat restaurant open for lunch and dinner, this requires roughly 50-55 covers per day at an average check of 440 THB (including service charge and beverage).

At current revenue of 1,035,000 THB, the restaurant has a **margin of safety of 32%** -- meaning revenue could drop by about a third before hitting break-even. That is a comfortable buffer, but not luxurious. An economic downturn, a new competitor opening nearby, or a few months of construction on the soi could eat into that margin quickly.

Knowing your break-even number also helps with pricing decisions. If you are considering a 10% discount promotion to drive traffic, you need to generate enough incremental volume to offset the lost margin. At 10% lower prices, your variable cost percentage shifts, and you need to serve significantly more covers just to stay at the same profit level. The math often reveals that broad discounting is a bad deal.

## Using Financial Statements for Decision-Making

Financial statements are not just reports. They are decision-making frameworks. Here are common decisions and which numbers inform them:

**Should I raise prices?** Look at your food cost percentage relative to benchmarks, your average check versus competitors, and your customer count trend. If food cost is above 35% and customer counts are stable, a price increase is justified. If customer counts are declining, the problem is probably not price.

**Should I cut staff?** Look at labour cost percentage and revenue per labour hour. If labour is above 35% and you have idle staff during slow periods, adjusting schedules (not cutting headcount) is usually the first move. Cutting a server saves 14,000 THB/month but may cost you 50,000 THB/month in lost revenue from slower service.

**Should I invest in renovation?** Look at your EBITDA margin and cash flow. A renovation that costs 500,000 THB needs to generate at least 10,000 THB/month in incremental revenue or savings to pay for itself within 5 years (the depreciation period). Can you tie the renovation to a measurable outcome -- more seats, faster kitchen throughput, higher perceived value enabling a price increase?

**Should I add delivery?** Model the delivery P&L separately. Delivery revenue minus food cost (typically 35-40% for delivery), minus platform commission (30-35%), minus packaging (4-6%), minus incremental labour for delivery prep -- what is left? In my experience, most restaurants that add delivery generate 10-20% net margin on delivery revenue, which is better than nothing but much lower than dine-in. The strategic value may still justify it (brand awareness, incremental volume using existing kitchen capacity), but go in with realistic expectations.

**Should I open a second location?** This is the biggest financial decision most restaurant owners face. The answer lives in your balance sheet and cash flow statement. Do you have enough working capital for a second location without endangering the first? Is the first location generating stable, growing cash flow? Have you systematised your operations enough to replicate them? A second location roughly doubles your financial risk while only incrementally increasing your operational capacity because management attention is split.

## Putting It All Together

The restaurants that succeed long-term in Thailand share a common discipline: they respect their numbers. Not as an afterthought, not once a year for the auditor -- as a weekly and monthly management practice.

Here is the routine I recommend:

**Daily:** Check POS revenue report. Compare to target. Reconcile cash.

**Weekly:** Review food cost estimate (purchases vs. revenue), customer counts, average check. Flag any metric that is more than 10% off target.

**Monthly:** Full P&L review with your accountant. Compare to prior month and to budget. Discuss variances. Identify two to three actionable items for the coming month.

**Quarterly:** Balance sheet and cash flow review. Update break-even analysis. Review pricing against current food costs. Assess staffing levels against revenue trend.

**Annually:** Full financial review with your accountant and auditor. Set budget for the coming year. Review tax position and plan for any strategic decisions (expansion, renovation, menu overhaul).

The discipline of regular financial review compounds over time. Month by month, you get better at spotting patterns, asking the right questions, and making decisions with data instead of instinct. Instinct matters in food. In finance, numbers are what keep you open.
