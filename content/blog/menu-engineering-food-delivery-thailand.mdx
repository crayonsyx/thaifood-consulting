Your dine-in menu does not work for delivery. I say this after watching dozens of Bangkok restaurants copy-paste their full 60-item menus onto GrabFood and LINE MAN, then wonder why their delivery margins are underwater and their ratings hover at 4.2 stars. The problems are structural: dishes that look beautiful on a plate arrive as a muddled mess in a plastic container, crispy items steam themselves soggy inside a sealed bag during a 30-minute ride, and a menu designed for a browsing dine-in customer overwhelms someone scrolling on a 6-inch phone screen while standing in their kitchen.

Delivery menu optimisation is a different discipline from dine-in menu engineering. The economics are different (30-35% platform commission eats your margin before you plate a single dish), the customer psychology is different (speed, convenience, and visual appeal on a small screen replace ambiance and service), and the operational constraints are different (packaging, travel time, and platform algorithms replace table turns and server upselling).

This article covers how to build a delivery-specific menu for Thai restaurants operating on Grab, LINE MAN, or both -- from dish selection and pricing to photography, platform mechanics, and the metrics that actually matter for delivery profitability.

## Why Your Dine-In Menu Fails on Delivery

Before building a delivery menu, it helps to understand why your current one is not working. The failure modes are predictable and consistent across every restaurant I have audited for delivery performance.

### Food Quality Degrades in Transit

A dish that tastes perfect at the pass has 25-45 minutes of travel ahead of it before the customer takes the first bite. That transit time is spent inside a sealed container inside a sealed bag, often on the back of a motorbike in 35-degree Bangkok heat. The consequences are predictable.

**Crispy items lose their crunch.** Fried chicken, spring rolls, fish cakes, and crispy pork belly trap steam inside sealed containers. By the time they arrive, they are soft. This is the most common reason for negative delivery reviews in Thai restaurants -- customers expecting crunch and getting sogginess.

**Noodle soups become overcooked mush.** If you deliver tom yum goong or boat noodles with the noodles sitting in hot broth for 30+ minutes, the noodles absorb the liquid, swell, and disintegrate. The customer opens a container of starchy sludge instead of a clean soup with distinct noodle texture.

**Salads wilt.** Som tum dressed and tossed at the restaurant is limp and pooling with liquid by the time it arrives. The papaya releases water, the tomatoes break down, and the lime juice cooks the raw ingredients in a slow acid bath during transit.

**Sauces separate and leak.** A beautifully plated curry that pools around rice on a plate becomes a container where the coconut cream has separated, the oil sits on top, and the rice at the bottom has absorbed all the liquid and turned into a dense block.

### Menu Size Overwhelms the Platform Customer

A dine-in menu with 50+ items works in a restaurant because the customer has time, a physical menu to browse, and a server to guide them. On GrabFood, that same 50-item menu requires the customer to scroll through 8-10 screens on their phone, process dozens of options, and make a decision within the 2-3 minutes they are willing to spend before moving to the next restaurant.

Platform data from Grab (shared at an industry event in 2025) showed that restaurants with 15-25 items on their delivery menu have 18% higher conversion rates (views to orders) than restaurants with 40+ items. Fewer options, faster decisions, more orders.

### Pricing Is Wrong

If your delivery prices are identical to your dine-in prices, you are giving away 30-35% of your revenue to the platform and your margins are collapsing. A pad kra pao at 180 THB dine-in with a 25% food cost gives you 135 THB gross profit. The same dish at 180 THB on GrabFood with a 33% commission gives you 120.6 THB in net revenue minus 45 THB food cost = 75.6 THB gross profit. That is a 44% drop in gross profit per dish compared to dine-in.

Most operators do not realise how severe this is until they run the numbers. And by then, they have been running delivery at near-breakeven for months, subsidising GrabFood's margin with their own.

<Callout type="warning">
Calculate your delivery food cost against **net revenue** (after platform commission), not gross. A dish that costs 50 THB to make and sells for 200 THB on GrabFood looks like 25% food cost. But after 33% commission (66 THB), your net revenue is 134 THB, and your actual food cost is 50/134 = 37.3%. That is the number that matters. I have seen operators celebrate a "healthy 25% food cost" on delivery while actually running 37% against net -- which leaves almost nothing after labour, packaging, and overhead. For the full math, see [Food Cost Control for Bangkok Restaurants](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).
</Callout>

## Building a Delivery-Specific Menu

A delivery menu is not a subset of your dine-in menu with a few items removed. It is a separate menu engineered for different constraints. Start from scratch.

### The 12-20 Item Sweet Spot

Platform data and my own client results consistently show that 12-20 items is the optimal delivery menu size. Fewer than 12 items feels too limited (customers worry they will not find something they want), while more than 20 creates scroll fatigue and decision paralysis.

Within those 12-20 items, the architecture should be:

- **4-6 main dishes** (your Stars and high-margin items that travel well)
- **2-3 rice/noodle combos** (one-dish meals that need no additional ordering)
- **2-3 set meals/bundles** (main + rice + drink, or 2-person sharing sets)
- **2-3 sides and appetisers** (add-on items that increase order value)
- **1-2 desserts** (only items that travel well -- nothing that melts or wilts)
- **1-2 beverages** (bottled or sealed Thai iced tea)

Every item on this list must pass two tests: (1) does it survive 30-45 minutes of transit in a sealed container? and (2) does it generate acceptable margin after platform commission?

### Which Thai Dishes Travel Well

**Curries:** Green curry, red curry, massaman, panang -- all travel well. Curry in a sealed container stays hot, the coconut cream base keeps the protein moist, and the flavour actually develops slightly during transit as the spices continue to infuse. Curries are the backbone of a Thai delivery menu. Pack rice separately (always), include a small container of fresh basil or chilli on the side, and the customer reheats in 90 seconds if it has cooled.

**Stir-fries:** Pad kra pao, pad prik king, pad pak ruam mit -- these travel reasonably well because the sauce coats everything and prevents drying. The main risk is overcooking during transit (residual heat continues cooking the vegetables), so slightly undercook stir-fries destined for delivery. Pull the pad kra pao 30 seconds before you would for dine-in and it will be perfect on arrival.

**Fried rice:** Excellent delivery item. Fried rice is a self-contained one-dish meal that holds heat well, does not separate or leak, and reheats easily. Pineapple fried rice in particular works because the pineapple adds moisture that prevents the rice from drying out. However, skip the carved-pineapple presentation for delivery -- it does not survive the bag and adds packaging complexity.

**Pad Thai and dry noodles:** Good if packaged correctly. The risk with pad thai is that the noodles stick together as they cool. Solution: toss with a thin film of oil before packing, and include the lime and peanuts in a separate container so the customer adds them fresh. This also makes the unboxing feel more interactive.

**Grilled items (satay, moo ping):** Solid delivery items because the marinade keeps them moist and they are finger food that does not need cutlery. Pack sauce separately.

### Which Thai Dishes Do Not Travel Well

**Crispy-fried items as standalone dishes.** Fried chicken, crispy spring rolls, tod mun pla -- all lose their crunch within 10-15 minutes in a sealed container. If you must include fried items, package them separately from any sauce or wet item, and use a vented container (small holes in the lid) to let steam escape. Even then, expect 4-star instead of 5-star texture on arrival.

**Noodle soups (tom yum, boat noodles, kuay teow).** The noodles overcook in transit. The only way to deliver noodle soups successfully is to pack the broth, noodles, and toppings in separate containers and let the customer assemble. This works, but it requires 3 containers per order (increasing your packaging cost by 15-25 THB) and clear assembly instructions. Some restaurants do this well; most do not bother and get complaints.

**Som tum and fresh salads.** The acid in the dressing wilts the papaya and vegetables. If you want to offer som tum on delivery, pack the dressing separately and instruct the customer to toss it themselves. But honestly, the extra packaging and assembly friction make this a low-priority delivery item unless som tum is a signature of your brand.

**Dishes with runny eggs.** A beautifully runny fried egg on pad kra pao is a dine-in pleasure. On delivery, that egg breaks open in transit, the yolk runs everywhere, and the photo the customer takes before eating (and they will take a photo) shows a yellow-smeared mess instead of an Instagram-ready dish.

**Anything requiring precise plating.** If the visual appeal depends on careful arrangement -- layered salads, composed plates, artistic sauce drizzles -- it will not survive a delivery bag. Delivery food needs to look good despite being shaken and tilted for 30 minutes.

<Callout type="tip">
Test every item on your delivery menu by ordering it yourself through the platform. Not from your kitchen to your table -- actually order through GrabFood, wait for the rider, and open it at home. The 30-minute, real-world experience will tell you instantly which items survive and which do not. I do this with every client, and the results always surprise the chef.
</Callout>

## Pricing for Delivery: Absorbing vs. Passing On Commission

The 30-35% platform commission is the defining economic challenge of food delivery. There are three approaches to handling it, and the right one depends on your situation.

### Approach 1: Mark Up Delivery Prices by 15-25%

This is the most common and usually the correct approach. A pad kra pao at 180 THB dine-in becomes 210-220 THB on GrabFood. A green curry at 250 THB becomes 290-310 THB.

**Why it works:** Most delivery customers do not compare platform prices to dine-in prices. They compare your platform prices to other restaurants on the platform. If every Thai restaurant on GrabFood charges 200-220 THB for pad kra pao, your 210 THB is competitive. The customer never sees your dine-in menu at 180 THB.

**The margin math at 20% markup:**
- Dine-in price: 180 THB. Food cost: 45 THB. Gross profit: 135 THB (75% margin).
- Delivery price: 216 THB. Commission at 33%: 71 THB. Net revenue: 145 THB. Food cost: 45 THB. Gross profit: 100 THB (69% margin on net).

The margin drops from 75% to 69%, which is significant but sustainable. Without the markup, it drops to 56% -- the difference between profitable and problematic.

### Approach 2: Create Delivery-Only Items at Higher Price Points

Instead of marking up existing items, create delivery-exclusive dishes or bundles that are priced to absorb the commission while offering perceived value that justifies the premium.

**Example -- "Delivery Lunch Set":**
- Pad kra pao + jasmine rice + Thai iced tea + mini spring rolls
- Food cost: 58 THB
- Delivery price: 249 THB
- Commission at 33%: 82 THB
- Net revenue: 167 THB
- Gross profit: 109 THB (65% margin on net)
- Perceived value: "A complete meal for 249 THB including a drink and appetiser -- that is a good deal"

The bundle costs you marginally more than the pad kra pao alone (the tea costs 10 THB, the spring rolls 12 THB) but sells at a significantly higher price point, improving your absolute gross profit per order while the customer feels they are getting value.

### Approach 3: Absorb the Commission (Rarely Recommended)

Some restaurants keep delivery and dine-in prices identical, absorbing the full commission hit. This only makes sense if: (1) your dine-in margins are already very high (80%+ on most items), (2) delivery is a small percentage of revenue (under 15%), and (3) you actively advertise "same price as dine-in" as a competitive differentiator.

For most Thai restaurants, this approach is not viable because casual dining margins are not fat enough to absorb 30-35% without going negative or breakeven.

## Photography for Delivery Platforms

On GrabFood and LINE MAN, the photo is your menu, your decor, your service, and your plating -- all compressed into a 300x300 pixel square that a customer glances at for 1-2 seconds. Items with professional photos get 25-30% more orders than items without. This is not an opinion; it is platform data that Grab has shared publicly.

### Technical Requirements

**Square format (1:1 ratio).** Both Grab and LINE MAN display food photos in square crops. If you shoot in landscape or portrait, the platform will auto-crop the sides or top/bottom, cutting off the food. Always compose in square.

**Bright, even lighting.** Natural window light or a ring light produces the most appetising food photos. Avoid direct flash (creates harsh shadows and oily-looking surfaces) and dim ambient light (food looks dark and unappetising on a phone screen). The goal is a photo that looks bright and inviting at thumbnail size.

**Overhead or 45-degree angle.** Overhead shots work best for dishes in bowls (curries, fried rice, noodles). A 45-degree angle works for dishes with height (stacked satay, a burger, a salad). Avoid straight-on shots for flat dishes -- they look like circles of brown.

**Container, not plate.** Photograph your delivery items in the actual delivery containers you use, not on beautiful ceramic plates. The customer will receive the food in a container; if the photo shows a plated restaurant dish, the delivery reality will feel like a downgrade, and that gap drives negative reviews. A well-composed photo in a clean, well-designed container sets accurate expectations and actually builds trust.

### What Makes a Good Delivery Photo

**Generous-looking portions.** The container should look full. A half-filled container looks stingy on screen. If your standard portion does not fill the container, either use a smaller container or arrange the food to cover the surface area.

**Visible main ingredients.** A green curry photo should show the chicken, the Thai eggplant, the basil, and the coconut cream -- not a solid green surface. Arrange so the hero ingredients are visible on top. This takes 20 seconds of plating effort per photo but significantly increases appeal.

**Colour contrast.** Thai food has natural colour advantages -- the green of basil, the red of chilli, the orange of curry, the white of rice. Use this. A photo of pad kra pao should show the green basil and red chillies, not a brown mass of minced pork. Garnish for the photo even if you do not garnish for standard delivery orders.

**No clutter.** The photo should show the food and nothing else. No napkins, no utensils, no background clutter. On a phone screen, every non-food element competes for the viewer's attention and distracts from the dish.

### Photo Investment

A professional food photographer charges 1,500-3,000 THB per dish in Bangkok, including styling. For a 15-item delivery menu, that is 22,500-45,000 THB. The photo set lasts 12-18 months (until you update the menu), so the amortised cost is 1,250-3,750 THB/month. Given that professional photos increase orders by 25-30%, the ROI is typically positive within the first 2-3 weeks.

If budget is tight, a good smartphone camera (iPhone 15+ or Samsung S24+), a ring light (500-800 THB), and a clean white surface produces acceptable results. Watch 2-3 YouTube tutorials on food photography composition before shooting. The difference between a mediocre phone photo and a good one is technique, not equipment.

## Menu Structure on Grab and LINE MAN

How you organise your delivery menu on the platform directly affects what customers order and how much they spend.

### Categories

Both Grab and LINE MAN let you create categories (sections) within your restaurant menu. The optimal structure for a Thai delivery menu:

1. **Recommended / Best Sellers** (3-5 items) -- your highest-margin, best-reviewed items. This category appears first and captures the attention of customers who do not scroll past it.
2. **Set Meals** (2-4 bundles) -- complete meals at a bundled price. These increase average order value and simplify the decision for the customer.
3. **Main Dishes** (5-8 items) -- your core offering. Curries, stir-fries, fried rice.
4. **Sides and Extras** (3-5 items) -- appetisers, extra rice, add-on proteins.
5. **Drinks and Desserts** (2-4 items) -- sealed beverages and delivery-friendly desserts.

**Do not replicate your dine-in category structure.** A dine-in menu might have "Appetisers, Soups, Curries, Stir-Fries, Rice, Noodles, Seafood, Desserts" -- that is 8 categories with 5-8 items each. On a phone screen, this is overwhelming. Consolidate into 4-5 categories max.

### Item Descriptions

Delivery platform descriptions serve a different purpose than dine-in menu descriptions. On a platform, the description needs to:

**Manage expectations.** "Green curry with chicken thigh, Thai eggplant, bamboo shoots, and sweet basil in coconut cream. Rice packed separately." The customer knows exactly what they are getting, including how it is packed. No surprises.

**Highlight unique selling points.** "House-made curry paste, free-range chicken" signals quality. "Spice level: medium (adjustable)" signals customisation. These are the details that differentiate you from the 50 other restaurants offering green curry within the customer's delivery radius.

**Include practical information.** "Serves 1-2 people," "Contains peanuts," "Comes with jasmine rice." Delivery customers cannot ask the server questions. The description is your only communication channel.

**Keep it under 150 characters for the preview.** Platforms truncate descriptions in the browse view. Front-load the most important information in the first sentence.

### Modifiers and Add-Ons

Modifiers (customisation options) are an underused revenue tool on delivery platforms.

**Protein upgrades:** "Choose protein: Chicken (included), Pork (+0 THB), Prawns (+40 THB), Crispy Pork Belly (+30 THB)." The upgrade price exceeds the ingredient cost difference, adding pure margin per order.

**Spice level:** "Spice: Mild / Medium / Spicy / Extra Spicy." No cost to you, but it reduces complaints from customers who receive food that is too hot or not hot enough. Fewer complaints = fewer refunds = better margins.

**Add-ons:** "Add fried egg (+20 THB)," "Add extra rice (+20 THB)," "Add Thai iced tea (+49 THB)." Each add-on increases order value at high margin. A customer who adds an egg and a drink to their pad kra pao has increased their order by 69 THB, of which approximately 55 THB is gross profit.

**Platform data shows that restaurants with 3+ modifiers per item have 12-18% higher average order values** than those with no modifiers. The setup takes 30 minutes in the platform dashboard and pays for itself on day one.

<Callout type="info">
Both GrabFood and LINE MAN allow you to set different prices for modifiers than your dine-in add-on prices. Use this. A fried egg that is "+15 THB" on your dine-in menu can be "+20 THB" on delivery -- the customer does not see your dine-in menu and will not compare. This is another small lever for recovering commission margin.
</Callout>

## Bundle and Set Meal Engineering for Delivery

Set meals are even more effective on delivery than dine-in, for three reasons.

First, the delivery customer wants convenience. They are ordering food because they do not want to cook or go out. A set meal that eliminates decision-making ("here is a complete meal, just add to cart") aligns with that mindset perfectly.

Second, bundles increase average order value. A customer who might have ordered a single dish at 200 THB will order a set meal at 299 THB because it includes a drink and side they would not have ordered individually. That extra 99 THB at 65-70% margin on net (after commission) adds meaningful profit per order.

Third, bundles improve your platform ranking. GrabFood's algorithm favours restaurants with higher average order values because Grab's commission is a percentage of the order -- a higher order value means more revenue for Grab per delivery. Restaurants with higher AOV tend to get better placement in search results and recommendations.

### How to Build Delivery Bundles

**"For 1" lunch set:** Main dish + rice + drink. Price at 199-249 THB. Food cost target: 55-65 THB. This is your volume play -- it captures the solo office worker ordering lunch.

**"For 2" dinner set:** 2 main dishes + rice + 2 drinks + 1 side. Price at 449-549 THB. Food cost target: 110-140 THB. Targets couples and roommates ordering dinner. The per-person value is better than ordering individually, while your margin per order is higher in absolute terms.

**"For 4" family set:** 3-4 main dishes + rice + 4 drinks + 2 sides + dessert. Price at 799-999 THB. Food cost target: 200-260 THB. This is your highest AOV option and it appeals to families and group orders. Even after commission, a 999 THB order at 26% effective food cost generates significant per-order profit.

**Name your bundles clearly.** "Office Lunch Set," "Couple's Dinner," "Family Feast" -- the name tells the customer who it is for and when to order it. Generic names like "Set A" and "Set B" require the customer to read the details and compare, which adds friction.

### Delivery-Exclusive Items

Creating items that only exist on your delivery menu serves two purposes. It prevents direct price comparison with your dine-in menu, and it lets you engineer items specifically for delivery constraints.

**Examples of delivery-exclusive items:**

- **"Curry Rice Bowl":** Green curry pre-poured over rice in a single container, topped with fried egg and crispy shallots. This would never appear on a dine-in menu (curry and rice are always served separately in Thai dining), but for delivery it eliminates a container, simplifies packaging, and creates a one-dish meal format that the customer eats directly from the container.

- **"Thai Street Combo":** Pad kra pao rice + chicken satay (3 pieces) + mini som tum (packed dry, dressing on side). Combines items that would be ordered from different sections on a dine-in menu into a delivery-specific combination.

- **"Build Your Own Box":** Choose 2 dishes from a list of 6 + rice + drink at a fixed price. Gives the customer customisation (good for platform engagement metrics) while you control which 6 dishes are available (all high-margin, all delivery-friendly).

## Ghost Kitchen Menu Strategy

A [cloud kitchen or ghost kitchen](/blog/cloud-kitchen-bangkok) is a delivery-only operation with no dine-in component. For ghost kitchens, the delivery menu is not a secondary channel -- it is the entire business. The margin math is even more critical because there is no dine-in revenue to cross-subsidise delivery losses.

### Ghost Kitchen Menu Principles

**Fewer items, more bundles.** A ghost kitchen should run 10-15 items maximum. Every item must earn its place through margin contribution. There are no loss leaders or brand-building menu items -- everything needs to be profitable after commission.

**Multiple brands from one kitchen.** Many successful Bangkok ghost kitchens operate 2-3 virtual brands from the same kitchen. "Thai Kitchen" serves curries and stir-fries, "Bangkok Noodle House" serves pad thai and noodle combos, "Sabai Bento" serves set meal boxes. Same ingredients, same kitchen, same staff -- but three separate storefronts on GrabFood, each with a focused menu of 8-12 items. This triples your platform presence without tripling your costs.

**Optimise for speed.** Ghost kitchens live and die by preparation time, because rider wait time affects your platform metrics (Grab tracks average preparation time and penalises slow restaurants with lower visibility). A ghost kitchen menu should be prep-heavy and fire-fast: curry pastes made in advance, proteins portioned and marinated, sauces batched, rice always ready. Target under 12 minutes from order receipt to handoff.

**Packaging is a core cost, not an afterthought.** Dine-in restaurants think of packaging as a minor delivery add-on. Ghost kitchens must budget packaging as a per-order cost that sits alongside food cost. A typical Thai delivery order uses 2-3 containers, a bag, utensils, and condiment packets -- totalling 15-30 THB per order. On a 200 THB order with 33% commission, your available revenue is 134 THB, food cost is 50 THB, packaging is 20 THB -- leaving only 64 THB for rent, labour, utilities, and profit. Every baht of packaging cost matters.

## Cross-Platform Menu Management

Most Bangkok restaurants are on both GrabFood and LINE MAN, and managing two menus across two platforms creates operational complexity that can erode your margins if not handled systematically.

### Should You Run the Same Menu on Both Platforms?

Yes, with minor tactical differences. Your core menu (items, descriptions, photos) should be identical to simplify kitchen operations. But pricing and promotions can differ.

**Pricing:** GrabFood and LINE MAN commission rates may differ by 1-3 percentage points depending on your contract. If LINE MAN charges 30% and GrabFood charges 33%, you can price slightly lower on LINE MAN -- or maintain the same prices and pocket the margin difference.

**Promotions:** Both platforms run promotional campaigns (free delivery, percentage discounts, flash sales) at different times. When a platform offers to subsidise a promotion (e.g., Grab pays for free delivery during a campaign), participate -- the subsidy is coming from the platform, not your margin. When the platform asks you to fund the promotion, calculate the net impact before agreeing.

**Exclusive items:** Consider a "LINE MAN exclusive" or "Grab exclusive" item to differentiate. This can help you win promotional placement on that platform (platforms reward exclusivity with better visibility), without cannibalising your other channel.

### Operational Consistency

The biggest risk of cross-platform delivery is inconsistency. The same pad kra pao should taste the same whether ordered on Grab or LINE MAN, because the customer does not care which platform they used -- they care about the food.

**Standard operating procedures for delivery orders:**

- Same recipe card for every dish regardless of platform
- Same container types and sizes
- Same garnishing and packing sequence
- Same quality check before handoff (is the lid sealed? is the bag complete? are condiments included?)
- Orders from both platforms flow into the same kitchen workflow

If you have a kitchen display system (KDS) or POS that integrates both platforms, use it. If not, a simple system of printing orders on colour-coded paper (green for Grab, yellow for LINE MAN) prevents mix-ups.

## Tracking Delivery-Specific Metrics

Dine-in metrics (covers, average check, table turns) do not apply to delivery. The metrics that matter for a delivery menu are different.

### Completion Rate

The percentage of orders that are successfully delivered without cancellation or refund. Target: 95%+.

A low completion rate means orders are being cancelled (usually because the kitchen cannot fulfil them -- ingredient stockout, item unavailable) or refunded (food quality issues, missing items, wrong order). Each cancelled or refunded order costs you the food cost with zero revenue, plus a platform penalty to your ranking. Track daily and investigate any order that drops below completion.

### Average Order Value (AOV)

The average revenue per delivery order. Target depends on your menu, but for a casual Thai delivery menu, 200-300 THB is typical for solo orders and 400-600 THB for group orders.

**How to increase AOV:**

- Bundle meals (sets include more items at a bundled price)
- Add modifier upsells (extra protein, fried egg, drinks)
- Minimum order incentives ("Free spring rolls on orders over 400 THB")
- Strategic pricing that encourages adding one more item ("Only 49 THB to add Thai iced tea")

A 50 THB increase in AOV across 50 daily orders is 75,000 THB/month in additional revenue. At 65% margin on net, that is approximately 49,000 THB/month in gross profit from a single metric improvement.

### Ratings and Reviews

Platform ratings directly affect your visibility. A restaurant at 4.7 stars gets significantly more impressions (views on the platform) than one at 4.3 stars. GrabFood's algorithm weighs ratings heavily in search ranking.

**The most common causes of negative delivery reviews:**

1. **Missing items** (server forgot to pack a drink or side). Fix: Use a packing checklist for every order.
2. **Cold food.** Fix: Insulated bags for riders (some restaurants provide their own), timing orders so food is packed just before rider arrival.
3. **Spills.** Fix: Secure lids, tape seals on liquid items, upright packing.
4. **Wrong order.** Fix: Print the order details on a sticker attached to the bag. Both kitchen and rider can verify.
5. **Soggy crispy items.** Fix: Vent containers for fried items, or remove crispy items from the delivery menu.

<Callout type="tip">
Respond to every negative review on the platform within 24 hours. A polite, specific response ("We are sorry the spring rolls were not crispy. We have switched to vented containers and retrained our packing team") demonstrates that you care about quality. Platform data shows that restaurants that respond to reviews maintain 0.2-0.3 higher average ratings than those that ignore reviews, because dissatisfied customers are less likely to leave harsh reviews when they see the restaurant is responsive.
</Callout>

### Reorder Rate

The percentage of customers who order from you a second time within 30 days. Target: 25-35%.

A high reorder rate means your food is good, your delivery is reliable, and your pricing is perceived as fair. A low reorder rate (below 15%) means something is broken -- food quality, packaging, or value perception. Track this metric monthly and compare it against your platform average (both Grab and LINE MAN provide restaurant analytics dashboards with this data).

## Optimising for Platform Algorithms

Both GrabFood and LINE MAN use algorithms to decide which restaurants appear first in search results and on the home screen. The specific algorithm details are proprietary, but the ranking signals are well understood from platform communications and experimentation.

### Known Ranking Factors

**Preparation time.** Restaurants that consistently fulfil orders quickly rank higher. Target: accept orders within 30 seconds (auto-accept is best), have food ready within 10-15 minutes.

**Completion rate.** Fewer cancellations and refunds = higher ranking. Target: 95%+.

**Rating.** Higher average rating = higher ranking. Target: 4.5+.

**Order volume.** Restaurants with more orders rank higher (momentum effect). This creates a chicken-and-egg problem for new restaurants -- you need volume to rank well, but you need ranking to get volume. Break the cycle with launch promotions (see below).

**Photo coverage.** Restaurants where every item has a photo rank higher than those with missing photos. Grab has stated this explicitly in merchant communications.

**Menu completeness.** Having descriptions, modifiers, and accurate operating hours all contribute to a "completeness score" that affects ranking.

**Promotion participation.** When a platform runs a campaign and you opt in, your restaurant gets boosted visibility during the campaign period. This is the platform incentivising your participation.

### Launch Strategy for New Delivery Menus

If you are launching delivery for the first time or relaunching with a revamped menu, the first 30 days are disproportionately important because both platforms give new restaurants a visibility boost (the "new restaurant" badge and algorithmic push).

**Week 1-2: Maximum quality, minimum friction.** Run a reduced menu (8-10 items only, your absolute best sellers that travel perfectly). Focus on zero cancellations, fast prep times, and perfect packing. Every order is a rating opportunity, and early ratings weight heavily.

**Week 2-4: Expand and optimise.** Add the remaining items, launch bundles, and start running platform promotions (GrabFood often offers subsidised promotions for new restaurants). Monitor ratings daily and address any negative feedback immediately.

**Month 2+: Steady state.** Optimise based on data. Which items sell? Which items get complaints? Which bundles convert? Prune low-performing items, double down on winners, and rotate seasonal specials quarterly.

## Seasonal Delivery Promotions

Delivery volume in Bangkok fluctuates with weather, holidays, and events. Smart operators plan promotions around these patterns.

**Rainy season (June-October):** Delivery volume peaks when it rains because customers do not want to go out. This is the time to run food-cost-efficient promotions that capture increased demand -- "Rainy Day Combo" at a bundled price, free extra rice on orders over 300 THB (cost: 3 THB, perceived value: 20 THB). Do not discount aggressively during peak demand -- volume will come naturally.

**Songkran (April 13-15) and long weekends:** People stay home or travel. Delivery from restaurants near residential areas spikes while restaurants near offices see a dip. Adjust your delivery hours and staffing accordingly.

**Month-end paydays (25th-1st):** Order volume and AOV increase around payday. This is when customers are willing to order premium items and larger sets. Feature your highest-margin bundles prominently during these windows.

**Platform-specific events:** GrabFood runs "GrabFood Festival" promotions and LINE MAN runs similar campaigns periodically. These events include subsidised promotions and boosted visibility. Opt into every platform-subsidised campaign -- you are getting marketing spend you did not pay for.

<Callout type="warning">
Never run a promotion that takes your margin below breakeven, even temporarily. "Lose money to gain customers" does not work in food delivery because delivery customers are notoriously disloyal -- they follow discounts, not brands. A customer acquired through a 40% discount will leave the moment a competitor offers a bigger discount. Build your delivery business on consistent quality and reasonable value, not on a discount race to the bottom. For more on [delivery platform strategy in Thailand](/blog/delivery-platform-strategy-thailand), the principle is sustainable margin over short-term volume.
</Callout>

## Packaging Cost as Part of Food Cost

Most restaurants track food cost and ignore packaging cost. For delivery, this is a mistake because packaging runs 15-30 THB per order -- which is 7-15% of your net revenue on a 200 THB order. Packaging is not overhead; it is a per-unit variable cost that should be tracked alongside food cost.

### How to Calculate True Delivery Cost

**True delivery COGS = food cost + packaging cost**

For a pad kra pao delivery order:
- Food cost: 45 THB
- Rice container: 3 THB
- Main container: 4 THB
- Sauce cup: 1.5 THB
- Utensils: 2 THB
- Bag: 4 THB
- **Total COGS: 59.5 THB**
- Delivery price: 216 THB
- Commission (33%): 71 THB
- Net revenue: 145 THB
- **True margin: (145 - 59.5) / 145 = 59%**

Without counting packaging, you thought your margin was 69%. With packaging, it is 59%. That 10 percentage point gap is real money -- on 50 delivery orders per day, the difference is approximately 25,000-35,000 THB/month in margin you thought you had but did not.

### Reducing Packaging Cost

**Buy in bulk.** Packaging costs drop 20-30% when you order 1,000+ units instead of 100. Even a small restaurant should order containers by the case (500-1,000 units) rather than by the pack (50-100 units).

**Standardise container sizes.** Using 2-3 container sizes for all dishes (a 650ml for mains, a 400ml for rice, a 100ml for sauces) lets you buy at volume and reduces storage complexity. A kitchen with 8 different container types generates confusion and waste.

**Skip the branded packaging unless it pays for itself.** A branded bag costs 5-8 THB versus 2-3 THB for a generic one. The brand value of a logo on a delivery bag is minimal -- the customer has already ordered from you, and they are going to throw the bag away. Use that 3-5 THB savings per order on food quality instead.

**Include packaging in your menu pricing model.** When costing a delivery item, add the packaging cost to the food cost before calculating your target selling price. If your target food+packaging cost is 35% of net revenue, price accordingly.

## Putting It All Together: A Delivery Menu Engineering Checklist

Here is a 10-step checklist for engineering a profitable delivery menu.

1. **Audit your current delivery menu for travel fitness.** Order every item through the platform yourself. Remove items that arrive in unacceptable condition.

2. **Cut to 12-20 items.** Keep only high-margin items that travel well. Remove seafood, crispy-fried standalone items, noodle soups (or engineer separate packing), and anything requiring precise plating.

3. **Mark up delivery prices by 15-25%.** Calculate your target margin against net revenue (after commission), not gross.

4. **Build 2-4 set meals.** "For 1," "For 2," and "For 4" bundles that increase AOV while delivering complete-meal convenience.

5. **Invest in photography.** Professional or high-quality smartphone photos for every item. Square format, bright lighting, shot in actual delivery containers.

6. **Structure your platform menu.** 4-5 categories, "Best Sellers" first, set meals second, main dishes third. Descriptions under 150 characters for the preview, with practical info (serves, allergens, packing details).

7. **Add modifiers to every item.** Protein upgrades, spice level, add-ons. Each modifier is a margin-positive upsell opportunity.

8. **Budget packaging as COGS.** Calculate true delivery cost (food + packaging) and price to achieve your target margin on net revenue.

9. **Track delivery-specific metrics weekly.** Completion rate, AOV, ratings, reorder rate. Investigate any metric that drops below target.

10. **Respond to every review.** Positive reviews get a thank-you. Negative reviews get a specific, solution-oriented response within 24 hours.
