Nobody opens a restaurant planning to sell it. You open because you love food, because you believe in your concept, because you want to build something. Selling is what happens when life, business, or opportunity pulls you in a different direction.

I have been involved in about two dozen restaurant sales and acquisitions across Thailand -- advising sellers on valuation and preparation, advising buyers on due diligence, and occasionally watching deals collapse because neither side understood what they were getting into. The restaurant resale market in Thailand is messy, opaque, and full of emotional decision-making. But when a sale is handled well, both parties walk away satisfied.

This guide covers everything from recognizing when it is time to sell to closing the deal and walking away. If you own a restaurant in Thailand and have thought -- even briefly -- about selling, this is the information you need before you talk to a broker or a buyer.

<Callout type="info">
This article focuses on selling a restaurant as an ongoing business (as opposed to closing a restaurant and liquidating assets). Asset liquidation is simpler but typically yields 15-30% of the value of a going-concern sale. If your restaurant is profitable and has a transferable lease, selling it as a business is almost always the better financial outcome.
</Callout>

## When to Sell Your Restaurant

The best time to sell is when you do not have to. That sounds paradoxical, but restaurants sell for the highest price when they are profitable, well-run, and growing. A restaurant being sold out of desperation -- the owner is burned out, the lease is expiring, the financials are deteriorating -- sells at a steep discount because the buyer knows they have leverage.

Here are the common triggers I see with clients:

**Burnout.** The most frequent reason, and the one most owners are reluctant to admit. Running a restaurant in Thailand is physically and emotionally exhausting. After 3-5 years, the 14-hour days, the staff turnover, the constant problem-solving -- it wears down even the most passionate operators. If you have reached the point where you dread going to work, selling while the business still has momentum is smarter than running it into the ground.

**A better opportunity.** You want to open a different concept, move to a different city, or pursue something outside food and beverage entirely. Selling a profitable restaurant to fund the next venture is a rational move. The capital locked up in your restaurant is not working for you if your heart is somewhere else.

**Partnership problems.** Many restaurants in Thailand are co-owned, often by a mix of Thai and foreign partners. When partnerships deteriorate, the business suffers. A clean sale -- either to one partner buying out the other, or to a third party -- is often better than years of passive-aggressive conflict. For the legal complexities of ownership structures, see my [foreigner restaurant ownership guide](/blog/foreigner-opening-restaurant-thailand).

**Retirement or lifestyle change.** Some owners have built exactly what they wanted, run it successfully for years, and are ready to do something else. This is the healthiest reason to sell, and these owners typically get the best price because they are not in a rush.

**The concept has peaked.** Every restaurant concept has a lifecycle. If you can feel the plateau -- reviews are still good but growth has stopped, the market is shifting, newer competitors are capturing attention -- selling at the peak yields a better price than selling during the decline. The buyer can refresh the concept or bring new energy; you capture the value you built.

**Lease expiration approaching.** If your lease has 18-24 months remaining and you are not certain you want to renew (or the landlord is proposing a significant rent increase), selling now gives the buyer enough remaining lease term to justify the purchase. A restaurant with 6 months left on the lease is nearly unsellable as a going concern.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not sell in a panic. If you are having a bad month or a temporary staff crisis, that is not a reason to sell -- it is a reason to fix the problem. Selling should be a strategic decision made with a clear head, not an emotional reaction to a rough quarter. Give yourself 30 days after the initial "I want to sell" impulse before taking any action. If you still want to sell after 30 days of calm reflection, proceed.
</Callout>

## Restaurant Valuation Methods in Thailand

The question every seller asks first: what is my restaurant worth? The honest answer is that it is worth whatever a willing buyer will pay. But there are frameworks for arriving at a defensible number.

### Method 1: Multiple of EBITDA

This is the most common valuation method for profitable restaurants globally, and it works in Thailand too.

**Formula:** Restaurant Value = Annual EBITDA x Multiple

EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation) represents your restaurant's operating cash flow. See my [financial statements guide](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand) for a detailed explanation of how to calculate it.

**Typical multiples for Thai restaurants:**

**Example:** A casual Thai restaurant in Bangkok with 1.5 million THB in annual EBITDA (about 125,000 THB/month) at a 2.5x multiple is valued at 3.75 million THB. At 3.5x, the same restaurant is valued at 5.25 million THB. That is a wide range, and where you land within it depends on the quality of your lease, the strength of your brand, the transferability of the operation, and how motivated the buyer is.

**What affects the multiple:**
- **Remaining lease term.** A restaurant with 5+ years remaining on a favorable lease gets a higher multiple. Less than 2 years remaining drops the multiple by 0.5-1.0x.
- **Lease transferability.** If the lease allows assignment to a new tenant (or the landlord is cooperative), the multiple holds. If transferring the lease requires landlord approval and the landlord is difficult, the risk discount is significant.
- **Owner dependence.** If the restaurant runs smoothly without the owner's daily involvement, the multiple is higher. If the owner is the head chef or the primary customer draw, the multiple drops because the buyer is getting a business that deteriorates the moment the owner leaves.
- **Revenue trend.** Flat or growing revenue supports a higher multiple. Declining revenue drops it.
- **Documented systems.** SOPs, recipe books, training manuals, and organized financials signal a professional operation and command a premium.

### Method 2: Asset-Based Valuation

This method values the restaurant based on the tangible assets a buyer is purchasing.

**Formula:** Restaurant Value = Fair Market Value of Equipment + Inventory + Lease Deposit Recovery + Intangible Assets (brand, customer list, recipes)

This method typically yields a lower value than EBITDA multiples for profitable restaurants, which is why sellers prefer the EBITDA method. But it sets a floor -- your restaurant should not sell for less than the replacement cost of its assets.

**Typical asset values for a Bangkok casual dining restaurant:**
- Kitchen equipment (used): 40-60% of original purchase price, depending on age and condition
- Furniture and fixtures: 30-50% of original
- Lease deposit: recoverable at face value (minus any deductions)
- Inventory: at cost
- Intangible assets: 200,000-500,000 THB for a recognized brand with online presence (highly subjective)

For a restaurant that originally invested 4 million THB in equipment and renovation, the asset-based value might be 1.5-2.5 million THB. If the same restaurant generates 1.5 million THB in annual EBITDA, the EBITDA method yields 3-5 million THB. The difference is the value of the ongoing business -- the customers, the reputation, the cash flow.

### Method 3: Revenue Multiple

Less common but sometimes used for restaurants that are not yet profitable or have inconsistent earnings.

**Formula:** Restaurant Value = Annual Revenue x Multiple

Revenue multiples for Thai restaurants typically range from 0.3x to 0.8x annual revenue. A restaurant doing 12 million THB/year in revenue might be valued at 3.6-9.6 million THB using this method.

I generally do not recommend this method because it ignores cost structure entirely. A restaurant generating 12 million THB in revenue with 15% EBITDA margin is worth significantly more than one generating the same revenue at 3% margin. The EBITDA method captures this difference; the revenue method does not.

### Method 4: Lease Value

In some cases, particularly in prime Bangkok locations, the value of the lease itself exceeds the value of the restaurant operations. A 5-year lease at 80,000 THB/month in an area where new leases are going for 150,000 THB/month has a below-market advantage worth 70,000 THB/month -- or roughly 4.2 million THB over the remaining term (before discounting).

Lease value becomes the primary valuation driver when:
- The restaurant is in a prime location (Thonglor, Sukhumvit, Silom, Ari) with below-market rent
- The lease has significant remaining term (3+ years)
- The restaurant itself is only marginally profitable (so the EBITDA multiple yields a low number)
- The buyer plans to change the concept entirely

In these situations, the buyer is really purchasing the lease with the restaurant as a bonus. Pricing should reflect the lease advantage.

<Callout type="tip">
Do not rely on a single valuation method. Calculate your restaurant's value using all applicable methods, then use the range to inform your asking price. Most transactions I see in Thailand close at a price between the asset-based floor and the EBITDA-multiple ceiling, with the final number determined by negotiation, buyer motivation, and the quality of the deal terms.
</Callout>

## Preparing Your Restaurant for Sale: The 12-Month Timeline

The best sales happen when the seller has spent 6-12 months preparing. Rushed sales -- listing the restaurant next week because you are done -- leave money on the table.

### Months 12-9: Clean Up the Financials

This is the most important preparation step and the one that takes the longest.

**The dual-books problem.** I need to address this directly because it is pervasive in Thailand's restaurant industry. Many Thai restaurants maintain two sets of books: one for the tax department (showing lower revenue and profit) and one for internal management (showing actual numbers). This is, to be clear, illegal. But it is common.

The problem for sellers: a buyer who sees "official" financials showing 500,000 THB/month in revenue when the restaurant actually does 900,000 THB/month is going to value the business based on the official numbers. You cannot credibly tell a sophisticated buyer "trust me, the real numbers are higher" without documentation.

If you are planning to sell, you need at least 12 months of clean, accurate financial statements. This means declaring actual revenue, paying taxes on it, and building a verifiable financial track record. Yes, your tax bill will increase during this period. Consider it a cost of sale preparation -- the higher valuation you will achieve with clean books more than offsets the additional tax.

**What clean financials look like:**
- 12-24 months of monthly P&L statements prepared by a licensed accountant
- Revenue verified against POS data and bank deposits
- All expenses documented with receipts and invoices
- Separate tracking of dine-in, delivery, and other revenue channels
- EBITDA calculated consistently using standard accounting principles
- Balance sheet showing assets, liabilities, and equity position

For a detailed breakdown of what buyers will examine in your P&L, see my [restaurant accounting guide](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand).

### Months 9-6: Fix Operational Issues

Buyers pay a premium for restaurants that run smoothly. Address deferred maintenance and operational problems before you list.

**Equipment.** Fix or replace anything that is broken, unreliable, or approaching end-of-life. A buyer who sees a worn-out walk-in cooler or a temperamental oven will negotiate a price reduction equal to the replacement cost -- and they will round up.

**Staff.** Resolve any lingering personnel issues. If you have a kitchen team member who is underperforming and you have been tolerating it because it is easier than finding a replacement, deal with it now. Buyers want to inherit a functional team, not one they need to restructure immediately.

**Lease.** Confirm the terms of your lease, particularly the assignment clause and remaining term. If your lease does not have an assignment clause (allowing transfer to a new tenant), talk to your landlord about adding one. If the lease expires within 18 months, negotiate an extension or at minimum get the landlord's written commitment to renew. A short or untransferable lease kills deals. My [lease negotiation guide](/blog/negotiate-restaurant-lease-bangkok) covers the specific clauses that matter.

**Legal and compliance.** Make sure all licenses are current, tax filings are up to date, and there are no outstanding legal issues. A buyer's lawyer will check all of this during due diligence, and surprises at that stage kill deals or reduce prices.

**Systems and documentation.** Document your recipes, SOPs, supplier contacts, and training procedures if you have not already. This increases value because it reduces the buyer's risk of operational deterioration after the sale.

### Months 6-3: Optimize Performance

Buyers look at recent performance more heavily than historical averages. The last 6 months of your financials will anchor the valuation discussion.

Push for the best possible numbers during this period:
- Drive revenue through marketing and promotions (but do not discount so aggressively that margins suffer)
- Tighten food cost through menu engineering and waste reduction
- Optimize labor scheduling to show clean labor cost percentages
- Control operating expenses to demonstrate cost discipline

This is not about faking the numbers. It is about running the restaurant as well as you possibly can, which is what you should be doing anyway. The goal is to show a buyer what the restaurant looks like at peak operational efficiency.

### Months 3-1: Prepare Sale Materials

**Information memorandum.** A professional document (5-15 pages) that presents your restaurant to potential buyers. Include:
- Business overview and concept description
- Financial summary (3 years if available, minimum 12 months)
- Lease terms and remaining duration
- Staff roster with roles and tenure
- Equipment list with age and condition
- Growth opportunities (what the buyer could do to improve the business)
- Asking price and preferred deal structure

**Lease documentation.** Copy of the full lease agreement, any amendments, correspondence with the landlord about assignment or renewal.

**Financial records.** 12-24 months of monthly P&Ls, tax returns, bank statements, POS reports.

**Legal documents.** Company registration, shareholder register (if selling a company), licenses, permits, insurance policies.

<Callout type="info">
Some sellers try to sell without an information memorandum, relying on verbal descriptions and informal conversations. This wastes time and attracts unserious buyers. A well-prepared IM signals professionalism and gives qualified buyers the information they need to make a decision. It also protects you -- everything is documented, reducing the risk of misunderstandings later.
</Callout>

## What Buyers Look For

Understanding the buyer's perspective helps you position your restaurant correctly and negotiate from strength.

**Profitable P&L with verifiable numbers.** This is the baseline. A buyer needs to see that the restaurant actually makes money, documented through POS data, bank deposits, and accounting records. "Trust me, we do well" is not evidence.

**Transferable lease with favorable terms.** The lease is often worth more than the restaurant's equipment. A buyer wants a lease with 3+ years remaining, below-market or market-rate rent, an assignment clause, and a cooperative landlord. Without this, the buyer is purchasing a restaurant that might not have a home in 18 months.

**Trained staff who will stay.** Staff turnover after a sale is one of the biggest risks. A buyer wants a team that is loyal to the restaurant (not just to the owner), properly trained, and unlikely to quit when ownership changes. Key person risk -- particularly the head chef -- is a major concern.

**Documented systems.** Recipe books, SOPs, training manuals, vendor lists, maintenance schedules. These assets prove the business can operate without the current owner.

**Brand value and reputation.** Google reviews, social media following, press coverage, Michelin recognition -- any evidence that the restaurant has built a reputation worth paying for.

**Clean legal structure.** No lawsuits pending, no tax disputes, no undisclosed liabilities, no complicated ownership arrangements that could create problems post-sale. Foreign ownership arrangements (nominee structures, BOI status, Treaty of Amity companies) add complexity that some buyers want to avoid.

**Growth potential.** Buyers pay more for restaurants with obvious opportunities for improvement: an underdeveloped delivery channel, an untapped private dining program, a menu that has not been engineered, or marketing that has not been seriously attempted. These are not problems -- they are upside the buyer can capture.

## Finding Buyers

### Business Brokers

Several business brokers in Thailand specialize in restaurant and hospitality sales. They typically charge 5-10% of the sale price as commission, paid by the seller. Brokers provide access to their buyer network, handle initial screening, and manage the sales process.

**When a broker makes sense:** If you do not have time to manage the sales process yourself, if you need access to a broader buyer pool, or if the deal involves complex legal structures (foreign ownership, multiple entities).

**When to go direct:** If you already know potential buyers through your industry network, if the sale is straightforward, or if you want to avoid the 5-10% commission.

Common platforms and brokers for restaurant sales in Thailand include:
- **FranchiseDeals.co.th** and **BizBuySell** (online listings)
- **CBRE Thailand** and **JLL** (for larger transactions, premium properties)
- Bangkok-based hospitality brokers (several small firms specialize in F&B)
- **Facebook groups** for F&B business sales in Thailand (surprisingly active)

### Industry Networks

The Bangkok F&B community is interconnected. Word spreads fast. Some of the best restaurant sales I have seen happened through direct industry connections -- a competitor who wants your location, a hotel group looking to add an in-house restaurant brand, or an investor who has been watching your restaurant with interest.

Tell your trusted industry contacts that you are considering a sale. Not publicly -- discreetly. A targeted approach to known buyers often yields better results than a public listing, which can spook staff and customers.

### Direct Approach to Strategic Buyers

If there is a restaurant group, hotel, or investor who would be a natural buyer, approach them directly. Common strategic buyers in Thailand's restaurant market:

- **Restaurant groups** expanding their portfolio (Minor Food, S&P, CRG, and smaller independent groups)
- **Hotel groups** wanting to bring in external restaurant brands for their properties
- **Investors and family offices** looking for operating businesses with cash flow
- **Experienced operators** who want a proven concept instead of starting from scratch
- **Returning expats** who want to buy into the Thailand F&B market

<Callout type="tip">
Confidentiality matters. If word gets out that your restaurant is for sale before you are ready, staff may start leaving, suppliers may tighten terms, and customers may worry about quality changes. Use NDAs (non-disclosure agreements) with potential buyers before sharing financial details. A simple one-page NDA, prepared by your lawyer, is standard practice.
</Callout>

## Negotiation and Deal Structure

### Pricing the Conversation

Your asking price should be 15-25% above your target price. Buyers expect to negotiate, and starting too low leaves money on the table while starting too high scares away serious buyers.

Be prepared to justify your price with data. "I invested 5 million THB" is not a justification -- buyers do not care what you spent; they care what the business is worth now. "The restaurant generates 1.5 million THB in annual EBITDA, the lease has 5 years remaining at below-market rent, and the team of 15 has been stable for 2+ years" is a justification.

### Common Deal Structures

**All-cash sale.** The simplest structure. The buyer pays the full price at closing (or in 2-3 tranches over a short period). Clean for the seller, but requires the buyer to have significant capital available. Most Thai restaurant sales under 5 million THB are all-cash.

**Earn-out.** The buyer pays a portion at closing and the remainder over 12-24 months, contingent on the restaurant maintaining specific financial targets (usually revenue or EBITDA). This bridges valuation gaps -- if the seller believes the restaurant is worth 5 million THB but the buyer is willing to pay only 3.5 million upfront, a 1.5 million THB earn-out tied to maintaining 120,000 THB/month EBITDA can close the gap.

Risk for the seller: if the buyer mismanages the restaurant, the earn-out targets may not be met and you receive less. Protect yourself with clear financial reporting requirements and operational covenants.

**Seller financing.** The seller effectively lends the buyer part of the purchase price, to be repaid with interest over an agreed period. Typical terms: 30-50% down payment, remainder paid over 18-36 months at 6-10% annual interest. This expands the buyer pool (more people can afford the down payment) but exposes the seller to repayment risk.

**Training and transition period.** Almost every restaurant sale includes a commitment from the seller to remain involved for 30-90 days after closing to train the new owner, introduce suppliers and staff, and ensure a smooth transition. Some deals include 6-12 months of advisory availability. This period is usually included in the purchase price; extended consulting is negotiated separately.

## Selling a Thai Company vs. Selling Assets

This is a structural decision with significant legal and tax implications.

### Share Sale (Selling the Company)

You sell the shares of the Thai limited company that owns the restaurant. The buyer acquires the entire legal entity, including all assets, liabilities, contracts, licenses, and obligations.

**Advantages for the seller:**
- Typically cleaner tax treatment (capital gains on share sale can be structured favorably)
- All contracts and licenses transfer automatically (the legal entity does not change)
- Simpler documentation

**Advantages for the buyer:**
- Business continuity -- the lease, licenses, supplier agreements, and employment relationships continue uninterrupted
- No need to re-apply for licenses

**Disadvantages for the buyer:**
- Buyer inherits all liabilities, including undisclosed ones (tax disputes, employee claims, supplier debts)
- Requires extensive due diligence to identify hidden risks

### Asset Sale (Selling the Restaurant's Assets)

You sell the individual assets -- equipment, inventory, recipes, brand, customer lists, lease assignment -- while retaining the company.

**Advantages for the buyer:**
- No hidden liability risk -- you only buy what you can see and inspect
- Clean start with a new legal entity

**Disadvantages:**
- Lease must be reassigned (landlord approval required) or a new lease negotiated
- All licenses must be re-applied for under the new entity
- Employment contracts must be terminated and re-issued (severance obligations for the seller)
- Supplier relationships need to be re-established

**In practice in Thailand,** most small to mid-size restaurant sales (under 10 million THB) are structured as asset sales because buyers are wary of inheriting unknown liabilities. Larger transactions and sales involving premium leases are more often structured as share sales because re-negotiating the lease with the landlord adds risk and cost.

**Foreign ownership complications.** If the selling entity has a foreign ownership structure (commonly used by expats to comply with the Foreign Business Act), the buyer needs to navigate the same regulatory requirements. This adds legal complexity and cost to the transaction. Some buyers prefer to start fresh with their own corporate structure rather than acquire an existing one with foreign ownership issues.

## The Due Diligence Process

Once a buyer is serious, they will (or should) conduct due diligence before finalizing the deal. Expect them to examine:

**Financial records.** 2-3 years of tax returns, monthly P&Ls, bank statements, POS data, and accounting records. They will cross-reference POS revenue with bank deposits to verify that reported revenue is accurate. Discrepancies between POS data and tax filings are the fastest way to lose a buyer's trust.

**Lease agreement.** Full review of terms, remaining duration, assignment clause, rent escalation schedule, landlord's history with tenants, and any verbal agreements not captured in the written lease.

**Staff records.** Employment contracts, salary records, social security registration, any pending labor disputes or unfiled severance obligations. Under Thai labor law, employees who have worked for more than 120 days are entitled to severance pay if terminated -- the buyer needs to understand this liability.

**Licenses and permits.** All current licenses, renewal dates, compliance history, any past violations or warnings from the health department or local authority.

**Supplier contracts.** Outstanding payables, any minimum purchase commitments, key supplier relationships and terms.

**Tax compliance.** VAT filing history, corporate income tax returns, withholding tax compliance, any outstanding disputes with the Revenue Department.

**Intellectual property.** Trademark registration for the restaurant name and logo (many Thai restaurants do not register trademarks, which is a risk for buyers).

**Physical inspection.** Equipment condition, building condition, health and safety compliance, pest control records.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not try to hide problems during due diligence. Sophisticated buyers will find them, and discovery of a hidden issue -- an undisclosed tax liability, a staff lawsuit you "forgot" to mention, a broken walk-in cooler covered by a tablecloth -- destroys trust and often kills the deal entirely. Disclose everything upfront, even if it reduces the price. A transparent seller gets better terms than a deceptive one.
</Callout>

## Transition Planning

A successful sale does not end at closing. The transition period determines whether the buyer succeeds with their purchase and whether you receive any deferred payments (earn-outs, seller financing).

**Staff retention.** Meet with your team before the ownership change becomes public. Be honest. Introduce the new owner. Address concerns about job security, pay, and working conditions. The buyer should commit (ideally in writing, within the sale agreement) to maintaining employment terms for at least 3-6 months post-closing. If key staff leave during the transition, the business loses value rapidly.

**Customer continuity.** If you have a personal relationship with regular customers, introduce them to the new owner. For restaurants with social media followings, plan the ownership transition announcement carefully -- position it positively, emphasize continuity, and avoid creating anxiety.

**Recipe handover.** This is where documentation pays off. If your recipes are written, costed, and photographed, the handover is simple. If they exist only in your head, you will spend weeks in the kitchen teaching the buyer's team. Factor this time into the training period.

**Supplier relationships.** Introduce the buyer to all key suppliers personally. Suppliers who have worked with you for years may be skeptical of a new owner -- your introduction carries weight and helps maintain terms.

**Systems transfer.** POS access, accounting software credentials, social media accounts, delivery platform accounts, reservation system, email accounts, bank accounts (if selling the company) -- create a complete list and transfer systematically.

## Tax Implications of Selling a Restaurant in Thailand

Tax treatment depends on whether you are selling shares or assets, and whether the seller is a Thai entity or an individual.

**Share sale by a Thai company.** Capital gains on the sale of shares by a Thai company are taxed as ordinary corporate income at the standard CIT rate (20%, or SME rates for qualifying companies).

**Share sale by an individual (Thai resident).** Capital gains from the sale of shares in a non-listed Thai company are taxed as personal income at the individual's marginal tax rate (up to 35%).

**Share sale by a non-resident individual.** Capital gains from selling shares in a Thai company are generally subject to a flat 15% withholding tax.

**Asset sale.** The selling company recognizes the sale proceeds as revenue and the net book value of the assets as cost of goods sold. The gain is taxed as ordinary corporate income. Additionally, VAT at 7% applies to the sale of tangible assets.

**Key planning considerations:**
- If selling shares, structure the transaction to optimize capital gains treatment
- Consider the timing of the sale relative to the company's fiscal year
- Severance pay obligations for staff (if asset sale) are deductible expenses
- Professional tax advice is not optional -- the difference between good and bad structuring can be millions of THB on a mid-size transaction

Consult a Thai tax lawyer or accounting firm experienced in M&A transactions. Budget 30,000-100,000 THB for professional advisory depending on deal complexity. The [profit margins guide](/blog/restaurant-profit-margins-thailand) covers the ongoing tax framework, but transaction tax planning is a separate specialty.

## Common Deal-Killers

I have watched deals die for avoidable reasons. Here are the patterns.

**The lease problem.** The landlord refuses to transfer or extend the lease. Or the remaining lease term is too short for the buyer to recover their investment. Or the lease has a clause prohibiting assignment. This is the number one deal-killer in Thailand's restaurant market, because leases are the single most valuable and most fragile asset. Solve the lease question before you list the restaurant for sale.

**The staff problem.** The head chef threatens to quit if ownership changes. Or the buyer discovers that three key employees are actually working illegally (no work permits) and would need to be replaced. Or the seller has been paying below minimum wage and the buyer inherits a potential labor dispute.

**Undisclosed liabilities.** The buyer's lawyer discovers a pending tax audit, an unresolved supplier dispute, or a former employee's unfiled severance claim. Any hidden liability discovered during due diligence creates a trust deficit that is almost impossible to recover from.

**Unrealistic pricing.** The seller wants 8 million THB for a restaurant that generates 800,000 THB/year in EBITDA (a 10x multiple). No rational buyer will pay that. Unrealistic pricing wastes everyone's time and signals that the seller either does not understand their business's value or is not serious about selling.

**The "I built this" emotional attachment.** Some sellers cannot separate their emotional investment from the financial value. "I spent 2 years of my life building this" is true but irrelevant to valuation. Buyers pay for future cash flows, not past effort. Sellers who anchor on their emotional investment rather than market value rarely close deals.

**Regulatory surprises.** The restaurant is operating without a proper license, or the building does not have a commercial use permit, or the alcohol license is expired and renewal is uncertain. Any regulatory issue that threatens the buyer's ability to continue operating is a deal-killer.

**Disagreement over deal structure.** The seller wants all cash; the buyer wants 50% seller financing. Neither side will move. Flexibility on structure -- as long as the total value is fair -- closes deals. Rigidity on structure kills them.

## Realistic Timeline: Decision to Closing

**Month 0:** Decision to sell. Begin financial cleanup and preparation.

**Months 1-6:** Clean up financials, fix operational issues, optimize performance. Talk to a lawyer about deal structure and tax implications.

**Month 6:** Prepare information memorandum and sale materials. Engage a broker or begin confidential outreach to potential buyers.

**Months 7-9:** Marketing and buyer identification. Initial meetings, sign NDAs, share information memorandum. Receive preliminary offers.

**Month 9-10:** Negotiate terms with the most serious buyer. Sign a Letter of Intent (LOI) or Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) outlining the key deal terms and giving the buyer an exclusivity period for due diligence.

**Months 10-11:** Due diligence. The buyer's team examines your financials, legal structure, lease, staff, and operations. Address any issues that arise.

**Month 11-12:** Final negotiation, contract drafting, and closing. Sign the Sale and Purchase Agreement (SPA). Transfer ownership. Begin the transition period.

**Months 12-14:** Transition period. Training, introductions, handover of systems and relationships.

Total realistic timeline: **6-12 months from decision to closing,** with another 1-3 months for transition. Some deals close faster (3-4 months) when the buyer is known and the business is well-prepared. Others drag on for 12+ months due to legal complications or financing delays.

<Callout type="info">
During the sales process, do not let the restaurant deteriorate. The temptation to mentally check out once you have decided to sell is strong, but declining performance during the sale period gives the buyer ammunition to renegotiate the price downward. Run the restaurant as if you plan to keep it forever, right up until the day you hand over the keys.
</Callout>

## Emotional Considerations: Letting Go

I want to address this because it is real and it affects decisions.

Selling a restaurant you built from scratch is not like selling a car or an apartment. You designed the menu, chose every tile, hired and trained the staff, solved a thousand problems, and built relationships with customers who know your name. Walking away from that is genuinely difficult, even when it is the right decision.

Some things that help:

**Separate identity from business.** You are not your restaurant. Your skills, relationships, and experience transfer to whatever you do next. The restaurant was a vehicle for those things; it is not the destination.

**Focus on what you are moving toward, not what you are leaving behind.** Whether it is a new concept, a different career, retirement, or simply rest -- define what comes next before you close the sale. Sellers who have a clear plan for their post-sale life handle the emotional transition better than those who sell into a vacuum.

**Allow yourself a mourning period.** It is normal to feel loss, doubt, and even regret in the weeks after closing. This passes. The financial security and freedom you gain from a successful sale become more apparent once the emotional intensity fades.

**Stay in the industry if it matters to you.** Many former restaurant owners stay connected to F&B through consulting, investing, teaching, or mentoring. Selling one restaurant does not mean leaving the industry permanently.

## What to Do After Selling

**Non-compete clauses.** Most sale agreements include a non-compete clause preventing the seller from opening a competing restaurant within a defined geographic radius and time period. Typical terms in Thailand: 2-5 km radius, 2-3 years duration. Make sure the clause is reasonable before you sign -- an overly broad non-compete can severely limit your future options.

**Reinvestment.** If you are staying in F&B, the sale proceeds from a successful restaurant provide seed capital for your next venture. Many of the best restaurant operators I know are on their second or third concept, having used proceeds from earlier sales to fund new ideas with less financial stress.

**Rest.** If you have been running a restaurant for 5+ years, you are probably exhausted in ways you do not fully recognize. Take at least 2-3 months off before starting something new. The clarity that comes from rest is worth more than the urgency you feel to stay busy.

**Financial planning.** A lump-sum payment from a restaurant sale should be managed carefully. Consult a financial advisor about tax-efficient investment and avoid the temptation to immediately pour everything into a new venture without proper planning.
