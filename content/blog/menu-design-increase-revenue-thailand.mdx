## The Golden Triangle: Where Eyes Land First on a Menu

Eye-tracking studies on restaurant menus -- the most cited is the Gallup study from the early 2000s, with subsequent work by the Cornell Food and Brand Lab and various hospitality researchers -- consistently show the same pattern. When a customer opens a physical menu, their eyes do not start at the top-left corner and scan left to right like a book. They follow a triangular pattern.

On a single-page or two-page spread menu, the gaze sequence is:

1. **Centre of the page first.** The eyes land in the middle, slightly above the midpoint.
2. **Upper right.** From centre, the eyes move to the upper right area.
3. **Upper left.** Then back to the upper left.

This creates an inverted triangle -- the "golden triangle" -- and the items positioned in these three zones get the most visual attention. Studies show that items in the golden triangle receive 2-3x more fixation time than items in the lower corners or along the edges.

What does this mean for your menu?

**Your highest-margin items belong in the golden triangle.** If your green curry at 78% margin is buried in the lower-left corner while your prawn pad thai at 55% margin sits in the centre, you are designing against your own profitability. Map your Stars and promoted Puzzles to the golden triangle zones, and your menu becomes a profit engine rather than a random list.

**Section headers in the golden triangle get read first.** If the centre of your menu says "Soups" but your highest-margin category is "Stir-Fries," swap them. The section that hits the golden triangle should be the one containing your best-margin items.

**The lower-left corner is the dead zone.** Items here get the least attention. This is where you put your Plowhorses -- items that sell well regardless of placement (fried rice, pad thai) because customers are already looking for them. They do not need prime real estate.

<Callout type="tip">
If you have a single-page menu, the golden triangle is compact and powerful -- centre, upper right, upper left. If you have a two-page spread (the menu opens like a book), the golden triangle shifts: the right page gets more attention than the left, and the upper right of the right page is the single highest-attention zone. Put your best-margin section on the right page.
</Callout>

### Testing the Golden Triangle in Bangkok

I ran an informal test with three restaurants in Sukhumvit in 2024. Each had a two-page laminated menu. We identified their top three margin items and moved them from wherever they were (in all three cases, buried in the middle or bottom of the left page) to the upper-right quadrant of the right page, inside a subtle bordered box with a "Chef's Selection" label.

Results across the three restaurants after 30 days:

- Orders of the repositioned items increased 35-65%
- Average check increased 9-14%
- Total food cost percentage dropped by 1.5-2 points (because the menu mix shifted toward higher-margin items)

No new dishes, no new ingredients, no price changes. Just repositioning three items on a page.

## Visual Hierarchy: Guiding the Eye Without Being Obvious

Visual hierarchy is the design principle that certain elements on a page attract attention before others, based on size, colour, contrast, weight, and position. On a restaurant menu, you use visual hierarchy to guide customers toward the items you want them to order -- your Stars and promoted Puzzles -- while making the rest of the menu easy to navigate.

The tools are straightforward.

**Size and weight.** A dish name in bold 14-point type gets more attention than one in regular 11-point type. Use larger or bolder type for items you want to promote, and standard type for everything else. You do not need to make the promoted item enormous -- even a 2-point size increase and bold weight creates noticeable contrast.

**Boxes, borders, and shading.** A light background shade or a thin border around an item immediately separates it from the surrounding text. Use this sparingly -- one or two boxed items per page, maximum. If everything is boxed, nothing stands out. I typically box the single highest-margin item in each section with a "Recommended" or "Signature" tag.

**Icons and markers.** A small chilli icon for spice level, a leaf for vegetarian, a star for chef's recommendation. These markers draw the eye and create visual anchors. The star or recommendation icon should go on your Stars and Puzzles -- not on random items the chef happens to like.

**Colour accents.** A single accent colour (gold, deep red, or whatever matches your brand) applied to item names, section headers, or small design elements creates a visual path across the menu. Use the accent colour on the items you want customers to notice. Do not use more than one accent colour -- it creates noise rather than guidance.

**White space.** This is the most underused tool. An item surrounded by generous white space attracts more attention than one crammed between six other items. Give your highest-margin items breathing room. Tighten spacing around Plowhorses and Dogs.

### What to Avoid

**Equal treatment of all items.** If every dish name is the same size, same weight, same colour, and spaced equally, the menu becomes a wall of text with no visual guidance. The customer defaults to scanning for familiar items (pad thai, green curry) or the cheapest option. Neither of these is optimal for your margins.

**Visual clutter.** Clip art, excessive decoration, multiple fonts, watermarks behind text, gradient backgrounds. These were standard in Thai restaurant menus 15 years ago and they are still common. Every decorative element that is not guiding the customer toward a purchase is a distraction. Fewer design elements, more deliberately placed.

**Tiny text to fit more items.** If you need to shrink your font below 10 points to fit everything on the page, you have too many items. A 30-item menu in readable type sells better than a 60-item menu in squint-required type, because overwhelmed customers default to the safe, familiar, low-margin option.

## Pricing Display: How to Show Prices Without Making Them the Focus

How you display your prices directly affects whether customers order based on what they want or based on what costs least. The goal is to make prices visible (customers need to know what things cost) but not dominant (you do not want them comparison-shopping down a column of numbers).

I covered several of these principles in [Menu Pricing Psychology for Thai Restaurants](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand), but here is the design-specific application.

### Remove the Price Column

The most common menu layout in Thailand looks like this:

> Pad Thai Goong ............ 189
> Green Curry Chicken ...... 229
> Tom Yum Goong ............ 259

Those dots and right-aligned prices create a vertical column of numbers. The customer's eye is drawn to the column, and they start comparing prices rather than reading dish names and descriptions. Research from Cornell's Center for Hospitality Research found that when prices are listed in a column, customers order lower-priced items 8-12% more often than when prices are embedded in the description.

The fix: embed the price at the end of the dish description, in the same font size and weight as the description text.

> **Pad Thai with Tiger Prawns** -- Tamarind sauce, crushed peanuts, lime, bean sprouts. Served with a lime wedge and dried chilli flakes. 189

The price is still visible, but it is not the first thing the eye catches. The customer reads the dish name, then the description (which builds perceived value), and then encounters the price in context. By the time they see "189," they have already imagined the dish and the price feels like an afterthought.

### Drop the Currency Symbol

Writing "189" instead of "฿189" or "189 THB" reduces the psychological association with spending money. The number becomes an abstract identifier rather than a cost. This is standard practice at Bangkok's upscale restaurants (Le Du, Gaggan, Sorn, Nusara) and rare at casual dining, but it works at every price point.

Add a small footer note: "All prices in Thai Baht. Subject to 7% VAT and 10% service charge." This covers the informational need without cluttering every line item.

### Use Descriptive Language to Justify Prices

A dish listed as "Green Curry 229" invites the question "is green curry worth 229?" A dish listed as "Slow-simmered green curry with free-range chicken, Thai eggplant, and sweet basil in house-made coconut cream. 229" makes the price feel earned. The description does the selling; the price just confirms the value.

This matters more at higher price points. A 180 THB stir-fry barely needs justification for most Bangkok diners. A 380 THB seafood dish absolutely does. Invest your description-writing effort proportionally -- longer, more evocative descriptions on your premium (and highest-margin) items, shorter descriptions on your entry-level dishes.

## Section Structure and Item Placement

The order and structure of sections on your menu shapes how customers navigate it, and what they order first, last, and not at all. This is not random -- it should be engineered.

### How Many Sections?

A typical Thai restaurant menu should have 5-8 sections. Fewer than five forces unrelated items together (soups and salads in the same section confuse the eye), while more than eight creates decision fatigue and slows down ordering.

A proven structure for a casual Thai restaurant:

1. **Chef's Selections / Signature Dishes** (3-5 items) -- your Stars and promoted Puzzles. This section exists to catch the golden triangle and to set an [anchoring price](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand) for the rest of the menu. Lead with your highest-priced item.
2. **Appetisers and Salads** (4-6 items) -- these are ordered early, often as "while we decide" impulse adds. Keep this section visible and concise.
3. **Soups** (3-5 items) -- tom yum, tom kha, clear soups. In Thai dining culture, soup is a shared table dish, not a starter. Position this after appetisers but before mains.
4. **Curries** (4-6 items) -- a core Thai category with strong margins (68-75%).
5. **Stir-Fries** (5-8 items) -- the workhorse category. Highest margins (70-80%), high volume.
6. **Rice and Noodle Dishes** (4-6 items) -- pad thai, fried rice, khao pad, pad see ew. These are Plowhorses: high demand, moderate margin.
7. **Seafood Specials** (3-5 items) -- higher price points. Works as a secondary anchor while providing premium options for bigger spenders.
8. **Desserts and Beverages** -- often on a separate card or the back page. Desserts in Thai dining are frequently impulse orders suggested by the server, not ordered from the menu.

### Item Placement Within Sections

Within each section, item order matters. The first and last items in a list get the most attention (the "serial position effect" -- primacy and recency bias). Items in the middle get the least.

**First position:** your highest-margin item in the category. This is the anchor and the item the customer reads with the most attention.

**Second position:** your second-best margin item or a strong seller you want to promote.

**Last position:** a solid seller with a good margin. The eye scans to the end and this is the second-most-read position.

**Middle positions:** Plowhorses and Dogs. Items that sell on name recognition (pad thai, fried rice) or items you are content to let fade.

### How Many Items Per Section?

Fewer is better. Research from the Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research found that sections with 5-7 items generate higher per-item sales than sections with 10+ items. The reason is straightforward: more choices create more deliberation, more comparison, and more defaulting to the cheapest or most familiar option.

If your current menu has 15 stir-fries, you probably have 5-6 that account for 80% of the orders and 9-10 that account for the remaining 20%. Those bottom 9-10 are diluting attention from your profitable items while adding complexity to your kitchen and inventory. Cut them. A focused menu with 25-35 items outperforms a 60-item menu in almost every metric: higher average check, lower food cost, faster kitchen throughput, less waste.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not confuse "fewer items" with "less choice." A 30-item menu with 6 well-designed sections gives customers plenty of variety. A 60-item menu with 12 sections gives customers anxiety. If you are worried about losing regulars who order a specific dish you are cutting, check your POS data. Items ordered fewer than 3 times per week are safe to remove. Nobody will miss them except the one cook who liked making them.
</Callout>

## Photography vs. No Photography: The Menu Image Debate

This is one of the most common questions I get from restaurant owners in Thailand, and the answer is not as simple as "photos sell more food." It depends on your positioning, your price point, and the quality of the photos.

### When Photography Works

**Casual dining targeting tourists.** International visitors who cannot read Thai and are unfamiliar with Thai dish names rely heavily on photos to make ordering decisions. A tourist-area restaurant without photos is forcing customers to guess, and guessing leads to safe, low-margin defaults ("I'll just have pad thai").

**Delivery platforms.** On GrabFood and LINE MAN, photos are not optional -- they are the primary sales tool. Items with professional photos get 25-30% more orders than items without. This is well-documented in platform data.

**Signature or visually striking dishes.** A whole grilled fish on a wooden board, a dramatically plated tom yum in a clay pot, a dessert with visible craftsmanship. If the dish looks better in real life than it reads on paper, photograph it.

**New or unfamiliar items.** Dishes that customers do not recognise benefit from photos because the image reduces the perceived risk of ordering something unknown.

### When Photography Hurts

**Fine dining.** No Michelin-starred restaurant in Bangkok uses menu photos. The absence of photos signals trust -- "order based on our description and reputation, not because it looks good in a picture." Photos at this level cheapen the experience and make the menu feel like a chain restaurant.

**Low-quality photos.** A bad photo is worse than no photo. Dim lighting, unflattering angles, inconsistent styling, or photos that do not match what the kitchen actually produces. If you are going to use photos, invest in professional food photography (30,000-60,000 THB for a full menu shoot, or 1,500-3,000 THB per dish). Smartphone photos from the kitchen are rarely acceptable.

**Every item photographed.** A menu where every single dish has a photo becomes a visual catalogue, not a curated dining experience. The eye does not know where to focus because everything is competing for attention. Photograph 6-10 key items (your Stars and Puzzles), and let the rest sell on description.

**Upscale casual trying to signal quality.** There is a middle zone -- restaurants at the 300-500 THB average check level -- where photos can go either way. If your concept leans toward "modern, sophisticated, quality-focused," skip the photos and invest in better descriptions and cleaner design. If your concept leans toward "fun, approachable, visual," photos work.

### The Hybrid Approach

The approach I recommend for most mid-range Thai restaurants: photograph 4-8 items maximum, and make those your highest-margin dishes. Feature these photos prominently -- full-colour, good size, with the item name and a short description adjacent. Leave the remaining items as text-only. This creates visual anchors that draw attention to specific dishes (the ones you want to sell) without turning the entire menu into a photo gallery.

<Callout type="tip">
If you use photos, update them annually. A photo of your pad thai from 2019 that no longer matches the current plating creates a trust gap when the dish arrives at the table. Seasonal dishes should be re-photographed each season. Budget 15,000-25,000 THB/year for menu photo updates -- it is a tiny percentage of the revenue those photos generate.
</Callout>

## Paper Quality, Format, and Physical Design

The physical properties of your menu communicate your positioning before the customer reads a single word. A laminated A3 sheet folded in half says "casual." A leather-bound book with heavy card stock says "premium." Neither is wrong -- but your menu's physical form needs to match your restaurant's identity and price point.

### Single Page vs. Multi-Page

**Single page (one side or both sides):** Best for restaurants with 20-30 items. Forces focus, speeds up ordering, and works well for concepts with a clear identity. Street-food-inspired restaurants, noodle shops, and focused Thai kitchens thrive with a single page. The golden triangle is tightest and most effective on a single page.

**Two-page spread (bi-fold):** The standard for casual to mid-range Thai restaurants with 30-50 items. Opens like a book, with the right page getting more attention than the left. Put your highest-margin categories on the right page.

**Multi-page (three or more pages):** Necessary for large menus, but every additional page reduces the effectiveness of any single item's placement. If you need more than two pages, consider splitting into a main menu and a separate drinks/dessert menu. This keeps each document focused and manageable.

### Material and Finish

**Laminated card stock:** Durable, wipe-clean, affordable (8-15 THB per menu to produce). Standard for casual dining. Reprinting is cheap when you need to update prices or items. The downside: laminated menus feel temporary, which is fine for a 180 THB average check but undermines restaurants aiming higher.

**Unlaminated heavy paper (250-300 gsm):** Feels premium, takes print well, and allows for textured or matte finishes. Works for upscale casual to fine dining. Costs more (30-80 THB per menu) and gets damaged by spills, so you need to reprint more frequently. Budget for a monthly reprint cycle if you go this route.

**Leather or faux-leather covers:** Signals fine dining. Works when the menu is a multi-page bound document with thick interior pages. Expensive to produce (200-500 THB per menu) but durable if maintained. Requires inserts that can be reprinted separately when menu items change.

**Clipboard or wood board:** A trending format in Bangkok's hip cafe and bistro scene. The menu is printed on a single sheet and clipped to a board or slotted into a wooden frame. Feels artisanal and casual. Easy to reprint, which makes menu testing simple. Works well for concepts that change their menu frequently.

## Digital Menu Considerations

QR code menus exploded during COVID and have stayed in many Bangkok restaurants. They save printing costs, allow real-time updates, and reduce physical contact. But they come with significant trade-offs for revenue.

### The Revenue Problem with Digital Menus

Multiple studies -- including a 2023 study from the International Journal of Hospitality Management -- found that customers ordering from digital menus on their phones spend 8-15% less than customers ordering from physical menus. The reasons:

**Small screen limits browsing.** A phone screen shows 3-5 items at a time, compared to 20-30 visible items on a physical two-page menu. Customers scroll less than they flip pages, which means items below the fold get significantly less attention.

**No golden triangle.** The eye-tracking patterns that make the golden triangle effective on a physical menu do not apply to a scrolling phone screen. The equivalent on mobile is "whatever is at the top of the list," which means item order becomes even more important while spatial design becomes irrelevant.

**Distracting device.** Once a customer picks up their phone to read the menu, they check notifications, reply to messages, and browse social media. The menu is competing with every app on the device. A physical menu commands undivided attention.

**Reduced impulse ordering.** On a physical menu, a customer browsing the curry section can see the appetiser section in their peripheral vision and think "oh, let us add the spring rolls too." On a phone, each section is a separate scroll or tap away, and that peripheral trigger does not exist.

### When Digital Menus Make Sense

**As a supplement, not a replacement.** Offer both a physical menu and a QR code for customers who prefer digital. Some customers -- particularly younger Thai diners and tech-forward tourists -- genuinely prefer browsing on their phone. Let them, but default to handing a physical menu to every table.

**For specials and rotating items.** A printed menu is expensive to change, but a digital supplement for daily specials, seasonal items, and promotions can be updated in minutes at zero cost.

**For multilingual support.** A QR code that links to menus in Thai, English, Chinese, and Japanese solves the translation problem without printing four separate physical menus. More on this in the bilingual section below.

**For delivery and takeaway.** When there is no physical dining experience, digital is the only option. Invest in your delivery platform photography, item descriptions, and menu structure as seriously as your physical menu.

<Callout type="info">
If you use a QR code menu, put your highest-margin items at the top of each category. The scroll decay on mobile is steep -- items below position 5-6 in a category lose 40-60% of views compared to position 1. Treat the top 3 positions in each digital category the way you treat the golden triangle on a physical menu.
</Callout>

## A/B Testing Your Menu Layout

Menu A/B testing is the process of running two different menu designs simultaneously to see which one generates better results. It is the only way to move from "I think this layout is better" to "I know this layout is better."

### How to Run a Menu A/B Test

**Print two versions of your menu.** Version A is your current menu. Version B has the specific change you want to test -- a repositioned item, a new section order, added photography, different pricing display, whatever.

**Alternate versions by table or by day.** The cleanest method: odd-numbered tables get Version A, even-numbered tables get Version B, for two full weeks. Alternatively, alternate by day (Monday = Version A, Tuesday = Version B, and so on) though this introduces day-of-week variability.

**Track per-cover metrics.** For each table, record which menu version they had, the total bill, and the items ordered. After two weeks, compare:

- **Average check per cover** (total bill divided by number of diners)
- **Order mix** (percentage of each menu item ordered)
- **High-margin item sales** (did Version B push more Star orders?)

**Run one test at a time.** If you change three things between Version A and Version B, you cannot attribute the result to any single change. Test one variable per cycle: item placement in week 1, pricing display in week 3, photography in week 5.

### What to Test

**Item positioning within a section.** Move your highest-margin item from mid-section to first position. This is the single highest-impact test and the one I run first with every client.

**Section order.** Swap two sections -- put curries before soups, or move your signature section from page 2 to page 1. Watch for changes in the order mix between those categories.

**Photography.** Add photos to 4-6 items on Version B and compare order rates for those specific items against Version A (text only).

**Descriptions.** Version A has short descriptions ("Green curry with chicken"), while Version B has evocative descriptions ("Slow-simmered green curry with free-range chicken, Thai eggplant, and sweet basil in house-made coconut cream"). Compare order rates and average check.

**Box/highlight treatment.** Version A has no highlighted items, while Version B has a bordered "Chef's Recommendation" box around two high-margin items in each section. This test typically shows a 20-35% increase in orders of the highlighted items.

### Tracking Menu Design ROI

The return on a menu redesign is straightforward to calculate:

> **Monthly revenue increase** = (New average check - Old average check) x Monthly covers
>
> **ROI** = Monthly revenue increase / Cost of menu redesign

For a restaurant doing 3,000 covers/month with an average check of 280 THB, a menu redesign that increases the average check by 10% (to 308 THB) generates an additional 84,000 THB/month. If the redesign (design fee + photography + printing) costs 60,000 THB, the ROI is 140% in the first month. Every subsequent month is pure profit from the investment.

Track these metrics monthly for at least six months after a redesign, because the full effect takes 2-3 months to stabilise as your team learns the new menu and regulars adjust their ordering patterns.

## Thailand-Specific Design Considerations

Designing menus for Thai restaurants involves challenges that do not exist in most other markets. The bilingual requirement, the split between local and tourist customers, and the cultural expectations around Thai dining all shape how you should approach layout and design.

### Bilingual Menus: Thai and English

Almost every restaurant in Bangkok that serves any non-Thai clientele needs a bilingual menu. How you handle the two languages affects readability, design, and the customer's experience.

**Option 1: Side by side.** Thai on the left, English on the right (or vice versa). Each dish appears twice, once in each language, with the price shown once. This works for menus with 20-30 items but becomes unwieldy at 40+ items because each dish takes double the vertical space.

**Option 2: Stacked.** Thai dish name on top (or first), English translation below in smaller or italic type. This is the most space-efficient bilingual format and the one I recommend for most Bangkok restaurants. It keeps both languages visible without doubling the menu length.

**Option 3: Separate menus.** A Thai-language menu and an English-language menu. Staff hands the appropriate one to each table. This allows each menu to be optimised for its audience (different descriptions, different emphasis, potentially different section ordering) but doubles your printing costs and requires staff to make a judgment call on which menu to give.

**Option 4: Thai menu + QR code for English.** The physical menu is in Thai, with a QR code linking to the English version on a phone. This is clean and cost-effective, but it means English-reading customers get the inferior digital experience while Thai-reading customers get the superior physical menu.

For most Bangkok restaurants, Option 2 (stacked) is the best balance. The Thai name comes first (respecting the Thai market as your primary audience), followed by the English name and a brief English description. Prices appear once. The menu feels unified rather than split.

### Tourist vs. Local Menu Versions

Some restaurant owners ask me whether they should create different menus for tourists and locals. My answer: no. Dual pricing -- or the perception of it -- destroys trust. If a tourist discovers that the menu at the next table has different prices, you lose that customer permanently and earn a damaging review.

What you can do, without dual pricing:

**Tailor descriptions to the audience.** In a tourist-heavy area, English descriptions should include more context: "Thailand's most famous soup -- a hot and sour broth with prawns, galangal, lemongrass, and kaffir lime leaves" helps a first-time visitor understand tom yum goong. In a local-heavy area, that level of explanation is unnecessary and can feel condescending.

**Offer a tourist-friendly set meal.** A "Taste of Thailand" set at 890-1,290 THB that includes 5 dishes, rice, and a drink, serves two purposes. For tourists, it solves the "I don't know what to order" problem. For your business, it [bundles high-margin items at a premium price point](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand) that tourists perceive as a good deal compared to their home market.

**Adjust spice level communication.** Thai customers know what "pet mak" (very spicy) means. Tourist menus benefit from a clear 1-5 spice scale with actual descriptions: "Level 3: Noticeable heat, comfortable for most. Level 5: Thai hot -- only for those who know what they are getting into." This reduces kitchen remakes and customer complaints, both of which have a cost.

### Cultural Considerations in Thai Menu Design

**Shared dining format.** Thai menus should be designed for table ordering, not individual ordering. Descriptions like "serves 1-2" or "great for sharing" help mixed groups (Thai + tourist) navigate the communal dining format that locals take for granted but visitors may not understand.

**Rice as a given, not a feature.** In Thai dining culture, rice is assumed. Many Thai-language menus do not even list rice -- it just arrives. English-language menus should list rice clearly (with pricing if applicable) because international diners expect to see it and will feel confused or cheated if a 30 THB rice charge appears on the bill unexplained.

**Dish name transliteration matters.** "Pad Kra Pao" versus "Pad Kaprao" versus "Stir-fried basil pork" -- inconsistent transliteration confuses tourists and makes your menu harder to find on Google. Pick one romanisation standard and use it consistently. I recommend the Royal Thai General System (RTGS) for consistency, supplemented by the most commonly Googled English name.

**Seasonal and religious awareness.** During Buddhist holidays and the vegetarian festival (Tesagan Gin Je, typically October), many Thai customers eat vegetarian. A clearly marked vegetarian section or "Jay" (เจ) markers on applicable items captures this demand without requiring a separate festival menu.

## Common Menu Design Mistakes I See in Bangkok

After auditing hundreds of menus, these are the recurring problems.

### 1. The Encyclopaedia Menu

A 60-80 item menu across 4+ pages that tries to be everything to everyone. Pad thai, sushi, pasta, burgers, steaks, and a full Thai menu. The kitchen cannot execute this volume well, the customer is overwhelmed, food cost is high because of ingredient diversity, and waste is inevitable because half the items sell fewer than twice a week.

**Fix:** Cut to 30-40 items maximum. Use POS data to identify your bottom 20 sellers and remove them. Your kitchen will be faster, your inventory tighter, and your customers will order more confidently. For guidance on [building a focused restaurant concept](/blog/restaurant-concepts-bangkok-2026), the most successful restaurants in Bangkok right now all have tight, opinionated menus.

### 2. Price as the Dominant Visual Element

Menus where every price is in bold, red, or a larger font than the dish name. The customer reads prices before food, compares numbers before descriptions, and defaults to the cheapest option. Your menu has become a price list rather than a sales tool.

**Fix:** Make dish names the dominant visual element (bold, larger type). Embed prices at the end of descriptions in the same font as the description text. Remove currency symbols. Kill dot leaders.

### 3. No Descriptive Copy

"Green Curry Chicken -- 229." That is it. No description of what is in the curry, how it is made, what makes it special. The customer has no reason to choose this 229 THB curry over the 180 THB stir-fry, because the menu gives them nothing to evaluate except name and price.

**Fix:** Write 1-2 line descriptions for every dish, emphasising ingredients, technique, or origin that justify the price. "Green Curry Chicken" becomes "Slow-simmered green curry with free-range chicken, Thai eggplant, sweet basil, and house-pressed coconut cream." The second version sells more units at the same price.

### 4. Ignoring the Back Page

On a bi-fold menu, the back page (the back cover when the menu is closed) is often blank, wasted on a logo, or dedicated to a low-value information block (restaurant hours, address). But the back page is valuable real estate -- it is what the customer sees when the menu is placed on the table face-down, and it is the last thing they see when they close it.

**Fix:** Use the back page for desserts, beverages, or a "Before You Go" section featuring high-margin add-ons. A dessert menu on the back page that the customer sees while waiting for the check drives impulse orders.

### 5. Menu Design by Committee

The owner wants photos, the chef wants descriptions, the designer wants white space, and the result is a compromise that serves none of these goals well. Menu design needs a single decision-maker who understands the revenue objective, not a group brainstorming session.

**Fix:** Define your menu's objectives before the design process starts. What is the target average check? Which items should sell the most? What is the positioning? Then brief your designer with those objectives and let them execute.

<Callout type="warning">
A beautiful menu that does not sell is a failure. A plain-looking menu that pushes customers toward high-margin items is a success. Design should serve revenue, not the other way around. If your designer cannot explain how each layout decision affects what customers will order, they are decorating, not designing.
</Callout>

## The Menu Redesign Process: Step by Step

If you are ready to redesign your menu for revenue, here is the process I use with my consulting clients:

**Step 1: Run your menu engineering analysis.** Classify every item as a Star, Plowhorse, Puzzle, or Dog using the framework in [Menu Engineering for Thai Restaurants](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand). You need to know which items to push before you design a menu to push them.

**Step 2: Decide what stays and what goes.** Cut Dogs. Reprice or reposition Puzzles. Identify your top 6-8 Stars and promoted Puzzles -- these are the items your menu design will feature.

**Step 3: Define your sections and item order.** Map sections to the golden triangle (highest-margin categories in the prime zones). Order items within each section by margin priority, not by price.

**Step 4: Write descriptions.** Longer and more evocative for premium and featured items. Shorter for Plowhorses that sell on name recognition alone. Every description should include at least one ingredient or technique that justifies the price.

**Step 5: Choose your format and materials.** Single page, bi-fold, or multi-page based on item count. Paper quality and finish based on positioning and price point. Budget for both design and printing.

**Step 6: Brief a designer.** Give them the item list, the section order, the featured items, the descriptions, and the specific design objectives. "These 8 items need to stand out. Prices should be deemphasised. The right page is the priority page. Use one accent colour for recommendations."

**Step 7: Print Version A and Version B.** If possible, run an A/B test between your old menu and the new design for 2 weeks before committing to a full rollout. This gives you data to refine before the final print run.

**Step 8: Train your staff.** The best menu design in the world fails if servers do not know which items to recommend, which items have the best margins, and how to describe the featured dishes. A 30-minute pre-shift training on the new menu is a worthwhile investment.

**Step 9: Track results.** Compare average check, order mix, and food cost percentage for the 30-60 days after the redesign against the prior period. Attribute the change. If the average check went up, calculate the monthly revenue increase. If specific items did not move as expected, adjust their placement or description in the next printing.

The restaurants that treat menu design as an ongoing optimisation -- testing, measuring, refining -- consistently outperform those that design once and forget. Your menu is a living document, not a monument.
