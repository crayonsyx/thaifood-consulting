Your restaurant's location determines roughly 60% of its success or failure. That number comes from analyzing the 50+ restaurant openings I have been involved in over the past 15 years — great food in a bad location struggles, while average food in a great location often does well enough to survive and improve.

Yet most restaurant owners choose their location based on emotion: "I walked past this space and loved it," or "my friend says this area is hot," or worst of all, "it was the cheapest option." None of these are strategies. They are gut feelings dressed up as decisions.

Here is how to choose a restaurant location using data instead of instinct.

## Step 1: Define Your Location Requirements Before You Start Looking

Before you visit a single space, write down exactly what your concept needs:

**Concept-driven requirements:**
- **Cuisine and format:** A quick-service lunch spot needs office foot traffic. A date-night restaurant needs evening foot traffic and parking. A cafe needs residential or creative-district foot traffic.
- **Target customer:** Who are they? Where do they live, work, and hang out? An expat-focused concept needs proximity to expat-dense neighborhoods. A Thai fine dining concept needs proximity to affluent Thai residential areas.
- **Price point:** Your average check determines the minimum income level of your surrounding population. A 2,000 THB/person restaurant will not work in a neighborhood where the average salary is 25,000 THB/month.

**Physical requirements:**
- **Size:** How many square meters does your concept need? (Include kitchen, storage, and customer area.)
- **Ceiling height:** Full kitchens with exhaust hoods need at least 3 meters. Some older Bangkok shophouses have 2.5-meter ceilings that cannot accommodate commercial kitchen ventilation.
- **Electrical capacity:** A full commercial kitchen needs 3-phase power (at least 45 amps). Many Bangkok shophouses have single-phase only, and upgrading costs 30,000-100,000 THB.
- **Water and drainage:** Grease trap installation is required by Bangkok municipal code. You need proper drainage access.
- **Ventilation/exhaust:** Can you route an exhaust duct to the exterior? Some buildings restrict this, particularly upper-floor units and shopping mall spaces.
- **Loading/delivery access:** How will suppliers deliver ingredients? A restaurant that requires carrying 50 kg of produce through a narrow staircase will pay a premium in delivery surcharges.

**Financial requirements:**
- **Maximum rent:** Rent should not exceed 10-15% of your projected monthly revenue. If you project 1 million THB/month in revenue, your rent should be 100,000-150,000 THB/month maximum.
- **Deposit capacity:** Most Bangkok commercial leases require 3 months' rent as a security deposit plus key money (a one-time payment of 1-6 months' rent in prime locations).
- **Renovation budget:** Does the space need a full gut renovation, or is it move-in ready? A raw concrete shell costs 15,000-30,000 THB/sqm to build out. A previously renovated restaurant space might need 3,000-8,000 THB/sqm in modifications.

<Callout type="tip">
Write these requirements down before you contact a single agent or landlord. Bring the list to every site visit and score each space against it. Emotional decision-making creeps in the moment you walk into a charming shophouse with exposed brick and bad infrastructure.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Understand Bangkok's Restaurant Neighborhoods

Bangkok's restaurant geography is highly clustered. Each neighborhood has a distinct customer profile, rent structure, and competitive environment.

### Tier 1: Premium Dining Districts

**Thonglor (Sukhumvit Soi 55)** is Bangkok's most competitive restaurant district. The clientele has high spending power and is design-conscious, but competition is heavy. Rent runs 600-1,500 THB/sqm with average restaurant checks of 500-2,000 THB. It works well for upscale casual, Japanese, Korean, Western, and cocktail bars. Be warned: the area is oversaturated with certain concepts (ramen, Korean BBQ, brunch cafes).

**Ekkamai (Sukhumvit Soi 63)** is Thonglor's slightly more affordable neighbor, growing rapidly with new restaurant openings. Rent runs 400-1,000 THB/sqm. The clientele is similar to Thonglor but slightly younger. Casual dining, bars, cafes, and creative concepts do well here. Soi 10 and the area near Gateway Ekkamai are the hotspots.

**Silom/Sathorn** is Bangkok's financial district with a strong weekday lunch and afterwork crowd, though weekends are quieter. Rent runs 500-1,200 THB/sqm. Business lunches, afterwork bars, and quick-service concepts are the natural fit. The sois off Silom (particularly Convent Road and the Sala Daeng area) have a more village-like feel with loyal repeat customers.

**Phrom Phong/Emquartier area** is upscale residential and retail. The Japanese expat population drives demand for Japanese cuisine. Rent runs 500-1,200 THB/sqm. Best for Japanese, fine dining, and family-friendly concepts.

### Tier 2: Mid-Range and Emerging

**Ari** is Bangkok's most popular residential dining district. It has a strong cafe culture, a creative food scene, and a loyal local customer base. Rent runs 300-700 THB/sqm with average restaurant checks of 200-600 THB. Cafes, casual Thai, bistros, and wine bars do well here. The area between Ari BTS and La Villa mall is the sweet spot.

**Charoen Krung/Talad Noi** is Bangkok's oldest road, now the city's creative and art district. You will find converted shophouses, galleries, and boutique hotels. The restaurant scene is growing with character-driven spaces. Rent runs 200-500 THB/sqm. Cafes, wine bars, creative dining, and cocktail bars fit naturally, though the spaces often need significant renovation.

**Sathorn Soi 12/Yen Akat** is a residential area with a growing dining scene. Expatriate families, embassies, and professionals live nearby. There is less foot traffic than Thonglor but more loyal repeat customers. Rent runs 300-600 THB/sqm. Neighborhood restaurants, wine bars, and family dining work well.

**Sam Yan/Chula area** is a university district with a massive student population and growing creative scene. Samyan Mitrtown mall anchors the area. Rent runs 250-500 THB/sqm. The sweet spot is affordable casual dining, cafes, and street-food-inspired concepts. Expect high volume but a lower average check.

### Tier 3: Affordable and Suburban

**Lat Phrao/Ratchada** is a growing residential district with a middle-class Thai customer base. Multiple condominium projects are bringing new residents. Rent runs 150-400 THB/sqm. Thai restaurants, casual dining, cafes, and cloud kitchens do well here. There is less competition and more room for new concepts.

**Bangna/On Nut** covers the eastern Bangkok suburbs. Residential development is rapid and the demographic skews younger. Rent runs 150-350 THB/sqm. Thai casual, affordable dining chains, and cloud kitchens are the natural fit. The area is growing but still underserved in quality dining.

**Phra Khanong/Udom Suk** sits between Ekkamai and On Nut, with a mix of Thai and foreign residents. Rent runs 200-500 THB/sqm. The dining scene is emerging with a handful of notable restaurants. Neighborhood concepts, casual dining, and bars work well.

## Step 3: Count Foot Traffic (Do Not Skip This)

Foot traffic counting is the most valuable piece of data you can collect, and almost nobody does it.

Stand outside the prospective space (or nearby) and count every person who walks past during a 30-minute window. Do this at four different times:
- Weekday lunch (12:00-12:30 PM)
- Weekday dinner (6:30-7:00 PM)
- Weekend lunch (12:00-12:30 PM)
- Weekend dinner (7:00-7:30 PM)

**What the numbers mean:**
- **Under 30 people per 30 minutes:** Very low traffic. You are entirely dependent on destination dining (people coming specifically for your restaurant). This only works if your concept is strong enough to draw people, and you have a marketing budget to build awareness.
- **30-80 people per 30 minutes:** Moderate traffic. Workable for many concepts if the demographics match your target customer.
- **80-200 people per 30 minutes:** High traffic. Most restaurant concepts can work here if the price point matches the foot traffic demographics.
- **200+ per 30 minutes:** Very high traffic (near BTS stations, malls, major intersections). Competition is fierce but volume is high.

<Callout type="info">
Do not just count total pedestrians — note demographics. 100 people walking past per hour means nothing if 80 of them are construction workers and your concept is a 500 THB/person wine bar. Count the people who look like your target customer.
</Callout>

Count competitors too. Walk a 500-meter radius around the prospective space and note every restaurant, their cuisine type, approximate price point, and how busy they appear. If there are already three Thai restaurants in the immediate area doing the same thing you plan to do, either differentiate sharply or find a different location.

## Step 4: Analyze the Competition

Beyond counting competitors, analyze them.

**Visit and eat at the top 3-5 restaurants near your prospective location.** Note their:
- Menu prices (average check per person)
- Occupancy at lunch vs. dinner
- Service quality
- Food quality
- Interior design and atmosphere
- Google/Wongnai ratings and review counts
- How long customers stay (table turn time)

**Check their Google reviews.** What do customers praise? What do they complain about? Customer complaints in the area are unmet needs that you can fill. If every nearby restaurant gets complaints about slow service, a fast-service concept has an opening.

**Look at closing patterns.** If 3-4 restaurants have closed in the same space in the last 2 years, the problem is the location, not the operators. Ask neighboring businesses what happened.

**Google Maps at night.** Open Google Maps' "Popular Times" feature for nearby restaurants. It shows when they are busiest and when they are empty. This tells you about the area's dining patterns — is it a lunch area or a dinner area? Weekday or weekend?

## Step 5: Evaluate the Lease

Once you have identified a promising space, the lease terms matter as much as the location itself.

### Lease Terms to Negotiate

**Lease duration:** 3 years minimum with a renewal option. Your renovation investment needs time to pay off. A 1-year lease makes no sense for any restaurant with a significant build-out.

**Renewal terms:** Get the renewal option in writing with a maximum rent increase cap (typically 5-10% per renewal). Without this, the landlord can raise rent to whatever they want when your lease expires, knowing you cannot afford to abandon your renovation.

**Rent-free period:** Ask for 1-3 months of rent-free time for renovation and pre-opening. This is standard practice in Bangkok, and most landlords will agree to at least 1 month. On a 100,000 THB/month lease, a 2-month rent-free period saves you 200,000 THB.

**Key money:** A one-time, non-refundable payment to the landlord common in prime areas. Ranges from 1-6 months' rent. Try to negotiate it down or eliminate it entirely. If you must pay key money, ensure your lease terms (duration, renewal, termination protection) justify the expense.

**Restoration clause:** Many leases require you to restore the space to its original condition when you leave. For a restaurant with a 2 million THB renovation, restoration could cost 300,000-500,000 THB. Negotiate a cap on restoration costs or waive the requirement for specific items (e.g., "landlord accepts the kitchen infrastructure as-is").

**Subletting and transfer:** Can you sublet or transfer the lease if you sell the business? This matters for your exit strategy.

**Rent escalation:** Annual rent increases should be capped at 3-5% per year. Some landlords try to include escalation clauses tied to inflation or "market rates," which gives them unlimited flexibility.

<Callout type="warning">
Never sign a lease without having a Thai lawyer review it. This costs 10,000-20,000 THB and protects an investment of millions. Pay particular attention to termination clauses — some leases allow the landlord to terminate with 30 days' notice "for any reason." This is unacceptable for a restaurant that has invested in a build-out.
</Callout>

### Red Flags in Lease Agreements

- **No renewal clause:** You have no guarantee of staying past the initial term
- **Landlord termination "for any reason" with short notice:** Your investment is not protected
- **Full restoration required:** Expensive and wasteful
- **No permission for structural changes:** You will need to modify the space for kitchen infrastructure
- **Escalation tied to undefined "market rates":** Open-ended rent increases
- **Restrictions on business type:** Make sure the lease explicitly permits a restaurant with food preparation and cooking (some commercial leases restrict uses that generate smoke or odors)

## Step 6: The Mall vs. Street Debate

Bangkok offers two fundamentally different location types: shopping mall food courts/restaurants and street-level or shophouse locations.

### Shopping Mall Locations

**Pros:**
- Built-in foot traffic (malls like Central, EmQuartier, Icon Siam see thousands of visitors daily)
- Air conditioning, parking, and infrastructure provided
- Mall marketing drives customers to you
- Prestige factor for certain brands

**Cons:**
- Very high rent (1,500-5,000+ THB/sqm, or revenue-sharing at 20-30% of sales)
- Restricted operating hours (10 AM-10 PM typically)
- Limited menu and design flexibility (mall management approves everything)
- Long lease commitments (3-5 years with limited exit options)
- Dependency on mall traffic patterns

**Best for:** Established brands with proven concepts, chain operations, food court stalls, fast-casual formats.

### Street / Shophouse Locations

**Pros:**
- Lower rent (150-1,200 THB/sqm depending on area)
- Flexible operating hours
- Full control over design, menu, and experience
- Character and uniqueness (Bangkok shophouses have authentic charm)
- Easier lease negotiation

**Cons:**
- No built-in foot traffic (you must generate your own)
- Parking challenges in many areas
- Infrastructure limitations (old buildings, limited electrical capacity)
- Variable landlord quality

**Best for:** Independent restaurants, creative concepts, cafes, bars, destination dining.

My recommendation for most first-time operators: start with a street-level or shophouse location. The rent is lower, the lease is more flexible, and the operating environment is more forgiving. Mall locations suit established operators who have already proven their concept and can handle the higher fixed costs.

## Step 7: Due Diligence Checklist

Before signing a lease, check every item on this list:

**Building and infrastructure:**
- Electrical capacity (3-phase power? Amps?)
- Water pressure and supply
- Drainage and grease trap potential
- Ceiling height
- Load-bearing capacity (for heavy equipment)
- Ventilation and exhaust routing options
- Internet/phone connectivity

**Legal and zoning:**
- Is the building zoned for restaurant use?
- Are there any restrictions on cooking (some mixed-use buildings prohibit cooking that produces smoke)
- Is the building legally constructed? (Check building permit with BMA)
- Are there pending litigation or ownership disputes?

**Neighborhood intelligence:**
- Talk to 3-5 neighboring business owners about their experience
- Check with the local police station about crime, noise complaints, or any area issues
- Investigate future development plans (new BTS stations, road widening, large construction projects)
- Look for planned condominium projects — each one brings 200-500 new potential customers within walking distance

**Landlord reference:**
- Talk to the previous tenant if possible (why did they leave?)
- Check the landlord's reputation with the local business community
- Verify the landlord actually owns the property (land title deed check)

<Callout type="tip">
The best location intelligence comes from talking to people — neighboring shop owners, delivery drivers, security guards, and regular customers in the area. Spend an afternoon sitting at a nearby cafe and observe the foot traffic, the customer demographics, and the overall energy of the area. This costs nothing and tells you more than any data analysis.
</Callout>

## Real Estate Agents for Restaurant Spaces

Most Bangkok restaurant spaces are found through:

**Direct search:** Walk the neighborhoods you are targeting and look for "For Rent" signs. Many landlords do not list with agents. This is time-consuming but gives you first-mover access to new listings.

**Online platforms:** DDProperty, Hipflat, and Baania list commercial spaces. Kaidee and Facebook Marketplace sometimes have listings from direct landlords.

**Commercial real estate agents:** CBRE, Colliers, Knight Frank, and JLL handle larger commercial spaces. For smaller shophouse spaces, local agents (often found through area-specific Facebook groups) are more useful.

**Restaurant-specific brokers:** A few brokers specialize in restaurant spaces and turnkey restaurant sales (buying an existing restaurant with its lease, equipment, and sometimes staff). These can save months of search time.

## Making the Final Decision

After all the data collection and analysis, the final decision often comes down to a comparison of 2-3 finalist spaces. Use this framework:

**Score each space 1-10 on:**
1. Foot traffic volume and demographic match
2. Competitive landscape (gap for your concept)
3. Rent as a percentage of projected revenue
4. Infrastructure suitability
5. Lease terms
6. Renovation cost to make the space work
7. Accessibility (BTS/MRT, parking, visibility)
8. Neighborhood trajectory (growing, stable, or declining?)

The highest-scoring space is usually the right choice. If two spaces score similarly, go with the one with better lease terms. You will be living with those terms for 3-6 years.
