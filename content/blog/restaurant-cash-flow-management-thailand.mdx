The most dangerous sentence in the restaurant business is: "But the P&L says we are profitable."

I have heard it from owners staring at empty bank accounts, scrambling to cover Friday's payroll while their accountant insists the books show a healthy margin. One client in Thonglor -- a modern Thai restaurant doing 1.4 million THB per month in revenue with a perfectly respectable 7% net margin on paper -- could not pay his fish supplier on time for three consecutive months. The supplier eventually cut him off. He had to switch to a more expensive vendor at 48 hours' notice, which blew up his food cost for the next quarter.

That restaurant was profitable. It was also broke. And the owner did not understand how both things could be true at the same time.

Cash flow is not the same as profit. Profit is an accounting concept that exists on your P&L statement, calculated using accrual rules that recognise revenue when earned and expenses when incurred -- regardless of when cash actually moves. Cash flow is the physical reality of money entering and leaving your bank account on specific dates. The gap between these two things is where restaurants die.

This article covers how to manage cash flow in a Thai restaurant -- the mechanics of tracking it, the seasonal patterns that make Thailand particularly tricky, the payment cycles you need to understand, and the systems that keep you solvent even when the P&L temporarily lies to you.

<Callout type="info">
All figures in this article are in Thai Baht (THB). At the time of writing, 1 USD is approximately 35.5 THB. Payment terms, tax deadlines, and labour law requirements are current as of March 2026. Cash flow management is inherently specific to your business -- use these frameworks and benchmarks as starting points, not rigid prescriptions.
</Callout>

## Cash Flow vs. Profit: Why the Difference Kills Restaurants

If you have read the [guide to restaurant financial statements](/blog/restaurant-financial-statements-thailand), you know that accrual accounting recognises revenue when food is served and expenses when ingredients are consumed -- not when cash changes hands. This creates timing gaps that are perfectly normal in accounting but potentially fatal in practice.

Here is a simplified example of how a profitable restaurant runs out of cash:

**Week 1:** You serve 200,000 THB in food. Your P&L records 200,000 in revenue. But 40,000 of that was on credit cards that will not settle for 2-3 business days, and 55,000 was through Grab which pays weekly. You also placed a 120,000 THB ingredient order from your primary supplier on 30-day credit terms, which your P&L records as COGS immediately but your bank account will not feel until next month.

**Week 2:** Payroll hits. Social security is due by the 15th. VAT filing is due by the 15th with a payment. Your landlord's rent is due on the 1st of the month -- which was 12 days ago. The 30-day invoice from last month's supplier comes due. And Songkran is next week, so you need to pre-order extra inventory at premium prices.

**The P&L for the month will look fine.** Revenue covers expenses with margin to spare. But the cash timing created a 4-5 day window where your bank account could not cover your obligations.

This is not hypothetical. It is the most common financial stress pattern I see in Thai restaurants, and it intensifies during the first 12 months of operation when you have not yet built cash reserves.

### The Timing Mismatches That Matter Most

**Cash comes in slower than you think:**
- Credit card settlements: 2-3 business days after the transaction
- QR payment (PromptPay): next business day -- the fastest channel
- Grab Food: weekly settlement (every Tuesday for the prior week)
- LINE MAN: bi-weekly settlement
- ShopeeFood: weekly settlement
- Corporate events and catering: often 30-60 days after the event, if billed
- Gift voucher sales: cash received immediately, but revenue is deferred (and you still owe the food cost when redeemed)

**Cash goes out faster than you think:**
- Payroll: monthly, but the obligation builds daily
- Social security: due by the 15th of the following month
- VAT remittance: due by the 15th of the following month
- Withholding tax: due by the 7th (or 15th for e-filing) of the following month
- Rent: typically due on the 1st -- no grace period with most Thai landlords
- Fresh ingredient suppliers: often expect payment on delivery or within 7 days
- Dry goods and beverage suppliers: 30-day terms if you have established credit
- Utility bills: due within 10-15 days of billing

<Callout type="warning">
The single biggest cash flow trap for new restaurants in Thailand is the gap between when delivery platforms pay you and when you need to pay for the ingredients used to make that food. If delivery is 30% of your revenue and Grab pays weekly while your fresh suppliers expect payment on delivery, you are effectively financing Grab's business with your working capital for 5-7 days every cycle. At 30% delivery mix on 1 million THB monthly revenue, that is roughly 75,000 THB perpetually in float. Plan for it.
</Callout>

## Weekly Cash Flow Tracking: The Habit That Saves Restaurants

Monthly financial statements from your accountant are for management decisions. Weekly cash flow tracking is for survival.

I require every consulting client to implement a weekly cash flow tracker from day one. The format does not matter -- a spreadsheet works fine, and I will share my recommended structure below. What matters is the discipline of updating it every week and comparing actual cash positions against projected cash positions.

### What to Track Weekly

**Cash Inflows:**
- Dine-in cash and QR payments received
- Credit card settlements received (match to transaction dates)
- Delivery platform deposits received (date and amount, reconcile against your POS delivery orders)
- Other income received (catering payments, corporate accounts, etc.)

**Cash Outflows:**
- Supplier payments made (fresh, dry goods, beverages -- each separately)
- Payroll paid
- Rent paid
- Utilities paid
- Tax payments (VAT, WHT, social security)
- Loan repayments
- Other operating payments (marketing, maintenance, subscriptions, etc.)

**Running Cash Balance:** Opening balance + inflows - outflows = closing balance. This closing balance becomes next week's opening balance.

The entire update takes 15-20 minutes per week if your POS data and bank statements are accessible. The owner or GM should do this, not the accountant. Your accountant sees the data weeks later; you need to see it now.

### The 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast

A 13-week rolling forecast is the standard tool for restaurant cash flow management, and it works well in Thailand where seasonal patterns are pronounced. Here is how it works:

**Week 1-4:** High confidence. You know your current bookings, your upcoming supplier invoices, your fixed obligations. Fill in actual known amounts.

**Week 5-8:** Medium confidence. Use last year's revenue data (adjusted for any known changes) and your current expense run-rate. Flag any large known outflows (quarterly insurance, annual license renewals, equipment deposits).

**Week 9-13:** Lower confidence. Use seasonal averages and trend data, while keeping fixed costs at current levels. The further out you forecast, the wider your confidence band should be.

**The point of the forecast is not to be perfectly accurate.** It is to identify weeks where your projected closing cash balance drops below your minimum threshold. When you spot a potential cash shortfall 6-8 weeks in advance, you have time to act -- negotiate a supplier payment deferral, accelerate a catering invoice, delay a non-essential purchase, or arrange a credit line. When you discover the shortfall on the day the payment is due, your options are terrible and expensive.

<Callout type="tip">
Set a minimum cash balance threshold and treat it as a hard floor. For a restaurant doing 1 million THB/month in revenue, I recommend a minimum operating cash balance of 300,000-400,000 THB. This is not your emergency reserve (that is separate) -- it is the minimum balance below which your weekly forecast should trigger an alert and a plan of action. The number should equal roughly 10-14 days of operating expenses.
</Callout>

## Seasonal Cash Flow Patterns in Thailand

Thailand's restaurant market has distinct seasonal patterns that create predictable cash flow swings. If you do not plan for them, you will be caught off guard every year. After managing restaurants through 15+ seasonal cycles in Bangkok, Phuket, and Chiang Mai, here is what the annual rhythm looks like.

### The Annual Revenue Cycle

**January - February (Recovery):** Post-holiday slowdown. Chinese New Year provides a bump (especially for restaurants with Chinese-Thai clientele), but January is typically 10-15% below peak months. Cash reserves from the December holiday season start to draw down.

**March - May (Low Season Start):** The hot season. Tourism drops, locals eat out less during the heat, and university students leave Bangkok. Revenue for tourist-area restaurants can drop 20-30% from peak. Songkran (April 13-15) creates a sharp one-week spike followed by a dead week as Bangkok empties out. This is the cash flow danger zone for many restaurants -- three months of below-average revenue while fixed costs remain constant.

**June - September (Monsoon / Genuine Low Season):** Rain suppresses walk-in traffic. Delivery revenue often increases during rainy months (good for volume, but remember the margin is thinner). Tourist-area restaurants in Bangkok, Phuket, and the islands hit their lowest revenue months. Chiang Mai fares better. For non-tourist restaurants in residential Bangkok neighborhoods, revenue dips 10-15% from peak.

**October - November (Recovery):** Weather improves, tourism ramps up, corporate event season begins. Revenue climbs. This is when cash starts rebuilding after the low season draw-down.

**December (Peak):** Holiday parties, tourist season, end-of-year celebrations. Revenue for most Bangkok restaurants peaks in December, running 15-25% above average months. The week between Christmas and New Year is the highest-revenue week of the year for many restaurants.

### How to Plan Cash Flow Around Seasons

The mistake I see most often is owners spending their December surplus in January. December generates cash; the next four months consume it. You need to hold December's excess cash -- and some of November's -- to cover the March-June shortfall.

**Practical seasonal cash flow rules:**

- **Build cash October through December.** Every THB of surplus above your operating needs during peak months should stay in the account, not fund a new renovation, a staff bonus, or an equipment upgrade.
- **Set a seasonal cash reserve target.** For most Bangkok restaurants, this is 1.5-2 months of fixed costs, or roughly 600,000-1,200,000 THB for a mid-range operation. This reserve covers the gap between peak and trough revenue.
- **Negotiate supplier terms that flex with volume.** Some suppliers will agree to extended payment terms during low season if you have been a reliable payer during high season. Ask. The worst they can say is no.
- **Adjust staffing before low season hits.** If you use part-time staff, reduce hours starting in March. If you rely on overtime to cover peak periods, eliminate overtime by April. Labour is your most controllable large cost category.
- **Front-load marketing spend.** Spend marketing budget in October-November to capture the upswing, not in June trying to fight the monsoon. A promotional push in low season yields lower ROI than the same spend during shoulder season.

## Managing Supplier Payment Terms

Your relationship with suppliers is a cash flow management tool, not just a procurement function. How you structure payment terms directly affects how much working capital you need to hold.

### Standard Payment Terms in Thailand's Restaurant Supply Chain

**Fresh produce, meat, seafood (wet market suppliers):** Cash on delivery or 7-day credit. These suppliers are typically small operators with thin margins who cannot afford to extend credit. New restaurants almost always pay cash on delivery until they build a 3-6 month track record.

**Dry goods, canned goods, sauces (distributors):** 15-30 day credit terms for established accounts. Major distributors like Makro (for cash-and-carry) are cash-at-purchase, but dedicated F&B distributors that deliver often offer 30-day terms.

**Beverages and alcohol (distributors):** 15-30 day credit terms. The major beer distributors (ThaiBev, Boon Rawd) and spirit importers will often extend 30-day terms with a signed agreement and sometimes a small deposit.

**Packaging and disposables:** 30-day terms from most suppliers, since these are non-perishable and the suppliers carry more inventory buffer.

**Equipment and smallwares:** Payment terms vary widely. Major equipment suppliers may offer 60-90 day terms or installment plans. Smaller vendors typically want immediate payment.

### Strategies for Optimizing Supplier Payment Terms

**Consolidate suppliers to gain leverage.** If you spread your fresh produce across five different wet market vendors, none of them sees enough volume to justify extending credit. If you consolidate to two primary suppliers, each doing 150,000-200,000 THB per month with you, they are far more likely to offer 14-day terms. The savings in payment flexibility often outweigh the small price advantages of shopping around.

**Pay early when cash is abundant to negotiate better terms later.** During peak season, pay your key suppliers in 7 days instead of the agreed 30. This builds goodwill and a track record. When low season arrives, ask for a temporary extension to 45 days. Suppliers remember who pays early and who always pushes to the deadline.

**Stagger large orders across billing cycles.** If your beverage order comes due on the same day as your rent, restructure the ordering schedule so the beverage delivery (and invoice date) falls on a different week. Spreading out large outflows prevents cash concentration on any single week.

**Never be surprised by a supplier invoice.** Maintain a simple accounts payable calendar -- a list of every supplier, their credit terms, your typical monthly spend, and the expected payment date. Review it weekly as part of your cash flow tracking.

<Callout type="info">
In Thailand, the personal relationship with your supplier matters more than the formal payment terms printed on the invoice. I have seen suppliers extend informal 60-day credit to restaurant owners who answer their LINE messages promptly and communicate openly about temporary cash flow pressure. I have also seen suppliers demand cash-on-delivery from owners who dodge calls and break promises. Be transparent. If you will be late, call before the due date, not after.
</Callout>

## Rent: Your Largest Fixed Cash Outflow

Rent deserves its own section because it is typically the single largest fixed monthly cash outflow -- 80,000 to 250,000 THB for a mid-range restaurant in Bangkok -- and it has zero flexibility. Your landlord does not care about your low season. The lease says the 1st, and it is due on the 1st.

### Timing Considerations

Most Thai commercial leases require rent on the 1st of each month, with a few allowing the 5th. This means your largest fixed outflow hits at the very beginning of the month, before you have collected much of that month's revenue. For a restaurant that does most of its business on weekends, the first week of the month is when the bank balance is at its lowest.

**The practical fix:** Time your cash flow so that end-of-month closing cash is always enough to cover the 1st-of-month rent plus the first week's operating expenses. If your rent is 120,000 THB and your first week's operating costs (payroll accrual, supplier payments, utilities) are 200,000 THB, you need at least 320,000 THB in the bank on the last day of the prior month. Build this into your minimum balance threshold.

### Negotiating Rent Timing

Some landlords will agree to alternative payment dates. I have negotiated 15th-of-month rent payments for clients, which aligns better with the cash flow cycle because mid-month credit card settlements and the first delivery platform payout of the month have usually cleared by then. This is easier to negotiate with individual landlords than with commercial property management companies, but it is always worth asking during lease negotiation.

For a full breakdown of rent costs by Bangkok neighborhood and negotiation strategies, see the [restaurant lease negotiation guide](/blog/restaurant-rent-bangkok-neighborhoods).

## Payroll: Legal Requirements and Timing

Thai labour law creates specific cash flow obligations around payroll that are non-negotiable. Understanding the timing is part of managing your cash.

### Payment Frequency

Thai labour law requires that wages be paid at least once per month. Most restaurants pay on either the last day of the month or the 25th. Some pay twice monthly (15th and last day), particularly for hourly or daily-wage workers.

**Cash flow consideration:** If you pay on the 25th, you have 5-6 days before the month ends, which means you are paying for work performed before the month's revenue is fully collected. If you pay on the last day, you are paying with the current month's cash, which is slightly better aligned. Either way, payroll for a 16-person restaurant runs 180,000-350,000 THB per month (including service charge distributions), and the date is fixed.

### Social Security and Tax Obligations

**Social security contributions (employer + employee share):** Due by the 15th of the following month. For a 16-person restaurant, the total remittance (employer 5% + employee 5%, each capped at 750 THB/month) is approximately 22,500 THB. This hits two weeks after the month closes.

**Withholding tax on employee salaries:** Due by the 7th (or 15th for e-filing) of the following month.

**Service charge distributions:** Most restaurants distribute 80-100% of collected service charge to staff, typically at the same time as payroll. For a restaurant collecting 80,000 THB in service charge and distributing 90%, that is 72,000 THB in additional staff payments that need to be funded on payday.

The [restaurant accounting guide](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand) covers these obligations in detail. From a cash flow perspective, the key takeaway is that employee-related outflows are clustered in the last week of the month (payroll) and the first two weeks of the following month (social security, withholding tax). This is a predictable pattern you can plan around.

### Severance Reserve

Thai law requires severance pay when employees are terminated without cause, ranging from 30 days' wages (after 120 days of service) to 400 days' wages (after 20+ years). This is a contingent cash outflow that can be substantial.

I recommend setting aside 2-3% of monthly payroll into a separate severance reserve account. This is not an accounting provision (your accountant handles that on the books) -- it is actual cash you ring-fence so that when you need to terminate an employee, the severance payment does not create a cash flow crisis. A restaurant with 300,000 THB monthly payroll setting aside 3% accumulates 108,000 THB per year in the reserve -- enough to cover one mid-tenure termination without touching operating cash.

## VAT: The Cash Flow Trap Nobody Warns You About

VAT at 7% creates a significant cash flow dynamic that many restaurant owners do not fully appreciate until it hits them.

### The Mechanics

You collect 7% VAT from every customer. This money sits in your bank account, looking like it is yours. It is not. It belongs to the Revenue Department, and it is due by the 15th of the following month.

For a restaurant doing 1 million THB in net revenue, you are collecting approximately 70,000 THB in output VAT per month. After deducting input VAT on your purchases (maybe 20,000-30,000 THB), you owe the Revenue Department 40,000-50,000 THB by the 15th.

**The trap:** That 70,000 THB in collected VAT inflates your bank balance throughout the month. If you manage cash by looking at your bank balance rather than your cash flow tracker, you will spend VAT money on operations and then scramble to find 45,000 THB for the VAT payment on the 15th.

### How to Handle It

**Option 1 (best practice):** Maintain a separate bank account for tax obligations. Transfer your estimated monthly VAT liability (and withholding tax and social security) into this account weekly. On the 15th, pay from this account. The money is never available for operations, so you cannot accidentally spend it.

**Option 2 (simpler):** Include the VAT payment as a fixed line item in your 13-week cash flow forecast, and ensure your minimum cash balance threshold accounts for the upcoming tax payment.

<Callout type="warning">
Late VAT payment triggers a 1.5% monthly surcharge on the outstanding amount -- that is 18% annualized. If you owe 50,000 THB and pay 30 days late, the surcharge is 750 THB. Not catastrophic, but it compounds over time, and a pattern of late payments can trigger a Revenue Department audit. On the other side, if you overpay VAT (input exceeds output), you can claim a refund, but the refund process takes 3-6 months. Do not count on VAT refunds in your cash flow forecast.
</Callout>

## Delivery Platform Payment Cycles

If delivery is a meaningful part of your revenue, understanding the payment cycles of each platform is non-negotiable for cash flow management.

### Platform-by-Platform Breakdown

**Grab Food:** Settles weekly, typically on Tuesday for the prior Monday-Sunday period. Payment is net of commission (30-35%), merchant-funded promotions, refunds, and adjustments. The settlement statement can be opaque -- reconcile it against your POS delivery orders weekly.

**LINE MAN:** Settles bi-weekly (every two weeks). This is the longest payment cycle among major platforms, meaning up to 14 days of revenue is in float at any given time. For a restaurant doing 150,000 THB/month through LINE MAN, that is 37,500-75,000 THB perpetually held by the platform.

**ShopeeFood:** Weekly settlement, similar to Grab. Payment net of commission and adjustments.

**Robinhood:** Weekly settlement. Robinhood's commission rates are lower (typically 20-25%) since it was launched as a "fair platform" initiative, but its order volume is significantly lower than Grab or LINE MAN in most areas.

## Credit Card and Payment Processing Delays

Beyond delivery platforms, standard payment processing introduces its own delays.

**Credit cards (Visa, Mastercard):** Settlement typically takes 2-3 business days from the transaction date. If a customer pays on Friday evening, you receive the funds on Tuesday or Wednesday. Weekend transactions create a built-in 2-day additional delay.

**QR payment (PromptPay):** Next business day settlement in most cases. This is the fastest settlement channel and one reason to encourage QR payments.

**TrueMoney, Rabbit LINE Pay, and other e-wallets:** Settlement varies by provider, typically 2-5 business days.

**Cash:** Immediate, obviously. But cash brings its own management costs (shrinkage risk, bank deposit time, counting and reconciliation labor). For a restaurant doing 30% cash transactions, tight cash handling procedures are part of cash flow management.

**Aggregate impact:** A restaurant doing 1 million THB in monthly revenue with 50% card, 20% QR, 15% delivery, and 15% cash has roughly 650,000 THB per month subject to settlement delays of 1-7 days. At any point, 50,000-150,000 THB is in transit between your customers' payments and your bank account. Your cash flow forecast needs to account for this float.

## Building a Cash Reserve: How Much Is Enough?

Every restaurant needs a cash reserve. The question is how much, and the answer depends on your risk profile, your cost structure, and how much seasonal variation you face.

### The Three-Layer Reserve

I recommend a three-layer approach:

**Layer 1 -- Operating Buffer (always maintain):** 10-14 days of total operating expenses. For a restaurant with 850,000 THB/month in total outflows, this is 280,000-400,000 THB. This covers normal timing fluctuations -- a late delivery platform payment, a supplier invoice that hits earlier than expected, a slow week.

**Layer 2 -- Seasonal Reserve (build during peak, draw during low):** 1.5-2 months of fixed costs. For a restaurant with 500,000 THB/month in fixed costs (rent, base salaries, insurance, subscriptions), this is 750,000-1,000,000 THB. This covers the revenue shortfall during low season (April-September) without requiring you to cut staff or defer supplier payments.

**Layer 3 -- Emergency Reserve (never touch except for real emergencies):** 2-3 months of total operating expenses, or 1,700,000-2,550,000 THB for our example restaurant. This covers catastrophic scenarios: a kitchen fire, a flood, a pandemic-related closure, loss of a liquor license, or a legal dispute with your landlord. This money should ideally sit in a separate high-interest savings account and should only be accessed for genuine emergencies.

### Building the Reserve

Most new restaurants cannot start with a full three-layer reserve. That is fine. Build it in order:

**Months 1-6:** Focus on Layer 1. Reach your 10-14 day operating buffer. This should be funded partially from startup capital and partially from early operating surplus.

**Months 7-18:** Build Layer 2. Allocate 5-10% of monthly net revenue to the seasonal reserve until you reach the target. If your restaurant generates 60,000 THB/month in net profit, putting 50,000 THB toward the reserve means the owner takes very little home during this period. That is the cost of building a sustainable business.

**Year 2 onward:** Build Layer 3 gradually. 3-5% of monthly net revenue into the emergency fund. This takes time -- possibly 2-3 years to reach the full target. But even a partial emergency reserve (say, 1 month of expenses) is dramatically better than nothing.

<Callout type="tip">
Open a separate bank account for each reserve layer. Label them clearly. When you look at your operating account and see the balance, it should reflect only the cash available for operations -- not money earmarked for tax payments, seasonal coverage, or emergencies. Commingling reserve cash with operating cash defeats the purpose because you will spend it without realizing.
</Callout>

## Managing Multiple Revenue Streams

A modern Thai restaurant often has four or five distinct revenue streams, each with different cash flow characteristics:

**Dine-in food and beverage:** Collected daily through a mix of cash, cards, and QR. Settlement within 0-3 days. Relatively predictable volume with weekly patterns (weekends higher).

**Delivery (Grab, LINE MAN, etc.):** Collected weekly or bi-weekly, net of commissions. Higher COGS per THB of revenue due to packaging costs and promotional contributions. Settlement delays of 5-14 days.

**Service charge:** Collected with dine-in transactions, but 80-100% flows through to staff. This is a pass-through that temporarily inflates your cash position before payday.

**Corporate events and catering:** Lump-sum income, often 30-50% deposit upfront with the balance due on the event date or within 30 days. Revenue is lumpy and unpredictable -- a single 200,000 THB event can make or break a month's cash position.

**Other (merchandise, cooking classes, vouchers):** Small and variable. Voucher sales create a timing mismatch (cash received now, food cost incurred later).

### The Multi-Stream Forecasting Challenge

Each stream needs its own line in your cash flow forecast because the timing differs. A restaurant that forecasts a single "revenue" line will consistently mispredict cash inflows. The dine-in component arrives within days; the delivery component arrives 1-2 weeks later; the corporate event payment may arrive 30-60 days after the work is done.

I recommend tracking cash inflows by stream and reconciling weekly. If your POS reports 120,000 THB in Grab orders for the week, you should see approximately 80,000-84,000 THB (net of commission) hit your bank account the following Tuesday. If it does not match, investigate immediately -- platform deductions for refunds, promotional contributions, and "adjustments" are common sources of discrepancy.

## Emergency Cash Strategies

Despite your best planning, there will be weeks when cash is tighter than projected. Here are your options, ranked from best to worst:

### Good Options

**Accelerate receivables.** If you have corporate clients with outstanding invoices, call them. A polite "we are reconciling our accounts, could you process this invoice this week?" often works. If you have pending delivery platform settlements, check whether the platform offers early payout (Grab introduced this feature in some markets).

**Delay non-essential purchases.** That equipment upgrade, the new set of plates, the marketing photo shoot -- can it wait two weeks? Deferring discretionary spending by 14 days costs nothing but buys time.

**Negotiate a temporary supplier extension.** Call your top 2-3 suppliers and ask for a one-time 15-day extension. If you have been a reliable payer and communicate proactively, most will agree. Do this before the invoice is overdue, not after.

### Acceptable Options

**Draw on your seasonal reserve (Layer 2).** This is what it is for. Replace the funds when cash flow recovers.

**Use a business credit card for short-term float.** Pay the full balance when the cash arrives. This costs nothing if cleared within the grace period. It costs 18-24% annualized if you carry a balance, so use this with discipline.

**Negotiate a supplier payment plan.** If you owe a large amount to a single supplier, propose a structured repayment over 2-3 months. Suppliers prefer to get paid slowly than not at all, and most will work with you if you initiate the conversation.

### Last-Resort Options

**Short-term bank overdraft.** If your business has an overdraft facility (most Thai business bank accounts can be set up with one after 1-2 years of operating history), draw on it. The interest rate is typically MRR + 2-3% (currently around 9-10% per year). It is expensive but available instantly.

**Owner capital injection.** The owner puts personal money into the business as a shareholder loan. This is common and sometimes necessary, but it should be documented properly (loan agreement, interest rate at or above the Revenue Department's deemed rate) and it should be a bridge, not a pattern. If you are injecting personal capital every quarter, the business model has a structural cash flow problem that needs to be addressed at the root.

**Do NOT default on tax payments to fund operations.** The Revenue Department's surcharges (1.5% per month for VAT, 2% per month for social security) are steep, and a pattern of late payments triggers audits. Pay the Revenue Department first, suppliers second, and other creditors third.

<Callout type="warning">
I have seen restaurant owners take high-interest personal loans (from informal lenders or even credit card cash advances at 24-28% APR) to cover temporary business cash shortfalls. This is almost never the right answer. If your business consistently cannot fund operations from its own cash flow, the problem is structural -- your revenue is too low, your costs are too high, or your business model does not work. More debt does not fix a broken model; it accelerates the failure.
</Callout>

## Common Cash Flow Mistakes in Thai Restaurants

These are the patterns I see repeatedly, and they are all avoidable.

### Mistake 1: Confusing the Bank Balance with Cash Available

Your bank balance includes VAT you owe, service charge you owe staff, delivery platform revenue that has not been reconciled, and possibly customer deposits for future events. The actual cash available for operations is your bank balance minus all these obligations. A restaurant with 500,000 THB in the bank might have only 350,000 THB available after accounting for owed taxes, upcoming payroll, and service charge distribution.

### Mistake 2: Ignoring Delivery Platform Reconciliation

Every week, every delivery platform. Reconcile the settlement amount against your POS delivery records. I have found discrepancies of 5,000-15,000 THB per month for clients -- sometimes in the restaurant's favor, sometimes not. These are usually caused by unnotified refunds, promotional cost-sharing deductions, or incorrect commission calculations. If you do not reconcile, you are leaving money on the table or paying for errors you did not catch.

### Mistake 3: No Separation Between Operating Cash and Reserve Cash

When all cash sits in one account, the reserve gets spent on operations imperceptibly. You never consciously decide to raid the emergency fund -- it just disappears through incremental overspending. Separate accounts solve this.

### Mistake 4: Over-Investing During Peak Season

December was great. Revenue hit 1.5 million. Time to renovate the bar area! Buy that new oven! Hire two more staff!

No. December's surplus funds March through June. Capital investments should be planned, budgeted, and timed for when you have built sufficient reserves -- not spent impulsively because the cash happens to be in the account. I have seen more cash flow crises caused by ill-timed capex than by poor revenue.

### Mistake 5: Not Forecasting Tax Obligations

Your mid-year corporate income tax estimate (PND.51, due August 31st) and your annual return (PND.50, due May 31st) can create substantial one-time cash outflows. A restaurant with 800,000 THB in annual net profit owes approximately 75,000-105,000 THB in CIT. If you have not been accruing for this, the May payment is a shock. The [accounting guide](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand) covers the rates and deadlines.

### Mistake 6: Extending Too Much Credit to Corporate Clients

Corporate event bookings are lucrative, but if you allow 60-day payment terms on a 200,000 THB event, you have spent the food cost (60,000-70,000 THB), the labour cost, and the consumables -- and you are waiting two months for the cash. For large events, always collect a 50% deposit upfront and the balance within 7 days of the event. The corporate client's procurement department may push back, but your cash flow depends on holding the line.

## Tools and Systems for Cash Flow Tracking

You do not need expensive software to manage restaurant cash flow. Here is what works.

### The Simple Spreadsheet Approach

A Google Sheets workbook with three tabs:

**Tab 1 -- Weekly Cash Flow Tracker:** Rows for each week. Columns for opening balance, cash inflows by source (dine-in, delivery, events, other), cash outflows by category (suppliers, payroll, rent, tax, other), and closing balance.

**Tab 2 -- 13-Week Forecast:** Same structure as the tracker, but projected forward. Update weekly by shifting the actual week from Tab 1 into Tab 2 and extending the forecast by one week.

**Tab 3 -- Accounts Payable Calendar:** List of every supplier and recurring obligation, with amount, terms, and due date. Color-code by urgency (red = due this week, yellow = due next week, green = due later).

This takes 20-30 minutes per week to maintain and gives you complete visibility into your cash position.

### POS-Level Tools

Most Thai POS platforms (FoodStory, Ocha, StoreHub) provide daily revenue reports and settlement tracking. Some integrate with bank accounts to show actual cash received versus POS-reported revenue. Use these reports as inputs to your weekly tracker -- they do not replace it, but they reduce the manual work.

### Accounting Software Cash Flow Features

PEAK and FlowAccount both offer cash flow reporting, but the reports are typically monthly and retrospective. They are useful for analysis but not for real-time cash flow management, which requires the weekly cadence described above.

### When to Upgrade

If you are managing multiple locations, or if your single location does more than 2 million THB/month in revenue with complex revenue streams (dine-in, delivery, catering, retail), a dedicated cash flow management tool like Float or Pulse (cloud-based, cash flow forecasting) may be worth the 1,000-3,000 THB/month subscription. For most single-location restaurants, the spreadsheet works fine.

## The Cash Flow Mindset

Cash flow management is not a skill you deploy during a crisis. It is a weekly discipline that prevents crises from happening.

The restaurants I work with that handle cash flow well share three habits. They track weekly without exception. They forecast 8-13 weeks ahead and update the forecast every Monday morning. And they never confuse the P&L with the bank account -- they understand that profit is an opinion while cash is a fact.

If you are opening a restaurant in Thailand, include cash flow projections in your pre-opening financial model. If you want help building a cash flow model tailored to your concept, location, and cost structure, our [feasibility study service](/services/feasibility-study) includes a complete cash flow forecast for your first 24 months of operation.

If you are running a restaurant and cash feels tighter than your P&L suggests it should, start the weekly tracking this week. The 13-week forecast will tell you exactly where the pressure is coming from and give you time to act. Knowing you have a problem 6 weeks in advance is management. Discovering it on the day the payment is due is panic.
