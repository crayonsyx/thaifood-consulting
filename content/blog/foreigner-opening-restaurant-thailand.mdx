The short answer is: not directly. The longer answer involves Thai company law, the Foreign Business Act, work permit requirements, and several ownership structures that range from perfectly legal to extremely risky.

I have helped over 50 foreign entrepreneurs open restaurants in Thailand. Some did it right. Some cut corners and paid for it later, sometimes with fines, sometimes with lost businesses. This guide covers what actually works, what does not, and where the real risks are.

## The Foreign Business Act: Why You Cannot Simply Open a Restaurant

Thailand's Foreign Business Act (FBA) of 1999 restricts foreign nationals from owning or operating certain types of businesses. Restaurants and food service fall under List 3 of the FBA, meaning a foreigner cannot hold more than 49% of the shares in a company that operates a restaurant.

This is not a gray area. The Department of Business Development (DBD) enforces it. Penalties include fines up to 1 million THB and imprisonment of up to 3 years for operating a restricted business without proper licensing.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not listen to anyone who tells you "nobody checks" or "it is fine as long as you keep a low profile." The DBD conducts random audits, and competitors or disgruntled partners can file complaints. I have seen three foreign-owned restaurants shut down in the last two years alone because of FBA violations.
</Callout>

So what are your options?

## Option 1: Thai Majority Company (Most Common)

The most widely used structure for foreign restaurant owners is a Thai limited company where Thai nationals hold at least 51% of shares.

Here is how it typically works.

The company issues two classes of shares, ordinary and preferred. Thai shareholders hold 51% or more of ordinary shares, giving them nominal majority ownership. You, the foreign investor, hold preferred shares with enhanced voting rights, dividend preferences, or both. You are also appointed as a director with signing authority. The Thai shareholders may be directors too, but the articles of association grant the foreign director operational control.

Setting this up through a reputable law firm costs 30,000-80,000 THB, which covers company registration, memorandum and articles of association, shareholder agreements, and initial filings with the DBD. You will also need minimum registered capital of 2 million THB if you plan to hire foreign employees (which you almost certainly will, yourself at minimum). This is a capital requirement, not an expense. The money sits in the company account. The whole process takes 2-4 weeks.

### Finding Thai Shareholders

This is the part that trips people up. You need Thai nationals willing to hold 51% of your company's shares.

The best scenario is a genuine Thai business partner who brings value: industry connections, local market knowledge, supplier relationships, or complementary skills. Someone with real skin in the game. The next most common option is a Thai spouse or family member. This works when things are stable, but if the relationship deteriorates, your Thai co-owner has legal majority control of the company. Always use a proper shareholder agreement drafted by a lawyer.

Then there are professional nominee services. This is where things get complicated.

## The Nominee Shareholder Problem

A "nominee" shareholder is a Thai person who holds shares on your behalf, with a private agreement that you are the true beneficial owner. This arrangement is technically illegal under Thai law.

The FBA specifically prohibits nominees, meaning Thai nationals who hold shares on behalf of a foreigner to circumvent the foreign ownership restrictions. If discovered, both the nominee and the foreign business owner face criminal penalties.

That said, the line between a "nominee" and a "passive investor" is blurry. Many Thai limited companies have Thai shareholders who invested a small amount, attend annual meetings, and collect dividends but are not involved in day-to-day operations. Whether this constitutes a nominee arrangement depends on the specific facts.

<Callout type="warning">
The DBD has become more aggressive about investigating nominee structures since 2023. Red flags include: Thai shareholders who cannot explain what the company does, Thai shareholders who hold shares in multiple foreign-operated companies, and Thai shareholders with no visible income source matching their investment. If your arrangement looks like a nominee structure, treat it as a ticking time bomb.
</Callout>

### How to Protect Yourself Legally

If you are going the Thai majority company route with passive Thai shareholders, here is how to minimize your risk.

First and most important: get a shareholder agreement drafted by a Thai law firm. This governs dividend distribution, share transfer restrictions, decision-making authority, and exit procedures. It is the most important legal document in your entire business.

Make sure your Thai shareholders actually transfer money for their shares rather than receiving them for free. Document everything. Keep clean corporate records: annual shareholder meetings, proper minutes, filed financial statements. Sloppy paperwork invites scrutiny.

Run major decisions through documented board meetings with clear resolutions. And pay your Thai shareholders actual dividends. If they never receive anything, the arrangement starts to look like a nominee setup.

## Option 2: BOI Promotion (100% Foreign Ownership)

The Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) offers promotional certificates that allow up to 100% foreign ownership in otherwise restricted industries. For restaurants, this falls under the food service and tourism categories.

To qualify, you need a minimum investment of 1 million THB (excluding land and working capital), the project must bring technology transfer, skills, or employment to Thailand, and the application takes 60-90 working days.

The benefits are real: 100% foreign ownership, corporate tax exemptions for up to 8 years, import duty exemptions on machinery and equipment, permission to own land in some cases, and an easier work permit process for foreign staff.

The downsides are also real. The application process is bureaucratic and slow. Your business plan must demonstrate value to the Thai economy. Ongoing compliance reporting is required. And not all restaurant concepts qualify. A single small restaurant is unlikely to be approved.

BOI promotion works best for larger operations: restaurant groups, hotel F&B, food manufacturing with a retail component, or concepts that include training programs for Thai nationals.

If you are planning to invest 10 million THB or more in a restaurant group or food-related business, BOI promotion is worth exploring. ## Option 3: Treaty of Amity (US Citizens Only)

The US-Thailand Treaty of Amity, signed in 1966, allows American citizens and US-registered companies to hold majority ownership in most Thai businesses, including restaurants.

You register a Thai limited company with US majority ownership, then apply for a Treaty of Amity certificate through the US Embassy and the Thai Ministry of Commerce. Processing takes 4-8 weeks and costs 80,000-150,000 THB total in government and legal fees.

At least one American citizen or US-incorporated company must hold majority shares, and you need a valid US passport. There is an annual compliance filing with the Ministry of Commerce. Only US citizens qualify (not residents or green card holders from other countries), and if the American owner sells their shares to a non-American, the Treaty of Amity protection ends.

This is the cleanest foreign ownership structure available. If you are American, use it.

## Option 4: Operate Without Ownership

Some foreigners bypass the ownership question entirely by structuring themselves as employees, consultants, or franchise operators.

Under a management agreement, a Thai-owned company operates the restaurant and you are hired as the general manager under a work permit. Your compensation includes a salary plus a performance-based bonus tied to profitability. Under a franchise or license model, you develop the concept, branding, recipes, and operational systems. A Thai company licenses these from you (or your offshore company) and operates the restaurant while you receive royalty fees. A consulting arrangement is similar: you provide ongoing consulting services and keep your IP offshore while generating income through service fees.

These structures are legally clean but require a high degree of trust with your Thai operating partner. They also mean you do not have equity in the Thai entity. You are a service provider, not an owner.

## Work Permits: The Part Everyone Forgets

Even if your company structure is perfect, you cannot legally work in Thailand without a work permit. "Working" includes anything from cooking in the kitchen to managing staff to signing checks.

You need a valid Non-B (business) visa, registered capital of at least 2 million THB per foreign employee, and a minimum of 4 Thai employees per 1 foreign work permit. The company must be generating revenue or show a clear business plan, and the permit requires annual renewal.

The work permit itself costs a few thousand baht in government fees. Legal assistance to prepare and submit the application runs 20,000-40,000 THB. Budget 30,000-50,000 THB total per work permit. Expect 30-60 days of processing time after the company is registered and the Non-B visa is in hand.

<Callout type="info">
The 4:1 Thai-to-foreign-employee ratio is enforced. If you want work permits for yourself and a foreign chef, you need at least 8 Thai employees on payroll. Most restaurants need at least this many Thai staff anyway, but plan your hiring timeline around your work permit applications.
</Callout>

### Common Work Permit Mistakes

I see foreigners working on tourist visas constantly. They arrive on a 60-day tourist visa and start "setting up" their restaurant, hiring staff, meeting suppliers, negotiating leases. Any business activity on a tourist visa is technically illegal. The risk of enforcement is low during the setup phase, but once you are operating a restaurant, you are exposed.

Another common mistake: not listing all work activities. Your work permit specifies what you are allowed to do. If it says "restaurant manager" and you are also cooking, that is a violation. List every function you will perform.

And do not let your permit lapse. Work permits must be renewed annually. If you miss the renewal window, you are working illegally until it is renewed. Set calendar reminders 60 days in advance.

## Practical Timeline: From Decision to Opening

Based on the projects I have managed, here is a realistic timeline for a foreigner opening a restaurant in Bangkok.

In months 1-2, you handle planning and legal setup: finalize your concept and business plan, engage a Thai law firm, register your Thai limited company, and apply for your Non-B visa.

Months 2-3 are location and design. Scout locations (this takes longer than you think, see our guide on [finding a restaurant location in Bangkok](/blog/how-to-find-restaurant-location-bangkok)), sign the lease, engage an architect or designer, and begin permit applications.

Months 3-5 are the build-out: renovation and construction, equipment procurement and installation, food license, liquor license, signage permit, and your work permit application.

Months 5-6 are pre-opening. Hire and train staff, develop and test the menu, run a soft launch, and prepare your marketing.

Month 6: you open.

Total timeline: 5-7 months from "let's do this" to "we're open." I have seen it done in 4 months, but that requires everything to go smoothly, which it rarely does.

## Five Things I Wish Every Foreign Client Knew Before Starting

Your Thai partner matters more than your concept. A brilliant restaurant concept with a bad Thai partner will fail. A mediocre concept with a trustworthy, competent Thai partner can succeed. Spend as much time vetting your partner as you spend developing your menu.

Speak some Thai. You do not need to be fluent, but basic conversational Thai changes your relationships with staff, suppliers, and government officials. Even 50 hours of lessons before you arrive makes a difference.

The legal setup is not the hard part. Any decent law firm can register your company in 2-3 weeks. The hard part is everything after: finding the right location, building the right team, getting your food cost under control, attracting enough customers to stay open.

Budget for mistakes. Every first-time restaurant owner in Thailand makes expensive mistakes. Wrong location, wrong menu, wrong pricing, wrong staff. Add an extra 20% above your "final" budget number. You will need it.

And get proper advice before you commit money. A two-hour consultation with an experienced F&B consultant costs 5,000-15,000 THB and can save you millions in avoidable mistakes. A conversation with a lawyer about your company structure costs 3,000-5,000 THB and protects your entire investment. These are the cheapest things you will spend money on.

## Specific Situations

### "My Thai girlfriend/wife will own the restaurant"

This is the most common arrangement and the most common source of problems. It works when the relationship is stable and there is a clear shareholder agreement. It falls apart when the relationship deteriorates and the Thai spouse has 100% legal ownership of the business.

Protect yourself with a proper shareholder agreement. Keep detailed records of your financial contributions. Consider a prenuptial agreement if you are not yet married. A Thai lawyer can advise on the specific protections available.

### "I want to open in Phuket/Chiang Mai/Koh Samui, not Bangkok"

The legal requirements are identical nationwide. The FBA applies everywhere. The practical details differ by city, though:

- **Phuket:** Higher rents in tourist areas, seasonal revenue fluctuations (high season November-April, dead season June-September), more competition from tourist-oriented restaurants
- **Chiang Mai:** Lower costs across the board, growing digital nomad population, limited fine dining market, less experienced workforce
- **Koh Samui/Islands:** Extreme seasonality, limited supplier options, higher logistics costs, smaller labor pool, infrastructure challenges

The company setup process and cost are the same regardless of location.

### "I already have a restaurant abroad and want to expand to Thailand"

If you have an existing restaurant brand, consider a franchise or licensing model. You license your brand, recipes, and operating systems to a Thai company (which you may partially own) and collect royalties. This gives you cleaner IP protection and revenue from licensing fees regardless of the restaurant's profitability. You retain the ability to terminate the license if the local operator underperforms, and the franchise may qualify for BOI promotion if it involves technology or skills transfer.

This model works particularly well for established brands with documented operating systems.

## Common Scams and Red Flags

Foreigners opening restaurants in Thailand are targets for scams. Watch out for these.

If someone tells you they can get you 100% foreign ownership for 50,000 THB, walk away. Unless this involves a BOI application or Treaty of Amity, they are setting up a nominee structure and charging you a premium for it.

Be wary of landlords who demand cash payments. Legitimate commercial landlords accept bank transfers with proper documentation. Cash-only arrangements suggest tax evasion and leave you without a paper trail.

Contractors should never request full payment upfront. Standard practice is 30% deposit, 30% at midpoint, 30% at completion, and 10% retained for a defects liability period. Anyone asking for 50%+ upfront is either desperate or dishonest.

If anyone tells you that you do not need a work permit, they are wrong. Any kind of work in Thailand, whether managing a restaurant, making business decisions, or signing contracts, requires a work permit.

And never give your Thai shareholders signing authority over the company bank account. The director (you) signs. This is the most basic protection for your investment.

<Callout type="tip">
Before committing any money, get a second opinion from an independent source. If your lawyer introduced your Thai shareholders, get a separate lawyer to review the arrangement. If your real estate agent recommended the contractor, get an independent quote. Verify everything independently.
</Callout>
