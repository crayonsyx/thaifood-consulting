The short answer is: not directly. The longer answer involves Thai company law, the Foreign Business Act, work permit requirements, and several ownership structures that range from perfectly legal to extremely risky.

I have helped over 50 foreign entrepreneurs open restaurants in Thailand. Some of them did it right. Some of them cut corners and paid for it later — sometimes with fines, sometimes with lost businesses. This guide covers what actually works, what does not, and where the real risks hide.

## The Foreign Business Act: Why You Cannot Simply Open a Restaurant

Thailand's Foreign Business Act (FBA) of 1999 restricts foreign nationals from owning or operating certain types of businesses. Restaurants and food service fall under List 3 of the FBA, which means a foreigner cannot hold more than 49% of the shares in a company that operates a restaurant.

This is not a gray area. The Department of Business Development (DBD) enforces this, and penalties include fines up to 1 million THB and imprisonment of up to 3 years for operating a restricted business without proper licensing.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not listen to anyone who tells you "nobody checks" or "it is fine as long as you keep a low profile." The DBD conducts random audits, and competitors or disgruntled partners can file complaints. I have seen three foreign-owned restaurants shut down in the last two years alone because of FBA violations.
</Callout>

So what are your options?

## Option 1: Thai Majority Company (Most Common)

The most widely used structure for foreign restaurant owners is a Thai limited company where Thai nationals hold at least 51% of shares.

Here is how it typically works:

**Share structure:** The company issues two classes of shares — ordinary and preferred. Thai shareholders hold 51% or more of ordinary shares, giving them nominal majority ownership. The foreign investor holds preferred shares with enhanced voting rights, dividend preferences, or both.

**Director control:** You (the foreigner) are appointed as a director with signing authority. The Thai shareholders may also be directors, but the articles of association grant the foreign director operational control.

**Typical cost to set up:** 30,000-80,000 THB through a reputable law firm. This includes company registration, memorandum and articles of association, shareholder agreements, and initial filings with the DBD.

**Registered capital:** Minimum 2 million THB if you intend to hire foreign employees (which you almost certainly will — yourself, at minimum). This is a capital requirement, not an expense — the money sits in the company account.

**Timeline:** 2-4 weeks for full registration.

### Finding Thai Shareholders

This is the part that trips people up. You need Thai nationals willing to hold 51% of your company's shares. Your options:

**Thai business partner:** The best scenario. Someone who brings value — industry connections, local market knowledge, supplier relationships, or complementary skills. A genuine partnership where the Thai partner has real skin in the game.

**Thai spouse or family:** Common but not without risk. If the relationship deteriorates, your Thai co-owner has legal majority control of the company. Always, always use a proper shareholder agreement drafted by a lawyer.

**Professional nominee services:** This is where things get complicated.

## The Nominee Shareholder Problem

A "nominee" shareholder is a Thai person who holds shares on your behalf, with a private agreement that you are the true beneficial owner. This arrangement is technically illegal under Thai law.

The FBA specifically prohibits nominees — Thai nationals who hold shares on behalf of a foreigner to circumvent the foreign ownership restrictions. If discovered, both the nominee and the foreign business owner face criminal penalties.

That said, the line between a "nominee" and a "passive investor" is blurry. Many Thai limited companies have Thai shareholders who invested a small amount, attend annual meetings, and collect dividends but are not involved in day-to-day operations. Whether this constitutes a nominee arrangement depends on the specific facts.

<Callout type="warning">
The DBD has become more aggressive about investigating nominee structures since 2023. Red flags include: Thai shareholders who cannot explain what the company does, Thai shareholders who hold shares in multiple foreign-operated companies, and Thai shareholders with no visible income source matching their investment. If your arrangement looks like a nominee structure, treat it as a ticking time bomb.
</Callout>

### How to Protect Yourself Legally

If you are going the Thai majority company route with passive Thai shareholders, minimize your risk:

1. **Use a shareholder agreement.** Drafted by a Thai law firm, this governs dividend distribution, share transfer restrictions, decision-making authority, and exit procedures. This is the single most important legal document in your entire business.

2. **Ensure Thai shareholders make real investments.** They should actually transfer money for their shares, not receive them for free. Document everything.

3. **Keep clean corporate records.** Annual shareholder meetings, proper minutes, filed financial statements. Sloppy paperwork invites scrutiny.

4. **Have a proper board resolution process.** Major decisions should go through documented board meetings with clear resolutions.

5. **Pay Thai shareholders actual dividends.** If your Thai shareholders never receive anything, the arrangement looks more like a nominee setup.

## Option 2: BOI Promotion (100% Foreign Ownership)

The Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) offers promotional certificates that allow up to 100% foreign ownership in otherwise restricted industries. For restaurants, this falls under the food service and tourism categories.

**Requirements:**
- Minimum investment of 1 million THB (excluding land and working capital)
- The project must bring technology transfer, skills, or employment to Thailand
- Application process takes 60-90 working days

**Benefits:**
- 100% foreign ownership
- Corporate tax exemptions (up to 8 years depending on the project)
- Import duty exemptions on machinery and equipment
- Permission to own land for the project (in some cases)
- Easier work permit process for foreign staff

**Limitations:**
- The application process is bureaucratic and slow
- Your business plan must demonstrate value to the Thai economy
- Ongoing compliance reporting is required
- Not all restaurant concepts qualify — a single small restaurant is unlikely to be approved

BOI promotion works best for larger operations: restaurant groups, hotel F&B operations, food manufacturing with a retail component, or concepts that include training programs for Thai nationals.

<Callout type="tip">
If you are planning to invest 10 million THB or more in a restaurant group or food-related business, BOI promotion is worth exploring. The tax savings alone can exceed the cost and effort of the application. </Callout>

## Option 3: Treaty of Amity (US Citizens Only)

The US-Thailand Treaty of Amity, signed in 1966, allows American citizens and US-registered companies to hold majority ownership in most Thai businesses, including restaurants.

**How it works:**
- Register a Thai limited company with US majority ownership
- Apply for a Treaty of Amity certificate through the US Embassy and the Thai Ministry of Commerce
- Processing time: 4-8 weeks
- Cost: Government fees plus legal fees, typically 80,000-150,000 THB total

**Requirements:**
- At least one American citizen or US-incorporated company must hold majority shares
- Annual compliance filing with the Ministry of Commerce
- The American owner must have a valid US passport

**Limitations:**
- Only available to US citizens (not residents or green card holders from other countries)
- Annual reporting requirements
- If the American owner sells their shares to a non-American, the Treaty of Amity protection ends

This is the cleanest foreign ownership structure available. If you are American, use it.

## Option 4: Operate Without Ownership

Some foreigners bypass the ownership question entirely by structuring themselves as employees, consultants, or franchise operators.

**Management agreement:** A Thai-owned company operates the restaurant. You are hired as the general manager under a work permit. Your compensation includes a salary plus a performance-based bonus tied to profitability.

**Franchise or license model:** You develop the concept, branding, recipes, and operational systems. A Thai company licenses these from you (or your offshore company) and operates the restaurant. You receive royalty fees.

**Consulting arrangement:** You provide ongoing consulting services to a Thai-owned restaurant. This keeps your IP offshore while generating income through service fees.

These structures are legally clean but require a high degree of trust with your Thai operating partner. They also mean you do not have equity in the Thai entity — you are a service provider, not an owner.

## Work Permits: The Part Everyone Forgets

Even if your company structure is perfect, you cannot legally work in Thailand without a work permit. "Working" includes anything from cooking in the kitchen to managing staff to signing checks.

**Work permit requirements:**
- Valid Non-B (business) visa
- Registered capital of at least 2 million THB per foreign employee
- Minimum of 4 Thai employees per 1 foreign employee work permit
- The company must be generating revenue or show a clear business plan
- Annual renewal required

**Cost:** The work permit itself costs a few thousand baht in government fees. The legal assistance to prepare and submit the application costs 20,000-40,000 THB. Budget 30,000-50,000 THB total per work permit.

**Timeline:** 30-60 days after the company is registered and the Non-B visa is in hand.

<Callout type="info">
The 4:1 Thai-to-foreign-employee ratio is enforced. If you want work permits for yourself and a foreign chef, you need at least 8 Thai employees on payroll. Most restaurants need at least this many Thai staff anyway, but plan your hiring timeline around your work permit applications.
</Callout>

### Common Work Permit Mistakes

**Working on a tourist visa.** I see this constantly. Foreigners arrive on a 60-day tourist visa and start "setting up" their restaurant — hiring staff, meeting suppliers, negotiating leases. Technically, any business activity on a tourist visa is illegal. The risk of enforcement is low during the setup phase, but once you are operating a restaurant, you are exposed.

**Not listing all work activities.** Your work permit specifies what you are allowed to do. If it says "restaurant manager" and you are also cooking, that is a violation. List every function you will perform.

**Letting it lapse.** Work permits must be renewed annually. If you miss the renewal window, you are working illegally until it is renewed. Set calendar reminders 60 days in advance.

## Five Things I Wish Every Foreign Client Knew Before Starting

**1. Your Thai partner matters more than your concept.** A brilliant restaurant concept with a bad Thai partner will fail. A mediocre concept with a trustworthy, competent Thai partner can succeed. Spend as much time vetting your partner as you spend developing your menu.

**2. Speak some Thai.** You do not need to be fluent, but basic conversational Thai transforms your relationships with staff, suppliers, and government officials. Even 50 hours of lessons before you arrive makes a difference.

**3. The legal setup is not the hard part.** Any decent law firm can register your company in 2-3 weeks. The hard part is everything that comes after — finding the right location, building the right team, getting your food cost under control, and attracting enough customers to stay open.

**4. Budget for mistakes.** Every first-time restaurant owner in Thailand makes expensive mistakes. Wrong location, wrong menu, wrong pricing, wrong staff. Budget an extra 20% above your "final" budget number. You will need it.

**5. Get proper advice before you commit money.** A two-hour consultation with an experienced F&B consultant costs 5,000-15,000 THB and can save you millions in avoidable mistakes. A conversation with a lawyer about your company structure costs 3,000-5,000 THB and protects your entire investment. These are the cheapest things you will spend money on.

## Specific Situations

### "My Thai girlfriend/wife will own the restaurant"

This is the most common arrangement and the most common source of problems. It works when the relationship is stable and there is a clear shareholder agreement. It falls apart when the relationship deteriorates and the Thai spouse has 100% legal ownership of the business.

Protect yourself: use a proper shareholder agreement, keep detailed records of your financial contributions, and consider a prenuptial agreement if you are not yet married. A Thai lawyer can advise on the specific protections available.

### "I want to open in Phuket/Chiang Mai/Koh Samui, not Bangkok"

The legal requirements are identical nationwide — the FBA applies everywhere. However, the practical details differ:

- **Phuket:** Higher rents in tourist areas, seasonal revenue fluctuations (high season November-April, dead season June-September), more competition from tourist-oriented restaurants
- **Chiang Mai:** Lower costs across the board, growing digital nomad population, limited fine dining market, less experienced workforce
- **Koh Samui/Islands:** Extreme seasonality, limited supplier options, higher logistics costs, smaller labor pool, infrastructure challenges

The company setup process and cost are the same regardless of location.

### "I already have a restaurant abroad and want to expand to Thailand"

If you have an existing restaurant brand, consider a franchise or licensing model. You license your brand, recipes, and operating systems to a Thai company (which you may partially own) and collect royalties. This provides:

- Cleaner IP protection
- Revenue from licensing fees regardless of the restaurant's profitability
- Ability to terminate the license if the local operator underperforms
- Potential BOI promotion if the franchise involves technology or skills transfer

This model works particularly well for established brands with documented operating systems.

## Common Scams and Red Flags

Foreigners opening restaurants in Thailand are targets for scams. Watch out for:

**"We can get you 100% foreign ownership for 50,000 THB."** Unless this involves a BOI application or Treaty of Amity, someone is setting up a nominee structure and charging you a premium for it.

**Landlords who demand cash payments.** Legitimate commercial landlords accept bank transfers with proper documentation. Cash-only arrangements suggest tax evasion and leave you without a paper trail.

**Contractors who request full payment upfront.** Standard practice is 30% deposit, 30% at midpoint, 30% at completion, and 10% retained for a defects liability period. Anyone asking for 50%+ upfront is either desperate or dishonest.

**"You do not need a work permit for this."** If you are performing any kind of work in Thailand — managing a restaurant, making business decisions, signing contracts — you need a work permit. Full stop.

**Thai shareholders who want signing authority.** Your Thai shareholders do not need and should not have signing authority over the company bank account. The director (you) signs. This is the most basic protection for your investment.

<Callout type="tip">
Before committing any money, get a second opinion from an independent source. If your lawyer introduced your Thai shareholders, get a separate lawyer to review the arrangement. If your real estate agent recommended the contractor, get an independent quote. Verify everything independently.
</Callout>
