A chef I work with spent four months planning a new Italian-Thai tasting menu that featured burrata from Puglia, a specific Calabrian chili oil, and a Sardinian bottarga that does not exist anywhere in the Thai domestic supply chain. He sourced suppliers in Italy, negotiated pricing, and calculated his food costs to the decimal. Then he tried to actually import the products and discovered that the burrata required an FDA import license he did not have, the chili oil was held at customs for 3 weeks because the label did not include Thai-language nutritional information, and the bottarga arrived room-temperature after a cold chain failure during the handoff at Suvarnabhumi.

He lost about 85,000 THB in product and 4 months of timeline. The menu launched 6 months late with substitute ingredients sourced locally.

That story plays out in different versions across Bangkok's restaurant scene every year. Importing food into Thailand is not impossible -- hundreds of restaurants and importers do it successfully every day. But the regulatory framework is specific, the documentation requirements are strict, and the logistics of getting temperature-sensitive food products across international borders and through Thai customs in good condition requires knowledge that most restaurant operators do not have until they learn it the hard way.

This article covers when importing makes sense versus using local alternatives, Thailand's food import regulatory framework, the step-by-step process for FDA registration, documentation requirements, customs procedures and duties, cold chain logistics, common imported ingredients and their real costs, and the mistakes that cost operators the most money and time.

## When Importing Makes Sense vs. Local Alternatives

Before committing to an import supply chain, ask whether you actually need to import.

**Import when:** The ingredient genuinely does not exist in Thailand at the quality level your concept requires. Real Parmigiano-Reggiano DOP, Japanese A5 wagyu from a specific prefecture, French butter with 84% fat content for laminated dough, specific wine labels for your curated list -- these either come from their origin country or they are not the real product.

**Do not import when:** A local alternative exists at acceptable quality and the import premium does not translate to a menu price your customers will pay. Thailand produces excellent dairy (Dairy Home's buffalo mozzarella), high-quality pork and chicken (S-Pure, Betagro premium lines), and a growing range of European-style vegetables and herbs from Royal Project farms and contract growers. Many Bangkok restaurants import commodity-grade products that are available domestically at lower cost with shorter lead times and no regulatory complexity.

**The gray zone:** Products where the imported version is better but the domestic version is good enough for your concept. Australian cheddar versus Thai-produced cheddar. Italian extra virgin olive oil versus a quality Spanish olive oil available through Bangkok importers. In these cases, the decision comes down to your concept positioning, your price point, and whether your customers can taste the difference.

<Callout type="info">
Before importing anything yourself, check with Bangkok's established specialty importers. Companies like Bon Fromage (cheese), Meat Premium Thailand (wagyu and premium beef), and various wine importers already bring in hundreds of products with all the regulatory compliance handled. Their markup covers the complexity you would otherwise manage yourself. If an importer already carries what you need, buying from them at a 20-40% premium over the landed cost is almost always smarter than importing directly -- unless your volume justifies the overhead. For a full breakdown of local sourcing channels, see our [supplier guide](/blog/food-suppliers-bangkok-restaurants).
</Callout>

## Thailand's Food Import Regulatory Framework

Three government bodies control food imports into Thailand. You will interact with all of them, directly or through a customs broker.

### Thai FDA (Food and Drug Administration)

**Role:** Regulates the safety, labeling, and registration of all food products sold or used in Thailand, including imported food. The Thai FDA sits under the Ministry of Public Health.

**What they control:**
- **Import licenses:** Before you can import food, you need an FDA import license (อ.17 or อ.18 depending on the food category)
- **Product registration:** Many food products require individual registration with the FDA before they can be imported
- **Labeling requirements:** All imported food products must have Thai-language labels meeting FDA specifications
- **Quality and safety standards:** The FDA sets standards for additives, contaminants, and microbiological limits that imported products must meet

### Thai Customs Department

**Role:** Controls the physical entry of goods into Thailand, assesses and collects import duties and taxes, and inspects shipments.

**What they control:**
- **HS code classification:** Every product is assigned a Harmonized System code that determines the duty rate
- **Duty assessment:** Calculating and collecting import duty, VAT, and excise tax (for alcohol)
- **Physical inspection:** Customs can inspect any shipment and hold products for testing
- **Documentation verification:** Checking that all required paperwork (FDA permits, certificates of origin, health certificates) is in order

### Department of Agriculture (DOA)

**Role:** Controls phytosanitary (plant health) requirements for fresh produce, and the Department of Livestock Development controls animal health requirements for meat and dairy.

**What they control:**
- **Phytosanitary certificates** for fresh fruits, vegetables, and plant products
- **Animal health certificates** for meat, dairy, and seafood
- **Quarantine inspection** of live and fresh products at the port of entry
- **Import permits** for specific restricted agricultural products

The practical reality is that most restaurant operators do not interact with these agencies directly. A customs broker handles the regulatory interface on your behalf. But you need to understand the framework so you can prepare the right documentation, anticipate timelines, and recognize when something is going wrong.

## FDA Registration for Imported Food

This is the step that trips up most first-time importers. FDA registration is not optional, and it is not fast.

### Step 1: Obtain an FDA Establishment License

Before you can register any product for import, your business needs an FDA establishment license. This license authorizes your company to import food into Thailand.

**Requirements:**
- Registered Thai company (Ltd. or Co., Ltd.) with food import listed in its business objectives
- Company registration documents (DBD certificate, shareholder list, director ID)
- Premises inspection by the FDA (your storage facility must meet food safety standards)
- Application fee: approximately 2,000-5,000 THB

**Timeline:** 30-60 days from application to approval, assuming your documentation is complete and your facility passes inspection.

**Important:** If you operate a restaurant, you likely already have a food establishment license for your restaurant premises. This is different from an import license. You need a separate import establishment license, which can be registered at the same or different premises -- but the storage/receiving location must meet import standards.

### Step 2: Product Registration

Each imported food product (or category of product) must be registered with the FDA.

**Categories that require registration:**
- **Controlled foods (อาหารควบคุมเฉพาะ):** Infant formula, medical food, special dietary foods -- requires extensive testing and documentation
- **Foods required to have standards (อาหารที่กำหนดคุณภาพ):** Dairy products, edible oils, condiments, beverages, canned foods, frozen foods -- must meet Thai FDA quality standards
- **Foods requiring labeling (อาหารที่ต้องมีฉลาก):** Most packaged foods -- must have Thai-language labels before sale
- **General foods (อาหารทั่วไป):** Foods not in the above categories -- still need notification but with simpler requirements

**Documentation for product registration:**
- Certificate of Free Sale (CFS) from the country of origin -- confirms the product is legally sold there
- Certificate of Analysis (COA) from an accredited laboratory showing the product meets Thai standards for additives, contaminants, heavy metals, and microbiological limits
- Product specification sheet with full ingredient list, nutritional information, and manufacturing process
- Label artwork with Thai-language translation meeting FDA formatting requirements
- Manufacturing facility GMP or HACCP certification

**Timeline:** 30-90 days for standard food products. Controlled foods can take 6-12 months.

**Cost:** FDA registration fees range from 2,000-10,000 THB per product, depending on the category. Laboratory analysis for the COA costs 5,000-20,000 THB depending on the tests required. Total cost per product registration: 10,000-30,000 THB including lab fees.

<Callout type="warning">
FDA product registration is per-product and per-manufacturer. If you import Parmigiano-Reggiano from two different Italian producers, you need two registrations. If your Italian producer changes their manufacturing facility or significantly alters the product formulation, you may need to re-register. Plan your supplier relationships with this administrative cost in mind -- switching imported product suppliers is not just a purchasing decision, it triggers regulatory work.
</Callout>

### Step 3: Thai-Language Labeling

Every imported food product sold or served in Thailand must carry a Thai-language label. For restaurant ingredients that are not resold to consumers (you use them in your kitchen, not sell them on a shelf), the labeling requirements are less strict than for retail products, but they still exist.

**Required label information:**
- Product name in Thai
- Ingredient list in Thai (in descending order by weight)
- Net weight/volume
- Manufacturing date and expiry date (or best-before date)
- Manufacturer name and country of origin
- FDA registration number
- Storage instructions
- Allergen declarations (8 major allergens)
- Nutritional information panel (for retail products; restaurant-use products may be exempt depending on category)

**Practical approach:** Most importers print Thai-language sticker labels that are applied over or alongside the original packaging. The sticker must be in place before the product clears customs. Budget 1-3 THB per label for sticker production.

## Required Documentation for Every Shipment

Beyond the one-time FDA registration, each shipment requires a set of documents. Missing any one of them can hold your shipment at customs for days or weeks -- with perishable products spoiling the entire time.

### Document Checklist

**From your supplier (country of origin):**
- **Commercial invoice:** Detailed product description, quantity, unit price, total value, Incoterms (FOB, CIF, etc.), and payment terms
- **Packing list:** Carton count, dimensions, gross and net weights per item
- **Bill of Lading (sea freight) or Air Waybill (air freight):** Shipping document proving the goods are in transit
- **Certificate of Origin (CO):** Proves where the product was manufactured. Required for preferential duty rates under free trade agreements (ASEAN-EU, JTEPA for Japan, TAFTA for Australia, etc.)
- **Health certificate / Veterinary certificate:** For meat, dairy, and seafood -- issued by the origin country's food safety authority. Must state the product is fit for human consumption and meets the importing country's sanitary requirements
- **Phytosanitary certificate:** For fresh produce and plant products -- issued by the origin country's agricultural authority
- **Certificate of Analysis (COA):** Lab results confirming the product meets specifications. Some importers provide this per batch; others per production run
- **Certificate of Free Sale (CFS):** Confirms the product is legally sold in the origin country. Some Thai FDA product registrations accept this once at registration; others require it per shipment

**Prepared by you or your broker (Thailand side):**
- **Import declaration (Customs form):** Filed electronically through the Thai Customs e-system
- **FDA import notification:** Filed electronically through the FDA e-submission system, referencing your establishment license and product registration numbers
- **Power of attorney:** If your customs broker is filing on your behalf (standard practice)

### Common Documentation Mistakes

**Wrong HS code.** The Harmonized System code determines your duty rate. A misclassified product can be charged a higher duty or flagged for inspection. Example: "cheese" has multiple HS codes depending on whether it is fresh, processed, grated, or mixed with other ingredients. The wrong code can mean 5% duty versus 30% duty on the same product.

**Mismatched quantities.** If the invoice says 500 kg but the packing list says 480 kg and the actual shipment weighs 510 kg, customs flags the discrepancy. Every document must match. Reconcile all documents before the shipment leaves the origin country.

**Expired or missing health certificates.** Health certificates for meat and dairy have validity periods (typically 30-90 days from issue). If your shipment is delayed and the certificate expires before customs clearance, you need a new one -- which requires coordination with the origin country authority while your product sits in a customs warehouse (often at room temperature for non-cold-stored warehouses).

**No Thai-language labels.** Products arriving without Thai labels cannot clear customs for food distribution. Some importers apply labels at a warehouse post-clearance, but this requires a customs bond and adds 2-5 days. Better to have labels applied before shipping or at the port bonded warehouse.

<Callout type="tip">
Hire a customs broker who specializes in food imports, not a generalist. A food-specialized broker knows the FDA submission process, has relationships with the FDA inspection officers at the port, understands cold chain requirements for bonded warehouse storage, and can expedite clearance for perishable shipments. The fee difference between a specialist and a generalist is 1,000-3,000 THB per shipment. The time and product you save is worth 10x that.
</Callout>

## Customs Procedures and Duties

Once your shipment arrives in Thailand -- typically at Suvarnabhumi Airport (air freight) or Laem Chabang Port (sea freight) -- it enters the customs clearance process.

### The Clearance Process

**Step 1: Arrival and documentation.** Your customs broker submits the import declaration and supporting documents electronically. The system assigns your shipment to one of three inspection channels:
- **Green channel:** Documents accepted, shipment cleared without physical inspection. Fastest path -- clearance in 1-2 days.
- **Yellow channel:** Documents require review by a customs officer. Clearance in 2-5 days.
- **Red channel:** Physical inspection required. Customs opens and inspects the shipment. Clearance in 3-10 days. Perishable products in the red channel are at risk.

**Step 2: Duty assessment.** Based on the HS code, customs calculates import duty, VAT, and any applicable excise tax.

**Step 3: Payment.** Duties and taxes must be paid before the shipment is released. Payment can be made by bank transfer, cashier's check, or through the broker's customs bond.

**Step 4: Release.** Once duties are paid and any inspections are cleared, the shipment is released to your designated warehouse or directly to your restaurant.

### Import Duty Rates for Common Restaurant Ingredients

Duty rates vary by product and origin country. Free trade agreements (FTAs) can significantly reduce or eliminate duties for products from partner countries.

**Cheese:**
- **General rate:** 30% of CIF value (cost, insurance, freight)
- **FTA rates:** Cheese from Australia (TAFTA): 0-5%. Cheese from New Zealand (AANZFTA): 0-5%. Cheese from EU: 20-30% (no broad FTA with EU as of 2026, though negotiations are ongoing)
- **Practical impact:** A 10 kg wheel of Parmigiano-Reggiano at 600 THB/kg CIF costs 6,000 THB. At 30% duty: 1,800 THB. Plus 7% VAT on (CIF + duty): 546 THB. Total landed cost before logistics: 8,346 THB, or 835 THB/kg

**Beef and wagyu:**
- **General rate:** 50% of CIF value (one of the highest food import duties)
- **FTA rates:** Australian beef (TAFTA): 10-23% (varies by cut and year of the FTA schedule). Japanese beef (JTEPA): 25-40%. US beef: 50% (no FTA)
- **Practical impact:** Japanese A5 wagyu at 5,000 THB/kg CIF with 40% duty: 7,000 THB/kg landed before logistics. Australian wagyu at 800 THB/kg CIF with 15% duty: 920 THB/kg plus VAT

**Olive oil:**
- **General rate:** 20% of CIF value
- **FTA rates:** From Australia: 0-5%. From EU: 20% (standard rate)
- **Practical impact:** Italian EVOO at 400 THB/liter CIF with 20% duty: 480 THB + VAT = 514 THB/liter landed

**Wine and spirits:**
- **Import duty on wine:** 54-60% of CIF value
- **Excise tax on wine:** Approximately 1,500-2,000 THB per liter (varies by alcohol content)
- **Interior tax:** 10% of excise tax
- **VAT:** 7% on (CIF + duty + excise + interior tax)
- **Practical impact:** The total tax burden on wine is approximately 200-400% of the FOB price. A bottle that costs 300 THB FOB lands at 900-1,500 THB before the importer's margin

**Fresh produce:**
- **Fruits and vegetables general rate:** 30-40% of CIF value
- **FTA rates vary widely.** Japanese fruits (JTEPA): 10-25%. Australian fruits (TAFTA): 0-10%. ASEAN fruits: 0-5%
- **Phytosanitary inspection** is mandatory and adds 1-3 days to clearance

## The Alcohol Excise Tax: Wine and Spirits Importing

Alcohol deserves its own section because the tax structure is the single biggest cost factor for wine and spirits in Thailand, and it surprises nearly every operator who has not dealt with it before.

### How the Tax Works

Thailand taxes alcohol through a layered system:

**Layer 1 -- Import duty:** 54-60% of CIF value for wine, varying rates for spirits depending on type

**Layer 2 -- Excise tax:** Calculated as the higher of two methods:
- **Ad valorem:** A percentage of the suggested retail price (currently around 10-36% depending on product type)
- **Specific rate:** A fixed amount per liter of pure alcohol (currently around 1,000-1,500 THB per liter of pure alcohol)

The excise department uses whichever calculation produces the higher tax.

**Layer 3 -- Interior tax:** 10% of the excise tax amount

**Layer 4 -- VAT:** 7% calculated on (CIF + import duty + excise + interior tax)

### Practical Impact on Wine Pricing

A bottle of French wine that costs the winery 200 THB (approximately 5 EUR) to produce and sells FOB at 300 THB:

- CIF Bangkok (add shipping, insurance): ~380 THB
- Import duty (54%): ~205 THB
- Excise tax (specific rate calculation for a 12% ABV, 750ml bottle): ~1,200-1,600 THB
- Interior tax (10% of excise): ~120-160 THB
- VAT (7% on cumulative): ~135-165 THB
- **Total landed cost: approximately 2,040-2,510 THB per bottle**

The importer then adds their margin (20-40%), bringing the wholesale price to 2,500-3,500 THB. Your restaurant adds its markup (typically 2.5-3.5x wholesale for wine), and the bottle lists at 6,000-12,000 THB on your menu.

This is why wine programs in Thailand operate very differently from those in Europe or Australia. The tax structure means your wine list is dominated by bottles in the 1,500-4,000 THB retail range (which represents FOB prices under 500 THB), and anything above 5,000 THB is a genuinely premium selection.

<Callout type="warning">
If you are planning to import wine or spirits directly (rather than buying from an established Thai importer), you need a separate excise tax license in addition to your FDA import license. The excise license application requires a security deposit (typically 50,000-200,000 THB depending on projected import volume), and the compliance requirements are stricter than standard food imports. For most restaurants, buying from licensed importers like Wine Connection, BBC, or specialist merchants is simpler and often cheaper after you factor in the compliance overhead of direct importing.
</Callout>

## Cold Chain Import Logistics

Getting temperature-sensitive food products from a foreign country to your Bangkok kitchen with the cold chain intact is the operational challenge that separates successful food importing from expensive waste.

### Air Freight vs. Sea Freight

**Air freight:**
- **Transit time:** 1-3 days from most origin countries to Bangkok
- **Cost:** 150-400 THB/kg (varies by origin, season, and cargo class)
- **Temperature control:** Airlines offer temperature-controlled cargo holds (2-8 degrees C for chilled, -18 degrees C for frozen)
- **Best for:** High-value perishables (fresh cheese, premium meat, fresh seafood, seasonal produce), small shipments (under 500 kg), and urgent orders
- **Risk point:** The tarmac-to-warehouse transfer at Suvarnabhumi. Products can sit on the tarmac or in a non-temperature-controlled holding area for 1-4 hours. Specify cold chain handling and use a broker who monitors this stage.

**Sea freight:**
- **Transit time:** 14-30 days from Europe, 7-14 days from Australia/New Zealand, 5-10 days from Japan
- **Cost:** 15-50 THB/kg (dramatically cheaper than air, but minimum container charges apply)
- **Temperature control:** Refrigerated containers (reefers) maintain consistent temperature for the full voyage
- **Best for:** Shelf-stable products (olive oil, canned goods, dried pasta, wine), frozen products that can withstand longer transit, and large volumes (full or half containers)
- **Minimum quantity:** A 20-foot reefer container holds approximately 20-25 tonnes. Half-container or less-than-container-load (LCL) options are available through consolidators but add handling stages and risk

### The Weak Points in Thailand's Cold Chain

**Airport handling.** Between aircraft landing and your broker taking possession, the product passes through airport ground handling -- unloading, transfer to the cargo warehouse, customs inspection. This is where temperature breaks happen most often. Request temperature logger data from your shipper (a small device in the carton that records temperature throughout transit) and check it upon receipt.

**Customs warehouse storage.** If your shipment is held for inspection or documentation issues, it sits in the customs bonded warehouse. Not all bonded warehouses have adequate cold storage. If your shipment hits the red channel, ask your broker immediately about cold storage availability and escalate if the product is perishable.

**Last-mile delivery.** From the customs warehouse or airport to your restaurant. Standard courier and logistics services do not always maintain cold chain. Use a specialized food logistics provider (companies like DHL Fresh, SCG Logistics, or local cold chain specialists) for the final delivery leg.

**Your receiving dock.** The last 15 minutes matter. If your receiving staff leaves boxes of imported cheese on the back dock for 30 minutes in 35-degree heat while they finish another task, the cold chain you maintained across 10,000 kilometers fails in the last 10 meters. Train your team: imported perishables go straight into cold storage. No exceptions, no delays.

<Callout type="info">
Temperature loggers are cheap insurance. A single-use temperature logger costs 100-300 THB and records the temperature every 15 minutes throughout transit. Include one in every shipment of perishable goods. When a dispute arises about product quality, the logger data proves whether the cold chain was maintained or broken -- and at which stage.
</Callout>

## Common Imported Ingredients and Their Real Costs

Here is what Bangkok restaurants actually import, why, and what it costs when you add up every layer.

### Cheese

**Why import:** Thailand's domestic cheese production is growing but still limited in range. Domestic mozzarella (Dairy Home) and basic cheddar are good, but aged European cheeses -- Parmigiano-Reggiano, Gruyere, aged cheddar, blue cheeses, soft-ripened varieties -- must be imported.

**Landed cost range:**
- Parmigiano-Reggiano DOP: 1,200-1,800 THB/kg (through established importers like Bon Fromage)
- French brie/camembert: 800-1,200 THB/kg
- Gruyere: 900-1,400 THB/kg
- Blue cheese (Roquefort, Stilton): 1,500-2,500 THB/kg

**Cost management tip:** Buy from established importers rather than importing directly unless your monthly cheese spend exceeds 50,000 THB. The importer absorbs FDA registration, cold chain logistics, and customs clearance. Their 20-40% margin is cheaper than managing it yourself at low volume.

### Wagyu and Premium Beef

**Why import:** Japanese A5 wagyu and specific Australian wagyu grades are not produced in Thailand. Thai domestic beef is generally grass-fed and lean -- excellent for certain preparations but not suitable for [fine dining](/blog/fine-dining-bangkok) steak programs or wagyu-specific dishes.

**Landed cost range:**
- Japanese A5 wagyu striploin: 8,000-15,000 THB/kg
- Australian wagyu MB6-9 striploin: 2,000-3,500 THB/kg
- Australian wagyu MB4-5 striploin: 1,200-2,000 THB/kg
- USDA Prime striploin: 1,500-2,500 THB/kg (50% duty makes US beef expensive)

**Cost management tip:** Meat Premium Thailand and similar established importers handle the entire chain from sourcing to delivery. Their minimum orders are typically 20-50 kg per cut. For most restaurants, this is the only practical channel unless you are running a dedicated steakhouse importing full primals by the container.

### Olive Oil

**Why import:** Thailand does not produce olive oil. All olive oil in the Thai market is imported. The question is whether you buy from an importer/distributor (Makro carries several brands) or import specific labels directly.

**Landed cost range:**
- Commodity EVOO (through Makro): 300-500 THB/liter
- Premium single-estate EVOO (through specialty importers): 600-1,200 THB/liter
- Direct import EVOO (if you handle it yourself): 400-800 THB/liter landed, but with minimum order of 200-500 liters

**Cost management tip:** Unless your concept specifically requires a named-estate olive oil that no Thai importer carries, buy from Makro or specialty importers. Olive oil is shelf-stable and well-represented in the Thai import market. The cost difference of direct importing versus buying from a distributor is 10-20% at best, which does not justify the compliance overhead for most restaurants.

### Wine

**Why import:** All wine in Thailand is imported (Thailand produces negligible commercial wine). The question is whether you buy from one of the many licensed Thai wine importers or import directly.

**Landed cost range:** See the excise tax section above. Wholesale prices from Thai importers:
- House wine (acceptable quality): 400-800 THB/bottle wholesale
- Mid-range wine: 800-1,500 THB/bottle wholesale
- Premium wine: 1,500-5,000+ THB/bottle wholesale

**Cost management tip:** For most restaurants, buying from established importers is the right choice. Wine importers negotiate volume-based excise rates, maintain proper storage, and handle all compliance. Direct importing only makes economic sense if you are a large restaurant group buying 500+ bottles per month of a specific label that no Thai importer carries.

### Specialty and Niche Ingredients

**Truffle products (oil, paste, fresh seasonal):** Fresh truffles are air-freighted seasonally (November-March for white, May-August for black) and cost 15,000-80,000 THB/kg depending on variety and grade. Truffle oil and paste are shelf-stable and imported through specialty distributors at 500-2,000 THB per bottle/jar.

**Vanilla beans:** Madagascar vanilla beans cost 30,000-50,000 THB/kg at current market prices (vanilla is one of the most expensive spices by weight). Imported through spice distributors. Tahitian vanilla is more expensive still. Most restaurants use vanilla extract (2,000-5,000 THB/liter for real extract) instead.

**Specialty flour (Italian 00, Japanese bread flour, French T55):** Imported at 80-200 THB/kg through bakery supply distributors. Available from companies like BOI Trading, Puratos, and Lesaffre's Thai operation. For comparison, standard Thai bread flour runs 25-40 THB/kg.

**Dried pasta (Italian):** Premium Italian dried pasta (De Cecco, Rummo, Garofalo) is widely available through importers at 150-300 THB/kg. Artisanal brands (Martelli, Mancini) run 400-800 THB/kg through specialty importers. Thai-produced dried pasta costs 60-120 THB/kg.

## Working with Import Agents and Customs Brokers

Unless you are importing full containers monthly, you will work with intermediaries who handle the regulatory and logistics complexity.

### Customs Brokers

**What they do:** File customs declarations, calculate duties, coordinate with FDA and other agencies, arrange physical inspections, and manage the documentation flow. They are your interface with the Thai government bureaucracy.

**How to find one:** Ask other restaurant operators or food importers for referrals. The Thai Customs Brokers Association maintains a directory. For food specifically, you want a broker with food import experience -- they understand FDA processes, cold chain requirements, and the specific documentation needed for different food categories.

**Cost:** 3,000-8,000 THB per shipment for standard clearance. Regular importers can negotiate a monthly retainer of 5,000-15,000 THB that covers a set number of shipments. The broker's fee does not include the actual duties and taxes, which are billed separately.

### Import Agents

**What they do:** Full-service import management -- sourcing products from international suppliers, managing shipping logistics, handling customs clearance, and delivering to your door. They take a percentage margin (15-30%) on the product cost in exchange for handling everything.

**When to use them:** When you need a product that no established Thai importer carries, your volume does not justify setting up your own import operation, and you do not want to manage the logistics yourself.

**When not to use them:** When an established specialty importer already carries what you need (use the importer instead), or when your volume is large enough to justify your own FDA registration and direct importing (typically 200,000+ THB/month in imports).

## Small-Volume Importing Strategies

Most restaurant operators do not need to import containers. They need 20 kg of a specific cheese, 50 bottles of a particular wine, or 10 kg of a specialty ingredient. Small-volume importing has its own set of strategies.

### Consolidation Services

Several freight forwarders offer consolidation services where your small shipment shares container space with other importers. This reduces the per-kg shipping cost from sea freight's container minimum to a more manageable per-carton rate.

**Companies:** DHL Global Forwarding, Kuehne + Nagel, and local Thai forwarders like Wice Logistics offer LCL (less than container load) consolidation. Rates for LCL are typically 50-100 THB/kg from Europe, 30-60 THB/kg from East Asia.

**Trade-off:** Consolidation adds 3-7 days to transit time (your goods wait for the container to fill before shipping) and introduces additional handling stages where temperature breaks can occur.

### Cooperative Importing

Band together with other restaurants to share a container. Three restaurants each importing 500 kg of Italian products can share a 20-foot container, splitting the shipping cost three ways while each maintaining their own FDA registration and customs clearance.

**How to organize:** Bangkok's restaurant community is well-connected. Propose shared orders through chef networks, restaurant owner groups, or your existing supplier contacts. One restaurant acts as the coordinator, and a customs broker handles the split clearance.

**Risk:** If one participant's documentation is incomplete or their products fail inspection, the entire container can be held. Mitigate this by working with a single customs broker who reviews all documentation before shipping.

### Traveler Imports (Personal Allowance)

Thai customs allows a personal food import allowance for travelers. Some small operators bring specialty ingredients back from international sourcing trips, declared at customs and subject to duty on amounts exceeding the personal allowance.

**Reality check:** This works for very small quantities (a few kg) and one-off sourcing. It is not a sustainable supply chain, and customs officers are aware of operators trying to use the personal allowance channel for commercial quantities. Undeclared commercial imports caught at customs incur fines of 2-4x the duty amount.

<Callout type="warning">
Importing food without proper FDA registration -- whether through personal luggage, informal shipping, or undeclared commercial quantities -- carries penalties including confiscation of goods, fines of up to 4x the duty amount, and potential criminal charges for repeat offenses. The regulatory framework exists for food safety reasons, and enforcement has tightened significantly since 2024. Do it properly or buy from an established importer.
</Callout>

## Import Permits for Specific Categories

Certain food categories require additional permits beyond the standard FDA registration.

### Meat (Beef, Pork, Lamb, Poultry)

**Additional requirement:** Import permit from the Department of Livestock Development (DLD), plus a veterinary health certificate from the exporting country's competent authority.

**Country restrictions:** Thailand restricts meat imports from countries with active animal disease outbreaks (BSE, avian influenza, African swine fever). The DLD maintains a list of approved exporting countries. Japan, Australia, New Zealand, and the US are generally approved for beef. Pork imports are restricted from several countries due to African swine fever.

**Timeline:** DLD import permit: 15-30 days. Must be obtained before each shipment.

### Dairy

**Additional requirement:** Health certificate from the origin country confirming the product meets Thai standards for microbial content, additives, and contaminants. The Thai FDA's dairy standards are aligned with Codex Alimentarius but with some Thailand-specific limits.

**Practical note:** Dairy products are among the most documentation-intensive food imports. Budget extra time for initial product registration and ensure your supplier provides batch-specific COAs.

### Seafood

**Additional requirement:** For fresh and frozen seafood, a health certificate from the origin country's fisheries authority, plus documentation of legal catch (to comply with IUU -- illegal, unreported, unregulated -- fishing regulations).

**Practical note:** Thailand takes IUU compliance seriously after receiving a yellow card from the EU in 2015 (since resolved). Documentation requirements for seafood imports are strict, and missing catch documentation can result in shipment seizure.

### Organic Products

**Additional requirement:** To label or market imported food as "organic" in Thailand, you need organic certification recognized by Thailand's National Bureau of Agricultural Commodity and Food Standards (ACFS). Accepted certifications include USDA Organic, EU Organic, and JAS (Japanese).

**Practical note:** If you are importing organic products for use in your kitchen (not reselling with an organic label), the certification requirement is less strict. But if your [menu or marketing](/blog/restaurant-marketing-thailand) claims "organic imported ingredients," you need the documentation to back it.

## Cost Comparison: Importing vs. Local Sourcing

The decision to import should be driven by numbers, not romanticism about ingredients. Here is how the math works for common imported vs. local alternatives.

### Case Study: Italian Restaurant Ingredient Basket

A mid-range Italian restaurant in Bangkok using a mix of imported and local ingredients:

**Imported basket (monthly):**
- Parmigiano-Reggiano (15 kg x 1,500 THB/kg): 22,500 THB
- Italian EVOO (20 liters x 700 THB/L): 14,000 THB
- Burrata (5 kg x 1,400 THB/kg): 7,000 THB
- Italian 00 flour (50 kg x 150 THB/kg): 7,500 THB
- San Marzano tomatoes, canned (40 cans x 200 THB): 8,000 THB
- Wine for cooking (10 bottles x 600 THB): 6,000 THB
- **Total imported: 65,000 THB/month**

**Local alternative basket (monthly):**
- Generic Parmesan from Makro (15 kg x 500 THB/kg): 7,500 THB
- Standard EVOO from Makro (20 liters x 350 THB/L): 7,000 THB
- Dairy Home mozzarella (5 kg x 600 THB/kg): 3,000 THB
- Thai bread flour (50 kg x 35 THB/kg): 1,750 THB
- Thai canned tomatoes (40 cans x 80 THB): 3,200 THB
- Cooking wine from Makro (10 bottles x 350 THB): 3,500 THB
- **Total local alternative: 25,950 THB/month**

**Monthly difference: 39,050 THB** (imported costs 2.5x more)

The question is whether that 39,050 THB difference translates into enough menu pricing power, customer perception, and repeat business to justify the cost. For a fine-dining Italian charging 2,000 THB per head, absolutely -- authentic ingredients are the core value proposition. For a casual Italian doing 400 THB pasta dishes, probably not -- the local alternatives are good enough, and the cost difference eats into thin margins.

This is a [food cost control](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok) decision. Your target food cost percentage determines how much premium ingredient cost your menu prices can absorb.

## Building Relationships with International Suppliers

If you decide to import directly (or want to identify specific products for your Thai importer to source), building international supplier relationships is a long-term investment.

### How to Find Suppliers

**Trade shows:** THAIFEX (Bangkok, May/June) is the largest food trade show in Asia. International producers exhibit here specifically to find Thai buyers. SIAL Paris, Anuga (Cologne), and Foodex Japan are the global equivalents. Attending once gives you direct contact with hundreds of producers.

**National trade promotion agencies:** Most food-exporting countries maintain trade offices in Bangkok that connect their producers with Thai buyers. Contact the commercial section of the relevant embassy:
- **Italian Trade Agency (ITA):** For Italian food products
- **Austrade:** For Australian products
- **JETRO:** For Japanese products
- **USDA Foreign Agricultural Service:** For US products

**Online sourcing platforms:** Alibaba (for Asian products), FoodChain ID's supplier database, and specialty platforms like TruffleHunter (truffles), iGourmet (specialty European), and direct producer websites.

### Supplier Negotiation for Import

**MOQ (minimum order quantity):** International suppliers typically require minimum orders of 100-500 kg for food products. Negotiate lower MOQs by offering consistent monthly orders rather than one-time purchases. "100 kg per month for 12 months" is more attractive to a supplier than "500 kg once."

**Payment terms:** International suppliers typically require prepayment or letter of credit for new customers. After 6-12 months of reliable orders and payment, you can negotiate 30-60 day payment terms. Bank transfer (T/T) is standard; letters of credit add bank fees of 1-3% but provide security for both parties.

**Exclusivity:** If you find a product that no other Bangkok restaurant uses, negotiate exclusive distribution rights for Thailand or Bangkok. This protects your competitive advantage and gives the supplier a dedicated partner in the market.

**Samples:** Always request samples before committing to a purchase order. Budget 2,000-5,000 THB for sample shipping (air freight, small quantity). Test the product in your kitchen, confirm it performs as expected with your recipes, and verify that it travels well before ordering at scale.

## Common Import Mistakes

After working with dozens of restaurants that import ingredients, these are the errors I see most frequently.

### Wrong HS Code Classification

**The mistake:** Classifying your product under an incorrect Harmonized System code, resulting in either overpaying duty or having customs reject the classification and reassess -- with penalties.

**The cost:** Overpayment on duty (5-25% of the shipment value) or reassessment penalties (1-2x the duty difference).

**Prevention:** Work with your customs broker to confirm the HS code before the first shipment. Provide the broker with the exact product specification, ingredient list, and production method. "Cheese" is not enough -- they need to know if it is fresh, aged, processed, grated, or blended, because each has a different HS code and duty rate.

### Underestimating Total Landed Cost

**The mistake:** Calculating import cost based on the supplier's price plus shipping, without accounting for duty, VAT, excise (for alcohol), broker fees, cold chain logistics, Thai labeling, and inventory holding cost.

**The cost:** Menu prices that do not cover true ingredient cost, resulting in food cost percentages 5-10 points above target.

**Prevention:** Build a full landed cost model before committing to any import. The formula: **FOB price + shipping + insurance + duty + VAT + excise (if applicable) + broker fee + cold chain logistics + labeling + storage = landed cost per unit.** Add 10% for contingency (delays, damage, documentation issues). Use this landed cost, not the FOB price, for your [food cost calculations](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).

### Ignoring Shelf Life After Import

**The mistake:** Importing a 6-month shelf-life product that spends 2 months in transit and clearance, leaving you with 4 months before expiry. If your usage rate means 4 months of stock, you are buying product that expires on arrival.

**The cost:** Waste from expired product, or rushed usage that compromises menu planning.

**Prevention:** Confirm manufacturing date and remaining shelf life before your supplier ships. For sea freight imports, require at least 75% of shelf life remaining at the time of shipment. For air freight, require at least 85%.

### Inadequate Storage After Customs Clearance

**The mistake:** Importing cold chain products without adequate cold storage capacity at your restaurant. The product clears customs perfectly, then breaks cold chain in your kitchen because your refrigerator is already full.

**The cost:** Product spoilage, food safety risk, and the entire import cost wasted.

**Prevention:** Confirm your cold storage capacity can handle the incoming shipment before ordering. If your regular inventory plus the import shipment exceeds 90% of your cold storage, either increase capacity (rent temporary cold storage, 3,000-8,000 THB/month) or reduce the order size.

### Not Having Backup Suppliers

**The mistake:** Building your menu around a single imported ingredient from a single supplier with no domestic alternative. When the shipment is delayed, customs holds it, or the supplier has a production issue, you have no Plan B.

**The cost:** Menu items unavailable for days or weeks, customer disappointment, and revenue loss on your most differentiated dishes.

**Prevention:** For every imported ingredient, identify a domestic or Thailand-available substitute that is acceptable (even if not ideal). Keep a relationship with a Bangkok importer or [local supplier](/blog/food-suppliers-bangkok-restaurants) who can provide an emergency alternative. Your menu should never be one delayed shipment away from collapse.

<Callout type="tip">
Create an "import contingency menu" -- a version of your most import-dependent dishes that uses locally available alternatives. A wagyu tartare can become a premium Australian grass-fed tartare. A dish featuring imported burrata can swap to Dairy Home's buffalo mozzarella. Having these contingencies pre-tested means your kitchen can switch without panic when a shipment is delayed. Your diners may not even notice.
</Callout>

## My Recommendations

**If you run a casual restaurant (under 800 THB average check):** Buy from established Bangkok importers and distributors. The 20-40% markup they add covers regulatory compliance, cold chain, and convenience that you should not manage yourself. Focus your energy on [negotiating better prices](/blog/negotiate-suppliers-thailand) with these importers through volume commitments and payment terms.

**If you run a fine dining restaurant (1,500+ THB average check):** Import directly for your 3-5 signature ingredients where authenticity is non-negotiable. Use a specialized customs broker, invest in FDA registration for those products, and build relationships with international producers through trade shows. For everything else, buy from Bangkok importers.

**If you are opening a new restaurant with an import-dependent concept:** Budget 6 months and 100,000-200,000 THB for setting up your import operation (FDA registration, first shipment costs, broker retainers, cold chain infrastructure). Start importing at least 3 months before your target opening date -- the first shipment always takes longer than expected.

**If you are considering importing wine or spirits:** Buy from licensed Thai wine importers unless your monthly wine purchasing exceeds 100,000 THB and you want specific labels unavailable through existing importers. The excise tax compliance alone makes direct wine importing impractical for most single-location restaurants.

Importing is a competitive advantage when done right -- it gives you ingredients that your competitors cannot easily replicate, and it supports menu positioning that commands premium pricing. But the cost, complexity, and risk of importing must be weighed against the operational simplicity and lower cost of local sourcing. The best Bangkok restaurants I work with import selectively, 5-10 ingredients that define their concept, and source everything else locally.
