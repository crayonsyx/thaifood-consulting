Every restaurant operator in Thailand knows the feeling. You open your supplier invoice on a Monday morning and pork belly is up 15 baht per kilo from last week. No warning, no explanation, just a number on a page that silently erodes your margins.

Ingredient costs in Thailand do not behave the way they do in North America or Europe. There is no USDA commodity forecast you can subscribe to, no futures market for galangal. Prices move with monsoon rains, Chinese New Year demand spikes, export quotas, and a dozen other factors that nobody teaches you about in hospitality school. The operators who manage food costs well in this country are the ones who understand these cycles and plan around them rather than reacting after the damage is done.

I have tracked Bangkok wholesale market prices across 120+ ingredients for the past eight years as part of my consulting work. This article shares what I have learned -- the seasonal patterns, the price ranges, the market strategies, and the menu design approaches that turn ingredient volatility from a threat into an advantage.

<Callout type="info">
All prices in this article are in Thai Baht (THB) and reflect Bangkok wholesale market rates as of Q1 2026. Provincial prices are typically 10-20% lower. Retail prices (supermarkets, convenience) run 30-50% higher than wholesale. At the time of writing, 1 USD is approximately 35.5 THB.
</Callout>

## Where Bangkok Restaurants Actually Buy Ingredients

Before we talk about pricing, we need to talk about sourcing. Where you buy determines what you pay, and the spread between different channels can be 30-60% on the same ingredient.

### Talad Thai (Pathum Thani)

The largest wholesale market in Southeast Asia. Open 24 hours but peak trading happens between midnight and 6am. This is where the serious volume moves -- hotel purchasing departments, chain restaurant commissaries, and independent operators who can handle early-morning logistics.

**Best for:** Produce, herbs, fruits, dried goods, Thai pantry staples
**Price level:** Lowest available in the Bangkok metro area
**Minimum order:** None technically, but vendors prefer bulk. Buying 2kg of basil when the next customer wants 200kg puts you at the back of the line
**Logistics:** You need your own transport. Talad Thai is in Pathum Thani, roughly 45 minutes north of central Bangkok at 3am (double that in daytime traffic)

### Makro (Siam Makro)

Thailand's answer to Costco, but specifically designed for F&B operators. Membership-based wholesale club with 140+ locations across the country. This is where most small-to-mid-size restaurant operators do their primary purchasing.

**Best for:** Proteins (chicken, pork, seafood in standardised packs), dairy, imported goods, frozen products, cleaning supplies, packaging
**Price level:** 15-25% above Talad Thai on produce, competitive on proteins and imported goods
**Minimum order:** Single units (packs designed for commercial use)
**Logistics:** Drive up, load your truck, drive away. Multiple locations across Bangkok make this the most convenient option

### Si Mum Muang Market (Khlong Toei)

Bangkok's central fresh market. Smaller than Talad Thai but more accessible for restaurants in the Sukhumvit, Silom, and Sathorn corridors. Trading starts around 2-3am, and by 9am the best product is gone.

**Best for:** Fresh seafood (direct from fishing boats), produce, herbs, meat
**Price level:** 5-15% above Talad Thai, 10-20% below Makro on fresh items
**Minimum order:** Flexible, good for smaller restaurants

### Or Tor Kor Market (Chatuchak)

The premium market. Or Tor Kor is famous for quality -- fruits are graded, vegetables are cleaned and trimmed, seafood is pristine. This is not where you go for value; it is where you go when ingredient quality is the product, which makes it the sourcing choice for fine dining and premium casual concepts.

**Best for:** Premium-grade fruits, organic vegetables, specialty produce, presentation-quality items
**Price level:** 30-50% above Talad Thai, sometimes higher
**Minimum order:** Single units, retail-friendly

### Direct Farm and Fishery Sourcing

Increasingly common among mid-to-high-end Bangkok restaurants. Direct relationships with farms in Chiang Rai (vegetables), Chumphon (seafood), Nakhon Pathom (poultry), and Prachuap Khiri Khan (pineapple, coconut) can reduce costs by 15-25% compared to market prices while guaranteeing supply during peak demand periods.

The trade-off is logistics. You need consistent order volume to justify delivery, and you lose the flexibility of walking into a market and choosing from ten vendors.

<Callout type="tip">
The single best investment a restaurant operator in Bangkok can make in food cost management is a relationship with a dedicated produce supplier at Talad Thai. Find a vendor who serves restaurants similar to yours, agree on a standing order with weekly adjustments, and negotiate pricing based on volume commitment. A good Talad Thai vendor will call you before prices spike and suggest substitutions. That relationship is worth more than any purchasing software.
</Callout>

A few things stand out in this data. Chicken is Thailand's most cost-stable protein because CP Group controls roughly 40% of domestic production and actively manages supply to prevent wild price swings. If your menu is chicken-heavy, your food cost variance will be lower month to month. Pork is more volatile because domestic supply is tighter, and the Chinese New Year demand spike (late January to mid-February) regularly pushes prices up 20-30% for 3-4 weeks. Seafood is the wildcard -- monsoon season, fishing bans, and export demand from China can swing prawn prices by 50% in a single month.

**Imported proteins deserve special attention.** Beef is Thailand's most expensive mainstream protein because domestic cattle farming cannot meet demand. Roughly 60% of beef consumed in Thai restaurants is imported from Australia, New Zealand, or the US. That means your beef cost is a function of three things: the international commodity price, the THB/AUD or THB/USD exchange rate, and Thai import duties. A 5% depreciation of the baht against the Australian dollar translates directly to a 5% increase in your Australian beef cost. Restaurants with heavy beef menus should monitor exchange rates monthly, something most operators never think about.

<Callout type="warning">
Pork prices in Thailand are not seasonal in the traditional sense -- they are event-driven. Chinese New Year, the Vegetarian Festival (October), and Songkran all create demand spikes. In January 2026, wholesale pork belly jumped from 145 THB/kg to 185 THB/kg in the span of two weeks leading up to Chinese New Year, then dropped back to 155 THB/kg by mid-February. If your menu features pork-heavy dishes, pre-purchase and freeze ahead of these events.
</Callout>

## Vegetables, Herbs, and Aromatics

Thai cuisine uses an enormous range of fresh herbs and vegetables, and the cost profile is entirely different from proteins. Individual herb costs are low -- a bunch of Thai basil costs 10-15 THB -- but the cumulative cost of maintaining 15-20 fresh herbs and aromatics adds up, especially when you factor in waste. Herbs are the most perishable category in a Thai kitchen. Shelf life ranges from 2 days (Thai basil) to 7 days (galangal, lemongrass) at proper refrigeration.

The standout pattern in Thai vegetable and herb pricing is the impact of rainfall. Thailand has three seasons -- hot (March-May), rainy (June-October), and cool (November-February) -- and each one shapes the supply chain differently.

### Hot Season (March - May)

Water scarcity reduces yields on leafy vegetables and herbs. Holy basil and Thai basil prices rise 20-30% as plants bolt in extreme heat. On the positive side, this is the peak season for mangoes, lychee, and rambutan, and mango sticky rice becomes the highest-margin dessert on any Thai menu.

### Rainy Season (June - October)

The paradox of Thai agriculture. Flooding destroys crops in low-lying areas, which drives prices up, while abundant rainfall produces bumper harvests in elevated growing regions, which drives prices down. The net effect depends on whether the floods are mild (prices stable or falling) or severe (prices spike 30-80% on affected crops). Chillis are the most flood-sensitive ingredient on a Thai menu. In bad flood years, bird's eye chilli prices have hit 300 THB/kg, more than triple the normal rate.

Seafood benefits from the monsoon -- prawn catches increase as nutrient-rich runoff feeds coastal waters, and wholesale prawn prices typically hit their lowest point between August and October.

### Cool Season (November - February)

The best season for most Thai ingredients. Cooler temperatures reduce spoilage, extend shelf life, and produce the best-quality herbs and vegetables. This is also peak season for river prawns, which drives up their price even as quality improves. Cool-season river prawns at 800-900 THB/kg are a completely different product from off-season specimens at 500 THB/kg.

Tourism demand peaks in the cool season too. Restaurants in tourist areas see 30-50% higher covers, which increases citywide ingredient demand and puts upward pressure on prices across the board. The net effect is small (5-10% on most items) but noticeable on a tight margin.

The single biggest imported-ingredient lesson I teach clients: **Thailand's excise tax on imported alcohol adds 300-400% to the landed cost.** A bottle of wine that costs $8 wholesale in Australia lands in Thailand at 350-500 THB (roughly $10-14) after duty, excise, and distributor margin. This is why wine-heavy concepts in Bangkok need a pricing model that accounts for the tax structure, and why many smart operators are building beverage programmes around local spirits (Thai rum, craft gin) and non-alcoholic pairings.

Dairy is the other imported category that catches people off guard. Thailand has limited domestic dairy production -- the climate is simply too hot for large-scale dairy farming. Butter, cream, and cheese are overwhelmingly imported from Australia and New Zealand. Restaurants running Western menus in Bangkok typically see dairy costs 2-3x higher than comparable restaurants in Melbourne or Auckland, which means your Italian restaurant in Thonglor needs to price its dishes 20-30% higher than you might expect to maintain margins.

<Callout type="tip">
For Italian and Western concepts, explore Thai-made alternatives before defaulting to imports. Thai mozzarella from producers in Saraburi province has improved significantly and costs 40-50% less than Italian imports. Thai butter from dairy cooperatives in Nakhon Ratchasima is comparable to Australian butter at 60% of the price. Your customers may not notice the difference; your P&L definitely will.
</Callout>

## Building Seasonal Menus That Ride Price Waves

The restaurants I work with that manage food costs best do not fight seasonality. They ride it. Instead of paying premium prices for off-season ingredients, they redesign their menus quarterly to feature whatever is cheapest and best at that moment.

This does not mean changing your entire menu four times a year. Your core menu -- the Stars and Plowhorses that define your restaurant -- should use year-round ingredients with stable pricing (chicken, pork, lemongrass, galangal, coconut milk, rice). Your seasonal specials are where you capture the margin opportunity.

### A Practical Seasonal Menu Framework

**March - May (Hot Season Specials)**

- **Mango everything.** Mango sticky rice, mango salad, mango and prawn, fresh mango smoothies. Mangoes drop to 30-50 THB/kg wholesale in peak season (versus 80-120 THB/kg off-season). At 30 THB/kg, mango sticky rice has a food cost of about 18 THB and sells for 120-180 THB. That is an 85-90% margin.
- **Lychee and rambutan drinks.** Fruit costs plummet in hot season and drink margins are already the highest on your menu. A lychee mojito with fruit at 40 THB/kg is almost free to produce.
- **Light, refreshing dishes.** Customers want cold salads and citrus-forward flavours when it is 38 degrees outside. Yum (Thai salads) have some of the highest margins on any Thai menu.

**June - October (Rainy Season Specials)**

- **Prawn-forward dishes.** White prawns hit their cheapest point in August-October. A tom yum goong special using prawns at 250 THB/kg instead of the cool-season price of 350 THB/kg saves 20-25 THB per serving.
- **Soups and hot pots.** Rainy evenings drive demand for warm, comforting dishes. Tom kha, jok (rice porridge), and clear soups use low-cost ingredients and command solid prices.
- **Avoid chilli-heavy specials** unless you have locked in pricing. Chilli prices are the most volatile category in the rainy season.

**November - February (Cool Season Specials)**

- **River prawn dishes.** Yes, river prawns are at their most expensive during cool season, but they are also at peak quality. A river prawn special at 480-550 THB positions your restaurant as a destination and the margin still works at premium pricing. Feature river prawns on your menu from November through January, then phase them out as quality and price both become unfavourable.
- **Durian (late season).** Late-season durian from southern Thailand appears November-January. Durian desserts draw Thai customers specifically, and a single durian sticky rice at 250 THB with a food cost of 60-70 THB is a strong seasonal Star.
- **Seafood platters.** Cool season brings the best fishing conditions in the Gulf of Thailand. Whole fish quality peaks, and blue crab prices bottom out in October-December before climbing again. Build a seafood platter around peak-quality, fair-priced catch.

The Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, and Dogs framework applies directly to seasonal items. Run your seasonal specials for 8-12 weeks, track their sales volume and margin, and only keep the ones that perform. A seasonal item that sells fewer than 5 orders per day is not worth the menu space and the additional inventory.

## Storage and Shelf Life in Thailand's Climate

Thailand's heat and humidity are the enemies of food storage. Ingredient shelf life in Bangkok is 20-40% shorter than in temperate climates, and this directly affects your waste rate and effective food cost.

**Cold chain is non-negotiable.** Your walk-in cooler is the most important piece of equipment in your kitchen, and an undersized or poorly maintained one costs you more in waste than the upgrade would cost in equipment.

Key shelf life benchmarks in Thai conditions (properly refrigerated at 2-4 degrees C):

- **Fresh herbs (basil, cilantro):** 2-3 days
- **Leafy vegetables:** 3-5 days
- **Root vegetables and aromatics (galangal, lemongrass):** 7-10 days
- **Fresh seafood:** 1-2 days (buy daily if possible)
- **Chicken and pork (fresh):** 2-3 days
- **Eggs:** 14-21 days
- **Coconut milk (fresh):** 2-3 days (canned is 12-18 months)
- **Curry paste (house-made):** 5-7 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen
- **Dry goods (rice, noodles, dried spices):** 3-6 months in airtight containers (humidity is the risk, not temperature)

**The waste calculation most operators get wrong:** A 5% waste rate on 500,000 THB/month in food purchases is 25,000 THB/month -- 300,000 THB/year -- going straight into the bin. Reducing waste from 5% to 3% through better purchasing, proper storage, and FIFO discipline saves 120,000 THB/year. That is a meaningful number for any restaurant, and it comes from operational discipline rather than spending money.

<Callout type="warning">
Bangkok's frequent power outages during the rainy season (June-October) are a real risk to your cold chain. A 4-hour power cut can raise your walk-in cooler temperature above the danger zone (5 degrees C) and potentially spoil an entire day's worth of perishable inventory. Budget for a backup generator or, at minimum, a battery-powered temperature alarm system that alerts you to outages. The 30,000-80,000 THB investment pays for itself the first time it prevents a 50,000 THB spoilage event.
</Callout>

## Supplier Relationships and Price Locks

In Thailand, personal relationships drive business more than contracts do. Your ability to manage food costs depends heavily on the quality of your supplier relationships.

### What Good Supplier Relationships Look Like

**Consistency beats price-chasing.** The operator who buys from the same Talad Thai vendor every week at a fair price gets better treatment -- priority on premium product, advance warning of price increases, willingness to hold pricing during temporary spikes -- than the operator who jumps between five vendors chasing the lowest daily price. Thai vendors reward loyalty, and the hidden value of that loyalty shows up when supply gets tight.

**Volume commitments in exchange for price stability.** Agree to purchase a minimum weekly volume from your primary vendor, and in return, negotiate a price that holds for 2-4 weeks at a time, with adjustments tied to documented market changes rather than daily spot pricing. This is standard practice among well-run Bangkok restaurants and hotels, but most small independent operators do not know to ask for it.

**Dual sourcing for high-volatility items.** For proteins and ingredients with seasonal price swings (prawns, crab, chilli, beef), maintain relationships with two suppliers. When one is expensive, the other may have better pricing due to different sourcing channels. You are not cheating on your vendor; you are managing supply risk.

**Pay on time.** This sounds obvious but deserves emphasis. Thai suppliers often extend 7-15 day payment terms to restaurant clients, and the vendors who get paid on time consistently receive the best pricing and priority allocations. Late-paying restaurants end up on the "charge them extra" list faster than you might expect.

### Pre-Purchasing and Freezing Strategies

For ingredients with predictable seasonal price patterns, buying ahead and freezing saves meaningful money:

- **Prawns:** Buy in bulk during August-October (seasonal low) and vacuum-seal for freezing. Quality impact is minimal for cooked preparations (tom yum, pad thai). Potential savings: 80-120 THB/kg compared to cool-season pricing.
- **Pork:** Pre-purchase 2-3 weeks before Chinese New Year and freeze. Saves 20-40 THB/kg on the post-holiday price spike.
- **Chilli:** Buy and freeze in the dry season when prices are stable. Frozen chilli works well in curry pastes and cooked preparations. Can avoid the 2-3x price spikes that hit during bad flood years.
- **Curry paste:** Make large batches when herb prices are low (cool season) and freeze in portioned bags. Shelf life of 3+ months frozen with minimal quality loss.

The freezer space investment required to execute this strategy properly is 50,000-150,000 THB for a standalone chest or upright freezer with sufficient capacity. The ROI, based on price-arbitrage savings of 10,000-20,000 THB/month, is 3-12 months.

## Organic and Premium Ingredient Premiums

The organic and premium ingredient market in Thailand is growing rapidly, driven by health-conscious Thai consumers and the expanding specialty restaurant segment.

**The premium spread by category:**

- **Organic vegetables:** 50-100% premium over conventional (e.g., organic morning glory at 60-80 THB/kg vs. conventional at 20-40 THB/kg)
- **Organic chicken (free range):** 40-60% premium (e.g., 130-160 THB/kg vs. conventional at 80-110 THB/kg)
- **Organic eggs:** 80-120% premium (e.g., 8-10 THB/egg vs. conventional 3.5-4.5 THB/egg)
- **Heritage pork (Kurobuta, Berkshire):** 100-200% premium (e.g., 300-450 THB/kg vs. standard pork at 120-180 THB/kg)
- **Organic rice (Jasmine, Hom Mali):** 30-50% premium (e.g., 45-60 THB/kg vs. standard at 30-40 THB/kg)

Whether the premium is worth paying depends entirely on your concept and your customer's willingness to pay more. A casual Thai restaurant charging 150-200 THB per dish cannot absorb a 50% ingredient premium without either raising prices beyond what the market will accept or accepting a crushed margin. A farm-to-table concept charging 350-500 THB per dish can absolutely pass through the premium -- and should, because the organic/premium story is part of the value proposition.

The practical middle ground is selective premium sourcing. Use organic herbs and aromatics (the per-dish cost impact is small because usage quantities are tiny) while keeping mainstream proteins at conventional quality. Your curry might use organic Thai basil (cost difference: 2-3 THB per serving) and organic coconut milk (cost difference: 5-8 THB per serving) alongside conventional chicken. The total food cost increase is 7-11 THB per dish, which supports a 30-40 THB price premium that customers will pay for the "made with organic ingredients" positioning.

- **Tom yum goong** at 250-320 THB with a 65-92 THB food cost = 63-74% gross margin. The prawn cost is the swing factor; during cheap-prawn season, margins are excellent.
- **Mango sticky rice** in season at 120-160 THB with an 18-28 THB food cost = 77-85% margin. Off-season at the same price with a 35-55 THB food cost = 56-71% margin. The seasonal difference is 10-15 margin points on the same dish.

This is why seasonal menu management matters. A restaurant that keeps mango sticky rice on the menu year-round at the same price is accepting a 10-15 point margin compression for half the year, while a restaurant that features it heavily from March through June and removes or reprices it from September through November captures the peak margin window.

For deeper analysis of how to categorise dishes by profitability and popularity, see: [Menu Engineering for Thai Restaurants](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand)

## Putting It All Together: A Food Cost Management System

Here is the system I implement with consulting clients, and you can adapt it for your own operation:

**Weekly:**
- Review supplier invoices against agreed pricing. Flag any variance above 5%.
- Check actual food cost percentage from your POS data against your target (most Thai restaurants should target 28-33%).
- Inspect walk-in cooler temperatures and waste bins. If you are throwing away herbs daily, your ordering quantity is too high.

**Monthly:**
- Re-cost your top 10 selling dishes using current ingredient prices. If any dish has drifted more than 3 percentage points above its target food cost, act immediately -- adjust portion, renegotiate with supplier, or raise price.
- Compare month-over-month ingredient spend by category (protein, produce, dairy, dry goods). Identify which category drove any food cost increase.
- Review seasonal outlook for the coming month and plan specials or substitutions accordingly.

**Quarterly:**
- Full menu costing review across every active dish.
- Renegotiate annual or quarterly supplier agreements based on volume history.
- Plan seasonal menu changes for the coming quarter.
- Benchmark your food cost against industry targets (see our [cost to open a restaurant guide](/blog/cost-restaurant-bangkok) for detailed benchmarks by restaurant type).

**Annually:**
- Audit supplier relationships. Are you getting competitive pricing? Mystery-shop your own supply chain by getting quotes from alternative vendors.
- Review import-dependent ingredients. Are there domestic alternatives that have improved in quality?
- Assess whether your menu structure still aligns with current ingredient economics.

<Callout type="info">
The restaurants I work with that follow this system consistently maintain food costs within 1-2 percentage points of their target, month after month. Restaurants that skip these reviews typically drift 3-5 points above target over the course of a year, which on 1 million THB/month in food revenue means 30,000-50,000 THB/month in lost margin. That is 360,000-600,000 THB/year -- enough to fund a full menu redesign, a kitchen equipment upgrade, or six months of marketing.
</Callout>
