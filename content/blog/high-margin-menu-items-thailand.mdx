Not every dish on your menu earns its place. Some dishes pull in excellent margins with low ingredient costs and fast prep, while others tie up your kitchen's time and your ingredient budget for a return that barely covers the plate. The difference between a Thai restaurant running 28% blended food cost and one running 36% is rarely about how much they charge -- it is about which items dominate the order mix.

I have costed thousands of Thai restaurant dishes over 15 years of consulting in Bangkok, and the patterns are remarkably consistent. Stir-fries and curries print money. Som tum costs almost nothing to produce. Beverages carry margins that would make a software company jealous. Meanwhile, prawn-heavy dishes, imported protein specials, and anything requiring slow or complex preparation eat into your margins in ways that are invisible unless you are tracking food cost at the item level.

This article breaks down the most profitable menu items for Thai restaurants, explains why they are profitable, and shows you how to build a menu architecture that maximises your margin mix without sacrificing the variety your customers expect. If you have not run a [food cost analysis on your current menu](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok), start there. If you know your numbers and want to improve them, keep reading.

<Callout type="info">
All food costs in this article are based on Bangkok wholesale pricing as of Q1 2026 (Makro and Talad Thai rates). Provincial pricing may run 10-20% lower. Selling prices reflect the casual to mid-range dining segment (150-350 THB average check). For fine dining, multiply ingredient quality and pricing accordingly while maintaining similar margin percentages.
</Callout>

## Understanding Food Cost by Dish Type

Before we look at specific dishes, here is the framework. Food cost percentage is the cost of ingredients for a dish divided by its selling price. A dish that costs 45 THB to produce and sells for 180 THB has a 25% food cost, which means a 75% gross margin. That 75% needs to cover everything else -- rent, staff, utilities, equipment, profit -- but the higher the margin on each dish, the more room you have.

Different dish categories carry fundamentally different margin profiles in Thai cuisine, and this is structural, not accidental.

The pattern is clear. Dishes built on rice, noodles, vegetables, curry paste, and cheap proteins (chicken thigh, minced pork) are structurally high-margin because the base ingredients cost very little. Dishes built on expensive proteins -- especially prawns, imported fish, and beef -- carry lower margins because the protein drives the cost, and there is a ceiling on what customers will pay.

This does not mean you should only serve stir-fries and skip seafood. It means you need to understand your margin mix and design a menu where high-margin items carry the weight while lower-margin items serve their purpose (attracting customers, creating perceived variety, anchoring prices) without dragging your blended food cost above target.

## The Highest-Margin Thai Dishes (And Why They Work)

Here are the specific dishes that consistently deliver the best margins across the restaurants I work with, along with the food cost math.

### Som Tum (Green Papaya Salad) -- 78-85% Margin

Som tum is the highest-margin main dish in Thai cuisine. Green papaya costs 20-30 THB/kg at wholesale and one kilogram yields 6-8 servings. Add dried shrimp (2 THB), lime juice (1 THB), fish sauce (1 THB), palm sugar (0.5 THB), chillies (1 THB), tomatoes (2 THB), long beans (1.5 THB), and peanuts (2 THB). Total ingredient cost per serving: 18-28 THB.

Selling price in casual dining: 120-180 THB. That is a 78-85% margin.

The prep is fast (3-5 minutes per serving with a skilled cook), the ingredients are shelf-stable or long-lasting, and the dish has universal appeal across Thai and tourist customers. There is a reason som tum appears on virtually every Thai restaurant menu -- it is both popular and absurdly profitable.

**Variants that expand the margin:** Som tum with salted crab (poo pla ra) adds 5-8 THB in ingredient cost but allows a 30-50 THB price premium. Som tum with soft-shell crab is a "Puzzle" item -- food cost jumps to 55-70 THB, but you can price it at 250-320 THB because the premium ingredient justifies the price while maintaining a 72-78% margin.

### Pad Kra Pao (Stir-Fried Basil) -- 72-80% Margin

Thailand's national comfort dish. Minced pork (200g at 24-30 THB), holy basil (3-5 THB), chillies, garlic, fish sauce, oyster sauce, oil, and a fried egg. Total ingredient cost: 40-55 THB depending on protein choice and sourcing.

Selling price: 160-220 THB in casual dining. Margin: 72-80%.

This dish sells in volume -- at many of the restaurants I consult for, pad kra pao accounts for 10-15% of all food orders. It is a Plowhorse by menu engineering definition (high popularity, moderate margin), but the margin is strong enough in absolute terms that high volume makes it a revenue pillar. The key to keeping margins tight is [portion control on the protein](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok) -- the difference between 200g and 250g of pork is 6-8 THB per serving, which adds up to 12,000-16,000 THB/month at 60 orders per day.

**Upsell opportunity:** Offer a "crispy pork belly" upgrade for +40-60 THB. The actual cost difference is 8-12 THB (crispy pork belly is cut from a pre-fried slab, waste is near zero), so the upgrade is almost pure profit.

### Green Curry, Red Curry, Massaman -- 70-78% Margin

Thai curries are margin-friendly because the base -- curry paste and coconut milk -- is inexpensive relative to its perceived value. A batch of green curry paste (galangal, lemongrass, green chillies, shallots, garlic, coriander root, shrimp paste, kaffir lime zest) costs 150-200 THB to make and yields 15-20 servings. That is 8-13 THB per serving in paste alone.

Add coconut milk (one can at 25-30 THB yields 2-3 servings, so 10-15 THB), chicken (200g at 18-25 THB), Thai eggplant and sweet basil (5-8 THB), and your total cost per serving is 42-60 THB.

Selling price: 200-280 THB. Margin: 70-78%.

Curries also have an advantage over stir-fries: perceived premium value. Customers view a curry as a more "complete" dish than a stir-fry, which supports a higher price point. A pad kra pao at 200 THB feels like you are pushing it; a green curry at 280 THB feels normal. The food cost on both is nearly identical.

**Massaman is the margin standout.** Massaman curry uses potatoes and peanuts as bulk, both of which cost almost nothing (3-5 THB per serving). The potato absorbs curry and adds substance, which means you can use slightly less protein per serving (150g versus 200g) without the customer noticing. Massaman with chicken at 40-50 THB food cost, sold at 240-280 THB: 78-83% margin.

<Callout type="tip">
Make your curry paste in-house. Pre-made curry paste from Makro costs 80-120 THB/kg and contains preservatives and flavour compromisers. House-made paste costs 40-60 THB/kg in ingredients, tastes better (which justifies premium pricing), and gives you a genuine point of differentiation. A prep cook can make a week's supply in 2-3 hours.
</Callout>

### Thai Fried Rice -- 75-82% Margin

Fried rice is the silent profit machine on every Thai menu. Cooked jasmine rice (3-5 THB per serving), egg (4-5 THB), garlic and shallots (2 THB), fish sauce and soy sauce (1.5 THB), vegetables (3-5 THB), and a small amount of protein (chicken at 12-15 THB or shrimp at 25-35 THB). Total food cost: 25-65 THB depending on protein choice.

Selling price: 140-220 THB. Margin: 75-82% for chicken/pork versions, 65-72% for prawn versions.

The margin lever here is rice. Rice is the cheapest base ingredient in Thai cooking at 30-40 THB/kg, and a single serving of fried rice uses roughly 200g of cooked rice (about 80g uncooked). Your rice cost per serving is 3-4 THB. The rest is supporting ingredients that individually cost almost nothing.

**Pineapple fried rice is a Puzzle worth promoting.** The dramatic presentation (served in a hollowed-out pineapple) costs 15-20 THB more in ingredients (mainly the pineapple itself) but supports a 280-350 THB price point because tourists and Instagram-oriented diners perceive the presentation as premium. Margin: 78-85%. It photographs well, it generates social media content, and it makes the surrounding tables curious enough to order it too.

### Deep-Fried Appetisers -- 75-82% Margin

Spring rolls, fish cakes (tod mun pla), chicken satay, and prawn crackers are high-margin because the fillings are mostly vegetables, starch, and ground protein -- the cheapest ingredients in your kitchen.

**Tod mun pla (Thai fish cakes):** Ground fish (often the cheapest fresh fish available, 60-80 THB/kg), red curry paste, long beans, kaffir lime leaves. Food cost per serving (4-5 pieces): 15-25 THB. Selling price: 100-160 THB. Margin: 78-85%.

**Chicken satay (4-5 skewers):** Chicken thigh (cheaper than breast, more flavourful), marinade (turmeric, lemongrass, coconut milk, cumin), peanut sauce. Food cost: 20-30 THB. Selling price: 120-180 THB. Margin: 78-83%.

**Spring rolls (4-5 pieces):** Rice paper or wheat wrapper, glass noodles, cabbage, carrot, mushroom. Food cost: 12-18 THB. Selling price: 90-140 THB. Margin: 82-87%.

Appetisers serve a dual margin function. They carry excellent margins on their own, and they increase the total order value because they are "extras" added to the main meal. A table that orders appetisers on top of their mains spends 15-25% more, and that incremental spend is at 78-85% margin. This is why appetiser positioning on your [menu design](/blog/menu-design-increase-revenue-thailand) matters -- they should be visible and tempting, not buried after the main courses.

<Callout type="info">
Appetisers are the highest-margin food category that many Thai restaurants under-promote. If your menu lists appetisers at the bottom or in a small sidebar, test moving them to a prominent position near the top. Appetiser order rates in the restaurants I work with increase 30-50% with better menu placement, and every order is at 78%+ margin.
</Callout>

## Beverage Margins: The Real Profit Centre

If food margins are good, beverage margins are extraordinary. Most restaurant owners I work with in Bangkok understand that drinks are profitable, but they underestimate by how much -- and they chronically under-invest in their beverage menu as a result.

### Thai Iced Tea (Cha Yen) -- 85-90% Margin

Thai tea mix (2-3 THB per serving), condensed milk (3-4 THB), evaporated milk (2-3 THB), sugar (0.5 THB), ice (1 THB). Total cost: 8-12 THB.

Selling price: 80-130 THB. Margin: 85-90%.

This is the most profitable item on most Thai restaurant menus, and it barely requires labour -- a server or bartender can make it in 60 seconds. Thai iced tea should be on every table. Train your servers to suggest it: "Can I get you a Thai iced tea while you look at the menu?" That one sentence, applied consistently, adds 30,000-60,000 THB/month in near-pure-profit revenue for a mid-sized restaurant.

### Fresh Juices and Smoothies -- 78-85% Margin

A fresh watermelon juice costs 8-12 THB in fruit (watermelon at 15-20 THB/kg, 300-400g per serving), sugar, and ice. Selling price: 80-120 THB. That is a 85-90% margin on watermelon.

Mango smoothie runs 15-25 THB in cost (seasonal), selling at 100-140 THB for a 78-85% margin, while passion fruit juice costs 10-15 THB and sells at 90-120 THB for 83-88%.

The seasonal element works in your favour here. During mango season (March-June), mangoes drop to 40-60 THB/kg, making mango beverages absurdly profitable while customers perceive them as a premium seasonal treat. Outside of season, switch your featured juice to whatever is cheapest and freshest -- coconut, guava, lime soda.

### Cocktails -- 78-85% Margin

A tom yum-inspired cocktail (vodka, lemongrass syrup, lime, kaffir lime leaf, chilli) costs 30-45 THB to make (spirit cost is the main expense at 20-30 THB per serve for mid-range vodka, with all other ingredients under 15 THB total). Selling price: 220-350 THB. Margin: 78-85%.

A classic mojito: 25-35 THB in ingredients. Selling price: 200-280 THB. Margin: 83-88%.

The key with cocktails is that spirit cost is fixed (you know exactly what 45ml of vodka costs) and mixer costs are negligible (lime, sugar, soda). There is almost no waste, no spoilage, and the preparation time is 2-3 minutes per drink.

**Thai-inspired cocktails outperform standard classics by 15-20% in order rate** because they offer something customers cannot get at every bar. A lemongrass gin and tonic, a butterfly pea flower spritz, a tamarind whisky sour -- these are unique to your restaurant and justify premium pricing because the customer cannot comparison-shop them.

### Wine Markup -- 70-80% Margin

Wine in Thailand is expensive due to import duties and taxes (wine carries a roughly 400% combined duty, excise, and VAT burden from landed cost to retail). A bottle that the importer sells to you for 400-600 THB appears on wine lists at 1,200-2,400 THB.

The standard Bangkok wine markup:

- **By the glass:** 3-4x your pour cost. A 600 THB bottle yielding 5 glasses at 120 THB cost per glass, sold at 350-490 THB per glass: 66-76% margin.
- **By the bottle:** 2.5-3.5x your purchase price. A 500 THB wholesale bottle sold at 1,400-1,750 THB: 64-71% margin.

Wine margins are lower than cocktails and local beverages, but wine drinkers tend to order multiple glasses or a full bottle, which drives significant per-table revenue. A table ordering a bottle of wine at 1,500 THB adds 1,050-1,200 THB in gross profit to the table's contribution -- and the kitchen did nothing.

**The beverage-food ratio target:** For a well-balanced casual Thai restaurant, beverage revenue should be 25-35% of total revenue. If your beverage revenue is below 20%, you are under-selling the highest-margin category in your business. Fix this through better menu placement, server training on suggesting drinks, and a beverage menu that is as thoughtfully designed as your food menu.

## Dessert Margins: The Overlooked Profit Layer

Thai desserts are structurally high-margin for the same reason stir-fries are: the base ingredients (coconut milk, sugar, rice, tapioca, fruit) are inexpensive, while the perceived value of a "sweet ending" supports solid pricing.

**Mango sticky rice (khao niao mamuang):** Glutinous rice (3-5 THB), coconut cream sauce (5-8 THB), mango (15-25 THB in season, 35-50 THB out of season). Total cost in season: 25-38 THB. Selling price: 150-220 THB. Margin: 78-83%. Out of season the margin drops to 65-72%, which is why smart operators run it as a seasonal special from March to June and pull it when mango prices spike.

**Roti with banana and condensed milk:** Roti dough (3-4 THB per piece), banana (2-3 THB), condensed milk (2-3 THB), sugar (0.5 THB). Total cost: 8-11 THB. Selling price: 80-120 THB. Margin: 86-91%. This is essentially free money if you have a roti station or can add one with minimal equipment.

**Coconut ice cream (with toppings):** Coconut ice cream (8-12 THB per scoop), toppings (peanuts, sweet corn, jackfruit, sticky rice -- 5-8 THB combined). Total cost: 13-20 THB. Selling price: 90-140 THB. Margin: 82-86%.

**Bua loi (glutinous rice balls in coconut cream):** Glutinous rice flour balls (3-5 THB), coconut cream (5-8 THB), sugar (1 THB), pandan leaf (negligible). Total cost: 9-14 THB. Selling price: 80-120 THB. Margin: 83-89%.

### Why Most Restaurants Under-Sell Desserts

The typical Thai restaurant sells desserts to 10-15% of tables. The best ones sell to 30-40%. The difference is not the dessert quality -- it is the selling mechanism.

**Desserts are not visible.** If desserts appear only at the bottom of page 4, after the customer has already decided they are done ordering, they will not order dessert. Put desserts on a separate card that the server presents after mains are cleared, or on the back page of the menu where customers see it throughout the meal.

**Servers do not ask.** "Would you like to see our dessert menu?" is a weak prompt because it invites "no." Train servers to present the specific option: "We have mango sticky rice today -- the mangoes are from Chachoengsao, they came in this morning. Can I bring you one to share?" That specificity and freshness signal converts at 2-3x the rate of a generic prompt.

**Dessert portions are too large.** A customer who has finished a full Thai meal does not want a 300g dessert. Offer small "tasting" portions at 60-80% of the full price. The customer gets a sweet finish without feeling overstuffed, and your margin on the smaller portion is actually higher because the ingredient savings exceed the price reduction.

<Callout type="tip">
A dessert upsell at 100 THB with 83% margin adds 83 THB in gross profit per table. If your servers convert 25% of tables (achievable with proper training and a separate dessert presentation), that is 20 additional dessert orders per day on 80 covers. That is 2,000 THB/day in gross profit, or 60,000 THB/month -- from one menu category that most restaurants ignore.
</Callout>

## The Star/Plowhorse/Puzzle/Dog Matrix Applied to Thai Menus

The menu engineering matrix classifies every item on your menu based on two dimensions: popularity (how often it is ordered) and profitability (contribution margin per serving). Understanding this framework is what separates operators who build menus by instinct from those who build menus by data.

For a full explanation of the methodology, read [Menu Engineering for Thai Restaurants](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand). Here is how the four categories typically map to a Thai restaurant menu.

### Stars (High Popularity + High Margin)

These are your best items. They sell well and make money on every order.

In a typical Thai restaurant, Stars include: pad kra pao (chicken or pork), green curry with chicken, som tum, fried rice (chicken), pad thai with chicken, tom yum chicken/pork, Thai iced tea, and mango sticky rice (in season).

**Strategy:** Protect these items. Do not change the recipe, do not reduce the quality, and do not hide them on the menu. Give them prime placement in the golden triangle of your menu layout and make sure your servers know to recommend them. Monitor their food cost monthly -- even a 2-percentage-point creep in food cost on a Star item costs you disproportionately because of the volume.

### Plowhorses (High Popularity + Low Margin)

These items are ordered constantly but generate below-average margins. They matter for traffic and customer satisfaction, but they drag your blended food cost upward.

Common Thai Plowhorses: pad thai with prawns (the prawn cost kills the margin), tom yum goong (same prawn problem), seafood fried rice, and imported beer.

**Strategy:** You have three levers. First, raise the price by 10-20 THB -- high-popularity items tolerate small price increases because customers are already committed to ordering them. Second, reduce the portion cost through [portion control and sourcing optimisation](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok). Third, engineer an upsell that pushes customers toward a higher-margin variant ("Would you prefer the chicken or the prawn pad thai?" -- leading with chicken).

### Puzzles (Low Popularity + High Margin)

These are profitable items that customers are not ordering enough of. They have great margins but low visibility or appeal.

Thai Puzzles often include: massaman curry (overlooked by tourists who default to green curry), som tum with salted crab (unfamiliar to non-Thai diners), specialty house cocktails (customers default to beer or known brands), and fish cakes as a standalone dish (often overshadowed by spring rolls).

**Strategy:** Increase visibility and selling effort. Move Puzzles to prime menu positions, add photos if you use photography, have servers recommend them, and write better descriptions. A massaman curry described as "48-hour braised beef cheek in roasted spice coconut curry with potato and peanut" sells better than "Massaman curry with beef." The margin is the same; the perception is different.

### Dogs (Low Popularity + Low Margin)

Low popularity and low margin. These items take up menu space, inventory, and kitchen bandwidth without contributing meaningfully to either revenue or profit.

Thai Dogs often include: complex seafood platters that require multiple prep techniques, imported-ingredient specials (truffle fried rice, wagyu pad kra pao) where the ingredient cost is not justified by sufficient order volume, and seasonal items kept on the menu after the season ends.

**Strategy:** Remove them. A shorter menu with fewer Dogs improves your average check (by redirecting attention to Stars and Puzzles), reduces waste and inventory complexity, and simplifies kitchen operations. If a Dog has sentimental value or is tied to your concept, at least reprice it or reformulate it with cheaper ingredients.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not run the Star/Dog analysis on instinct. You need actual POS data (order counts) and actual food cost data (recipe costing cards) for every item. "I think the green curry sells well" is not data. "Green curry sold 847 times last month at 72% margin" is data. The matrix is only as good as your numbers.
</Callout>

## Building a Menu Around Margin Mix

Your blended food cost is the weighted average of every item's food cost, weighted by sales volume. This means one high-volume, low-margin item can drag your entire blended food cost upward even if the rest of your menu is profitable.

Here is an example. A restaurant with 30 items where 20 items run 25% food cost and 10 items run 40% food cost will have a blended food cost somewhere between 25% and 40% -- exactly where depends on the sales mix. If those 10 high-cost items account for 50% of sales volume, the blended food cost is roughly 32.5%. If those same 10 items account for only 20% of sales, the blend drops to around 28%.

The goal is not to eliminate low-margin items. It is to ensure that high-margin items dominate the sales mix.

### The 60/20/20 Rule

A practical target for most casual Thai restaurants:

- **60% of orders** should come from high-margin items (75%+ margin): stir-fries, curries, rice dishes, salads, appetisers, beverages
- **20% of orders** from medium-margin items (65-75%): soups with prawns, noodle dishes, grilled items
- **20% of orders** from low-margin items (50-65%): seafood mains, premium specials, imported-ingredient dishes

If your current mix is closer to 40/30/30, you are leaving significant profit on the table. Shift the mix by:

**Menu design.** Position high-margin items in the golden triangle, use boxes and "Chef's Recommendation" markers, and write better descriptions for profitable dishes. This alone shifts 5-10% of the mix toward your Stars.

**Server training.** When a table asks "What do you recommend?", the answer should be a Star -- not whatever the server personally likes or the most expensive item. Train your team on which items to push and why. Incentivise it: a small bonus for the shift that generates the highest average check, or a reward for the server who sells the most of a specific Star item.

**Set meals that feature high-margin items.** Build your set meals around Stars and promoted Puzzles. A "Bangkok Favourites" set that includes som tum, green curry with chicken, pad kra pao, rice, and Thai iced tea runs a blended 75-80% margin while feeling like a curated experience. For more on [how set meal bundling works with pricing psychology](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand), the principle is that the customer perceives a discount while you achieve a higher total margin than a la carte.

**Specials boards that rotate high-margin dishes.** A daily "Chef's Special" board near the entrance that features a different Star or Puzzle each day creates urgency ("available today only") and draws orders toward profitable items.

## Seasonal Ingredient Impact on Margins

Bangkok's ingredient prices fluctuate more than most operators realise, and these fluctuations directly affect your item-level margins. An item that is a Star in February can become a Plowhorse or even a Dog in July if the key ingredient price doubles.

### Prawn Prices: The Biggest Margin Variable

Prawn pricing in Bangkok swings 30-40% across the year, cheapest during cool season (November-February) at roughly 180-220 THB/kg for 31/40 count and most expensive during May-August at 260-320 THB/kg.

Impact: a tom yum goong that costs 65 THB to produce in January costs 85 THB in July -- same dish, same recipe, same selling price. Your margin drops from 72% to 63%.

**Response:** Promote prawn-heavy dishes during cool season when margins are best. During expensive months, shift your specials board toward chicken and pork dishes. Consider a "seasonal market price" notation for prawn dishes during volatile periods -- common in Bangkok seafood restaurants and accepted by diners.

### Mango Season: A Margin Windfall

From March to June, nam dok mai mangoes drop to 40-60 THB/kg (versus 100-150+ THB/kg out of season). Mango sticky rice, mango smoothies, mango salads, and mango-based cocktails all become extremely profitable during this window.

A smart operator launches a "Mango Festival" mini-menu with 4-5 mango items during peak season, promotes it on social media and in-restaurant signage, and pulls it when prices climb. This is pure margin capture: the ingredients are at their cheapest, customer demand is at its highest, and the limited-time positioning creates urgency.

### Lime Price Volatility

Bangkok lime prices are notoriously unstable -- from 60 THB/kg during good supply to 200+ THB/kg during shortages. Since lime is used in virtually every Thai dish as a garnish or ingredient, a price spike affects your entire menu.

Mitigation: maintain a 2-week buffer stock of limes when prices are normal, switch to bottled lime juice for cooking applications (acceptable quality, fraction of the cost), and reduce garnish portion size during spikes (a quarter lime instead of a half).

## Delivery Menu Margin Considerations

Delivery revenue through GrabFood and LINE MAN carries a structural penalty: 30-35% platform commission. A dish that runs 28% food cost on dine-in effectively runs 40-43% food cost on delivery (because your net revenue is 65-70% of the listed price). This means your delivery menu needs to be even more margin-conscious than your dine-in menu.

### Rules for a Profitable Delivery Menu

**Only put your highest-margin items on delivery.** Stir-fries, fried rice, curries with chicken or pork. Remove prawn dishes and seafood platters from the delivery menu unless you have raised the delivery price enough to compensate.

**Raise delivery prices by 15-20%.** Most customers expect this and do not compare delivery prices to dine-in. A pad kra pao at 180 THB dine-in and 210 THB on GrabFood is standard practice.

**Build delivery-specific bundles.** A "Lunch Box" at 199-249 THB that includes a main, rice, drink, and small dessert costs you 45-55 THB in food. After 35% commission, your net revenue is 130-162 THB, giving you a 66-72% margin on net -- better than most a la carte delivery items.

**Skip the salads and soups.** Som tum does not travel well (it wilts within 15 minutes). Soups risk spilling and arriving lukewarm. Focus your delivery menu on items that travel well and maintain quality: curries, stir-fries, fried rice, noodle dishes, and desserts that do not melt.

**Invest in delivery photos.** On platforms, the photo is the menu. Items with professional photos get 25-30% more orders. Photograph every item on your delivery menu -- the cost (1,500-2,500 THB per dish) is negligible relative to the order volume it drives.

<Callout type="warning">
Track your delivery food cost against net revenue (after commission), not gross. I have seen cloud kitchen operators celebrate a "30% food cost" that was actually 43% after commission. The math: if GrabFood takes 35% of your 200 THB selling price, your net is 130 THB. If the dish costs 55 THB to make, your actual food cost is 55/130 = 42.3%. That is a very different number. For the full breakdown, see [Food Cost Control for Bangkok Restaurants](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).
</Callout>

## Upselling Strategies That Increase Per-Table Margin

Upselling is not about pressuring customers to spend more. It is about presenting options that increase their perceived value while simultaneously improving your margin.

### Protein Upgrades

Offer a protein upgrade on every stir-fry and curry: "Regular (chicken thigh)" as the default, with options for "Crispy pork belly (+40 THB)," "River prawns (+60 THB)," or "Wagyu beef (+80 THB)."

The economics: the actual ingredient cost difference between chicken and crispy pork belly is 8-12 THB, but the customer pays 40 THB more. That 28-32 THB in additional profit per upgrade, across 20-30 upgrades per day, adds 17,000-29,000 THB/month.

### Beverage Pairing Suggestions

Print a "Pairs well with..." suggestion next to your Stars. "Pairs well with: Thai Iced Tea (90 THB) or Lemongrass Cooler (110 THB)." This gentle prompt increases beverage attachment rate by 10-15% without feeling pushy. Given beverage margins of 80-90%, every additional drink is nearly pure profit.

### Side Dish Prompts

"Add steamed morning glory (+45 THB)" or "Add a fried egg (+20 THB)" are low-friction upsells because the incremental cost is tiny (8-12 THB for morning glory, 4-5 THB for an egg) and the customer does not feel "upgraded" -- they feel like they are completing their meal.

### Dessert as Standard Practice

Do not wait for the customer to ask. Present a dessert after clearing mains: "We have coconut ice cream with peanuts and sticky rice -- it is the perfect finish. One to share?" Frame it as the expected next step, not an optional add-on.

### Size Upgrades on Beverages

As covered in the [pricing psychology article](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand), a three-tier size structure on beverages (small / regular / large) with a decoy-priced regular pushes customers toward the large -- where your margin is best because the cost increase from regular to large is almost entirely ice and water.

## Putting It All Together: A Margin-Optimised Thai Menu

Here is what a margin-optimised menu looks like for a casual Thai restaurant at the 200-300 THB average check level.

**Appetisers (all 78%+ margin):** Som tum, tod mun pla, chicken satay, fresh spring rolls. 4 items, all high-margin, positioned prominently.

**Soups (70-78% margin):** Tom yum (chicken version as default, prawns as upgrade), tom kha, clear soup. 3-4 items. Chicken-based soups push margins higher.

**Curries (70-78% margin):** Green curry, massaman, panang, jungle curry. 4-5 items. Chicken as default protein, with upgrade options for prawns or premium cuts.

**Stir-fries (72-80% margin):** Pad kra pao, pad pak ruam mit, pad prik king, pad cha. 5-6 items. The backbone of your margin mix.

**Rice and noodles (72-80% margin):** Fried rice (chicken), pineapple fried rice, pad thai (chicken), pad see ew. 4-5 items. Chicken versions as the highlighted default.

**Seafood specials (55-68% margin):** Whole grilled fish, prawns in garlic pepper, steamed fish with lime. 3-4 items. Priced as premium and acts as the price anchor for the rest of the menu.

**Beverages (78-90% margin):** Thai iced tea, fresh juices, cocktails, beer, wine. At least 8-10 options. The most underinvested category in most restaurants.

**Desserts (78-88% margin):** Mango sticky rice (seasonal), roti with banana, coconut ice cream. 3-4 items on a separate dessert card presented after mains.

Total items: 32-40. Blended food cost target: 27-31%. Beverage revenue target: 25-35% of total.

The menu above pushes high-margin categories (stir-fries, curries, appetisers, beverages) while keeping lower-margin seafood as a premium section that works as a price anchor and variety option without dominating the order mix. Every item earns its place either through high margins, high volume, or strategic function (anchoring, variety, brand identity).
