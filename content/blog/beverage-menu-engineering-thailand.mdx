Most restaurant operators in Bangkok spend weeks engineering their food menu and give their beverage program about 45 minutes of attention. I know this because I have audited over 200 Thai restaurant menus, and in roughly 80% of them, the beverage list is a photocopy of the distributor's catalogue with round-number markups applied by the owner or bar manager on a quiet afternoon.

This is a problem because beverages carry 70-85% gross margins versus 60-70% on food. A restaurant doing 3 million THB/month in total revenue with a 25% beverage ratio generates 750,000 THB in beverage sales. At 80% margin, that is 600,000 THB in gross profit from drinks alone -- and most of that profit requires no kitchen capacity, no prep cook overtime, and minimal ingredient waste. When I work with a client on bar menu pricing in Thailand, the beverage program is usually where the fastest margin gains live, because it has been neglected the longest.

This article covers how to engineer a beverage menu for Thai restaurants and bars -- from beer and wine to cocktails and Thai iced tea -- with the tax environment, competitive dynamics, and customer behaviour specific to Thailand in 2026.

## Why Beverage Margins Matter More Than Food Margins

The numbers are structural. A well-run Thai restaurant kitchen generates 60-70% gross margin on food. A well-run beverage program generates 75-85%. That 10-15 percentage point gap exists because beverages have fundamentally different economics than food.

**No spoilage on spirits and beer.** A bottle of whisky does not expire tomorrow. Beer has a shelf life of 6-12 months. Wine, stored properly, lasts years. Compare this to your morning market run where the prawns need to be used today, the herbs wilt by Thursday, and the pre-cut vegetables turn brown in 48 hours. Beverage waste is almost entirely preventable, while food waste is a permanent line item.

**Labour cost is minimal.** A bartender makes a cocktail in 2-3 minutes. A server pours a beer in 30 seconds. Your kitchen needs a full brigade to produce one table's food order. Beverages scale without proportional labour cost, which means every additional drink sold drops almost entirely to gross profit.

**Portion control is exact.** A 45ml spirit pour is a 45ml spirit pour -- if you use jiggers. There is no equivalent of a cook adding an extra handful of prawns because the plate looked sparse. Beverage portions are standardised by design, which makes cost prediction reliable.

**Price elasticity is higher.** Customers are more willing to pay a premium for drinks than food. A 350 THB cocktail does not trigger the same mental comparison as a 350 THB stir-fry, because there is no street cart selling cocktails for 60 THB. The absence of a cheap reference point -- the one that haunts every food item on your menu -- gives beverages more pricing room.

The target beverage-to-food revenue ratio for a casual Thai restaurant is 25-35%. A bar-restaurant or cocktail-focused concept should aim for 40-55%. If your beverage revenue is below 20% of total, you are under-monetising your highest-margin category, and fixing that is likely the single fastest path to improving your bottom line.

## Thailand's Alcohol Excise Tax: How It Shapes Your Menu

You cannot engineer a beverage menu in Thailand without understanding the tax structure, because it determines your cost base and creates competitive advantages for certain categories.

Thailand's Excise Tax Act imposes a dual-rate system on alcohol: a percentage of the ex-factory price (ad valorem) or a specific rate per litre of pure alcohol, whichever yields the higher tax. On top of excise tax, you pay VAT (7%), a local development tax (10% of excise), and a health surcharge (2% of excise). The combined burden differs dramatically by category.

**Local beer (Chang, Singha, Leo):** Excise tax is roughly 22% of retail price plus approximately 430 THB per litre of pure alcohol. For a standard 5% ABV beer, the specific rate dominates. Your wholesale cost for a 330ml bottle of Leo or Chang sits at 22-28 THB. The tax burden is already baked into this price because the breweries are domestic producers who pay excise at the factory gate.

**Imported beer (Asahi, Heineken, craft imports):** Same tax rates apply, but on top of the CIF (cost, insurance, freight) import price plus import duty (typically 60% for beer). A 330ml Heineken that costs the importer 25-30 THB in landed product ends up at 35-55 THB wholesale to your restaurant after all duties and taxes. This is why imported beer margins are lower than local beer -- the tax-inflated cost base is 40-80% higher.

**Wine:** Thailand taxes wine at roughly 10,000-13,000 THB per litre of pure alcohol (specific rate) or 5-10% ad valorem, whichever is higher. Combined with import duties of 54% on wine from most countries (lower from ASEAN Free Trade Agreement partners like Australia at 8%, New Zealand at 0%), a bottle of wine that costs the importer 120-200 THB in Europe lands in your restaurant at 350-800 THB wholesale. The effective tax burden on wine is the highest of any beverage category -- which is why wine is expensive in Thailand and why your markup strategy needs to be different from markets where wine is cheap.

**Imported spirits:** Excise tax on spirits runs approximately 1,500 THB per litre of pure alcohol or a high percentage ad valorem. A bottle of mid-range imported vodka or gin that retails for 300-400 THB in Europe costs 800-1,200 THB wholesale in Bangkok. Premium whiskies (Scotch, Japanese) carry similar burdens -- a bottle of Johnnie Walker Black Label wholesales at approximately 900-1,100 THB.

**Thai spirits (Mekhong, Sang Som, Regency):** Domestically produced spirits benefit from much lower landed costs. Sang Som rum wholesales at 140-180 THB per 700ml bottle. Mekhong at 120-160 THB. These are the margin kings of your spirit inventory.

<Callout type="warning">
Thailand's excise tax rates are revised periodically (the last major revision was in 2023). The rates above are effective as of early 2026, but always confirm with your distributor. Also note that restaurants holding a liquor licence are subject to the [2026 alcohol control regulations](/blog/thailand-alcohol-law-2026), which affect when you can serve and how you can promote alcoholic beverages. Non-compliance penalties start at 10,000 THB and scale up.
</Callout>

The strategic implication is clear. Local beer and Thai spirits give you the best cost base. Cocktails made with Thai spirits or mid-range imported spirits, extended with cheap mixers and house-made syrups, generate the highest margins. Wine is your lowest-margin alcoholic category but serves an important role for certain customer segments. And non-alcoholic beverages -- where there is no excise tax at all -- are where margin percentages peak.

## Beer Margins: Draft vs. Bottle, Local vs. Imported

Beer is the easiest beverage category to manage and the one where most Thai restaurants leave money on the table through lazy pricing.

### Local Beer (Chang, Leo, Singha)

These three brands account for roughly 85% of beer consumption in Thai restaurants. Your wholesale cost for a 330ml bottle is 22-28 THB. A 620ml large bottle runs 38-48 THB.

**Standard pricing and margins:**

- **330ml bottle:** Sell at 90-130 THB. Cost 22-28 THB. Margin: 75-79%.
- **620ml bottle:** Sell at 160-220 THB. Cost 38-48 THB. Margin: 76-78%.
- **Draft beer (330ml pour):** Cost depends on your keg pricing and system, typically 18-24 THB per pour. Sell at 100-150 THB. Margin: 80-84%.

The margin difference between bottled and draft is 3-6 percentage points in favour of draft. Over a month, for a restaurant selling 50 beers per day, that translates to 15,000-30,000 THB in additional gross profit from draft versus bottles. The catch is the capital cost of a draft system (40,000-120,000 THB depending on number of taps) and the ongoing maintenance plus CO2 costs. For most casual Thai restaurants doing 30+ beer sales per day, the draft system pays for itself within 3-5 months.

**Which brand should you push?** From a pure margin perspective, the difference between Chang, Leo, and Singha is 2-4 THB per bottle at wholesale -- negligible. The real question is which brand your customers prefer, because a customer who asks for Singha and hears "we only have Chang" does not order Chang instead -- they order water or nothing. Stock at least two of the three, lead with whatever your dominant customer segment prefers, and do not waste menu space on brands nobody is asking for.

### Imported and Craft Beer

Imported commercial beer (Heineken, Asahi, Tiger, Hoegaarden) wholesales at 35-55 THB per 330ml. Craft beer from Thai craft breweries or imports runs 80-150 THB wholesale per 330ml bottle or can.

**Standard pricing and margins:**

- **Imported commercial (330ml):** Sell at 150-220 THB. Cost 35-55 THB. Margin: 72-77%.
- **Thai craft beer (330ml):** Sell at 220-320 THB. Cost 80-150 THB. Margin: 53-64%.

Craft beer margins are the weakest in the beer category. The wholesale cost is 3-5x a local beer, and you cannot always charge 3-5x the price because the target market for craft beer in Bangkok is price-aware and comparison-shops across venues. A 330ml craft IPA at 320 THB only works in a bar-forward concept where the craft selection is a draw; in a casual Thai restaurant, it sits on the menu unsold while the fridge space it occupies could hold more Chang.

My recommendation: stock 1-2 imported commercial options (Heineken and one Japanese beer satisfy most requests), skip craft unless your concept specifically caters to that market, and keep 70%+ of your beer sales in the local category where margins are best.

<Callout type="tip">
**Beer tower math.** A 3-litre beer tower costs you 130-180 THB in draft beer (roughly 9 x 330ml pours) and sells at 590-790 THB. That is a 73-77% margin on the tower itself, but the real win is table dynamics -- a group that orders a tower stays longer, orders more food, and spends 25-40% more per head than the same group ordering individual bottles. If you have a draft system, beer towers should be on every table tent and suggested by every server.
</Callout>

## Wine Markup Strategies for Thai Restaurants

Wine is the most complex beverage category to manage in Thailand because the cost base is high (tax-driven), customer knowledge varies wildly (from "whatever's cheapest" to genuine oenophiles), and spoilage risk on by-the-glass programs is real.

### By-the-Glass Programs

A by-the-glass program is where your best wine margins live, but it requires discipline.

**The math:** A 750ml bottle yields 5 glasses at 150ml each. If you buy a house white at 400 THB wholesale, your pour cost is 80 THB per glass. Sell at 280-350 THB per glass. Margin: 71-77%. If you sell all 5 glasses, total revenue is 1,400-1,750 THB on a 400 THB bottle -- a 3.5-4.4x return.

The risk: you do not sell all 5 glasses. An opened bottle of white wine stays drinkable for 3-5 days with proper re-sealing and refrigeration. Red wine, 2-4 days. If you pour 3 glasses and dump the remaining 2, your effective cost per glass rises to 133 THB, cutting your margin to 52-62%.

**How to manage by-the-glass waste:**

- **Limit by-the-glass options to 4-6 wines** (2 white, 2 red, 1-2 rose/sparkling). Fewer options mean faster turnover per bottle.
- **Use a wine preservation system** (Coravin or argon gas) for premium pours. Coravin runs 8,000-12,000 THB and pays for itself in 2-3 months if you are pouring wines at 450+ THB per glass.
- **Track daily pours.** If a wine is not selling 3+ glasses per day, remove it from by-the-glass and offer it only by the bottle.
- **Match your by-the-glass selection to your food.** A house white that pairs with 60% of your menu (a crisp Sauvignon Blanc or unoaked Chardonnay works with most Thai food) will sell more than an obscure varietal that pairs with three dishes.

### By-the-Bottle Strategy

**Standard Bangkok restaurant markups:**

- **House wine (entry level):** 2.5-3x wholesale. A 350 THB wholesale bottle sells at 900-1,050 THB. This is your "value" option and should appear first on the wine list.
- **Mid-range:** 2.5-3x wholesale. A 600 THB wholesale bottle at 1,500-1,800 THB. This is where most of your bottle sales will land.
- **Premium (800+ THB wholesale):** 2-2.5x. A 1,200 THB wholesale bottle at 2,400-3,000 THB. Lower multiplier because the absolute profit per bottle is already high (1,200-1,800 THB), and aggressive markups at this level push the price into territory where customers order beer or cocktails instead.

**House wine selection matters.** Your house wines will account for 40-60% of all wine sales. Choose them for three criteria: (1) consistent availability from your distributor (running out mid-service damages credibility), (2) broad food pairing versatility (Thai cuisine is spice-forward, so wines with residual sweetness or low tannin work best -- off-dry Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Gruner Veltliner for whites; light Pinot Noir or Grenache for reds), and (3) a wholesale cost that supports your target glass and bottle pricing.

I have seen restaurants lose 30,000+ THB/month by choosing a "cool" house wine that nobody orders because it does not pair with the food and the name is unpronounceable. The best house wine is the one that sells, not the one that impresses other sommeliers.

### Wine List Structure

Keep it short. A casual Thai restaurant needs 12-20 wines on the list. A wine-focused concept might carry 40-60. Beyond that, you are holding inventory that does not move and confusing customers who then default to beer.

Organise by style, not by country. "Light and Crisp Whites," "Rich and Full Reds," and "Sparkling and Rose" are more useful to a customer choosing wine with Thai food than "France, Italy, Australia, Chile" sections. Add a one-line food pairing note next to each wine: "Great with green curry and seafood" or "Pairs well with grilled meats and massaman."

## Cocktail Program Profitability

Cocktails are where the money is. The margin profile is excellent, the creative freedom allows differentiation, and a good cocktail program drives customer traffic and social media visibility in ways that beer and wine do not.

### Spirit Cost and Pour Economics

The core cost in any cocktail is the spirit. Everything else -- mixers, syrups, citrus, garnishes, ice -- typically runs 10-25 THB combined per drink.

**Spirit cost per 45ml pour (standard cocktail measure):**

- **Thai rum (Sang Som):** 9-12 THB. The margin champion.
- **Thai whisky (Mekhong):** 8-11 THB.
- **Mid-range vodka (Smirnoff, Absolut):** 22-30 THB.
- **Mid-range gin (Beefeater, Gordon's):** 24-32 THB.
- **Premium gin (Hendrick's, Monkey 47):** 45-70 THB.
- **Mid-range tequila (Jose Cuervo, Olmeca):** 28-38 THB.
- **Mid-range whisky (Johnnie Walker Red):** 25-35 THB.
- **Premium whisky (Johnnie Walker Black):** 40-55 THB.

**Cocktail costing example -- Thai Basil Smash:**

- Gin (45ml, mid-range): 28 THB
- Lime juice (30ml): 3 THB
- Simple syrup (15ml): 1 THB
- Thai basil: 2 THB
- Ice: 1 THB
- **Total cost: 35 THB**
- **Selling price: 280-350 THB**
- **Margin: 87-90%**

That margin is why cocktail bars can afford expensive real estate in Thonglor and Charoenkrung while a noodle shop in the same neighbourhood struggles. The economics are structurally different.

### Signature Drinks vs. Classics

Every cocktail menu should include both, but the balance matters.

**Classic cocktails (Mojito, Margarita, G&T, Old Fashioned)** are what customers order when they do not know what else to get. They are low-risk orders with predictable taste profiles, and they anchor your cocktail menu in familiar territory. Cost: 25-45 THB. Sell at 220-320 THB. Margin: 80-86%.

**Signature cocktails** are where you differentiate and justify premium pricing. A "Chiang Mai Mist" (butterfly pea gin, lemongrass syrup, coconut water, lime) or a "Siam Sunset" (Mekhong, tamarind, chilli, palm sugar, lime) cannot be comparison-shopped because nobody else has them. This allows pricing at 280-390 THB without the customer thinking "I could get this cheaper down the street." Cost: 30-55 THB. Margin: 82-90%.

Thai-inspired signature cocktails outperform standard classics by 15-20% in order rate at the restaurants I work with, and they generate significantly more social media content. A purple butterfly pea cocktail that changes colour when lime is added gets photographed and posted. A gin and tonic does not.

**Aim for 5-8 signature cocktails and 6-8 classics on your menu.** Rotate 2-3 signatures quarterly to keep regulars interested and give your social media something to post about.

### Batch Cocktails: The Efficiency Play

A batch cocktail is a cocktail pre-mixed in volume (typically 5-20 litres) that is portioned to order. The spirit, sweetener, and acid components are combined in advance, then poured over ice and garnished at service.

The economics are compelling. Batch cocktails reduce bartender time per drink from 2-3 minutes to 30-45 seconds, which matters during peak hours when your bar is the bottleneck. They also ensure consistency -- every pour tastes identical, eliminating the variance that comes from different bartenders free-pouring.

**What batches well:** Negroni, Old Fashioned, Margarita (minus the fresh lime, added at service), Daiquiri, Whisky Sour base, Sangria, Punch bowls.

**What does not batch well:** Anything with dairy (White Russian), fresh egg (Pisco Sour -- the egg needs shaking to order), carbonated components (the fizz dies in storage).

Cost savings from batching are typically 5-10% per drink (from reduced waste and faster prep) plus the ability to serve 30-40% more cocktails per hour during peak service without adding bar staff.

<Callout type="tip">
Batch your highest-volume signature cocktails for Friday and Saturday service. If your Thai Basil Smash sells 40 units on a Saturday night, pre-batching the base (minus ice and fresh basil garnish) saves your bartender 80-100 minutes of labour over the shift. That freed-up time translates to either faster service (better customer experience) or additional drink sales (more revenue).
</Callout>

### Garnish Costs: Small But Sneaky

Garnishes are the line item nobody tracks and everybody overspends on. A lime wheel costs 2-3 THB. A sprig of rosemary, 3-5 THB. An edible flower, 8-15 THB. A dehydrated citrus wheel (trendy in Bangkok's cocktail scene), 5-10 THB.

On a 280 THB cocktail, a 15 THB garnish does not sound significant -- until you multiply by 80 cocktails per night, 7 nights per week. That is 42,000 THB/month in garnish costs. At 5 THB per garnish instead, you save 28,000 THB/month while the customer barely notices the difference.

**Rules for garnish cost control:**

- **No garnish should exceed 5% of the drink's selling price.** On a 300 THB cocktail, the garnish budget is 15 THB maximum.
- **Use ingredients you already have in the kitchen.** Thai basil, kaffir lime leaves, chillies, lemongrass -- these are in your walk-in already. Buying specialty garnish ingredients adds cost and waste.
- **Prep garnishes in bulk during slow hours.** Cut citrus wheels, pick herbs, and store properly. Prep waste is a major garnish cost driver.

## Non-Alcoholic Beverages: The Hidden Profit Centre

If alcoholic beverages are your best margin category, non-alcoholic beverages are even better -- and they serve 100% of your customers, including the growing segment that does not drink alcohol.

### Thai Iced Tea (Cha Yen)

I covered this in the [high-margin menu items article](/blog/high-margin-menu-items-thailand), but it bears repeating here because Thai iced tea should be the foundation of your non-alcoholic beverage program.

**Cost: 8-12 THB. Sell at 80-130 THB. Margin: 85-92%.**

This is the highest-margin item on most Thai restaurant menus, period. It requires no special equipment, the ingredients are shelf-stable, and every customer -- Thai, expat, tourist -- knows what it is and how it tastes. Train your servers to suggest it before the customer opens the menu: "Can I start you with a Thai iced tea?" That one question, asked consistently, adds 30,000-60,000 THB/month in near-pure-profit revenue.

### Fresh Juice Program

Fresh juices are the second highest-margin non-alcoholic category, and they benefit from a health-conscious perception that supports premium pricing.

**Cost and margin by fruit:**

- **Watermelon:** 8-12 THB cost, sell at 90-120 THB. Margin: 87-90%.
- **Orange:** 12-18 THB cost, sell at 100-130 THB. Margin: 83-86%.
- **Passion fruit:** 10-15 THB cost, sell at 100-130 THB. Margin: 85-88%.
- **Mango (in season):** 15-25 THB cost, sell at 110-140 THB. Margin: 80-86%.
- **Coconut (fresh):** 15-20 THB cost, sell at 90-130 THB. Margin: 78-85%.

The key variable is fruit cost, which fluctuates with season and supply. Run your featured juice based on whatever is cheapest and freshest that week -- watermelon year-round as a staple, with a seasonal rotation for the second and third options.

**Juice waste management:** Fresh juice oxidises quickly. Do not pre-juice more than you will sell in 2-3 hours. For slower-selling juices, juice to order (adds 1-2 minutes of wait time but eliminates waste). For high-volume juices (watermelon at a beach restaurant), batch-juice in 2-litre quantities and keep cold.

### Mocktails: Serving the Non-Drinker

Thailand has a large non-drinking population (roughly 40% of Thai adults do not consume alcohol regularly), plus Muslim tourists from Malaysia and the Middle East, health-conscious expats, and designated drivers. A restaurant that offers only water, Coke, and Thai iced tea for non-drinkers is leaving money on the table.

A mocktail program does not need to be elaborate. Five well-crafted options that mirror the creativity of your cocktail menu will capture the non-drinker's spend at cocktail-adjacent pricing.

**Mocktail cost:** 15-30 THB (fruit puree, syrups, herbs, soda water, ice).
**Sell at:** 140-220 THB.
**Margin:** 82-89%.

The price positioning is important. Mocktails priced too low (80-90 THB) feel like juice in a fancy glass and will not be ordered by customers who want a "drink" experience. Priced at 160-200 THB, they sit comfortably below cocktail pricing while delivering a higher margin, and the non-drinking customer feels included in the bar experience rather than relegated to a Coke.

### Coffee Program

Coffee revenue in a Thai restaurant depends entirely on whether you position it as an afterthought or a standalone offering.

**Afterthought approach:** Drip coffee from a thermal carafe. Cost: 3-5 THB per cup. Sell at 50-70 THB. Margin: 90%+. Zero labour. This works for a casual restaurant where coffee is a meal-ending gesture.

**Invested approach:** Espresso-based drinks (latte, cappuccino, espresso) from a proper machine. Cost: 12-20 THB per drink (beans, milk). Sell at 90-150 THB. Margin: 83-87%. Requires a 50,000-150,000 THB machine and a trained operator, but the incremental revenue justifies it if you are open for breakfast or afternoon service and your location has foot traffic.

**Thai iced coffee (oliang):** Cost: 5-8 THB. Sell at 70-100 THB. Margin: 88-92%. Uses the same equipment as Thai iced tea and taps the same customer base. If you offer cha yen, add oliang.

## Beverage Menu Design: Separate vs. Integrated

How you present your beverage menu affects what and how much customers order.

### The Case for a Separate Beverage Menu

A dedicated beverage menu -- a separate card, booklet, or table tent -- signals that your beverage program is serious. It gives drinks the visual real estate they need for descriptions, pairing suggestions, and grouping. It also stays on the table after food ordering is complete, which prompts second and third round orders.

Restaurants with separate beverage menus sell 15-25% more beverages per table than those with drinks listed on the back page of the food menu. I have tested this with four clients in Bangkok, and the effect is consistent. The separate menu keeps beverages visible throughout the meal, while a drinks section buried on page 3 of the food menu disappears the moment the food order is placed.

### When Integration Works

For very small restaurants (under 30 seats) with a compact beverage offering (8-12 drinks), integrating beverages into the food menu is fine -- it simplifies service and avoids cluttering a small table with multiple menus. Place the beverage section at the beginning of the menu (before appetisers), not at the end. The customer should encounter drinks before they encounter food, because the first order a server takes should be beverages.

### Visual Cues That Sell

Whether separate or integrated, your beverage menu should use these design elements:

**Pairing suggestions next to food items.** "Pairs well with: Lemongrass Gin Fizz (280 THB)" printed next to your green curry is a subtle prompt that increases cocktail attachment by 10-15%. This works on the food menu even if you have a separate beverage menu.

**Photos for signature cocktails.** A colourful, well-composed photo of your butterfly pea cocktail or Thai basil smash sells the drink before the customer reads the description. On delivery platforms this is obvious; on dine-in menus it is underused. 2-4 photos of your most photogenic (and highest-margin) cocktails are worth the design investment.

**Section ordering that leads with premium.** List signature cocktails first, then classics, then beer, then wine, then non-alcoholic. The first section sets the price anchor. If the first thing a customer sees is a 340 THB signature cocktail, the 120 THB beer looks affordable. If the first thing they see is the 90 THB Chang, the 340 THB cocktail feels like a stretch.

**Descriptive language that justifies the price.** "Gin and tonic -- 280 THB" invites comparison shopping. "Siam Botanical G&T -- Chalong Bay gin, house-made pandan tonic, kaffir lime, butterfly pea ice -- 280 THB" tells a story that makes the price feel earned. The ingredients are the same; the words are different.

<Callout type="info">
The [menu pricing psychology](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand) principles that apply to food -- anchoring, decoy pricing, charm pricing, killing dot leaders -- apply equally to beverages. A three-tier cocktail size (single, double, pitcher) with the double as a decoy pushing customers toward the pitcher works exactly like a three-tier tom yum. Apply the same framework to drinks and the results are just as strong.
</Callout>

## Inventory Control for Beverages

Beverage inventory is simpler than food inventory because products do not expire quickly and portions are standardised. But "simpler" does not mean "unmanaged." The three threats to beverage margin are pour cost creep, spillage, and theft.

### Pour Cost Tracking

Pour cost is the beverage equivalent of food cost: the cost of the drink divided by its selling price. Your target pour cost depends on category:

- **Spirits/cocktails:** 15-22%
- **Beer:** 20-28%
- **Wine:** 25-35%
- **Non-alcoholic:** 8-18%
- **Blended beverage cost:** 18-25%

Track pour cost weekly by comparing inventory used (opening stock minus closing stock plus deliveries) against revenue generated. If your cocktail pour cost target is 18% and actual comes in at 24%, something is wrong -- over-pouring, spillage, unrecorded comp drinks, or theft.

### The Biggest Leak: Free-Pouring

A bartender who free-pours spirits (no jigger, no measured pour) consistently over-pours by 15-25%. On a 45ml target pour, that is an extra 7-11ml per drink. At 30 drinks per shift using mid-range spirits, that overpour costs you 150-250 THB per shift, or 4,500-7,500 THB per month from a single bartender's habit.

**Fix:** Mandate jiggers. Every pour, every time, no exceptions. Some bar managers resist this because "it slows down service" -- it adds approximately 3 seconds per drink. The cost savings from accurate pouring are 50-100x the value of those 3 seconds.

### Spillage and Breakage

Honest spillage (dropped bottles, broken glassware, spilled drinks) should run below 1% of beverage revenue. Track it daily on a waste sheet -- every spilled drink and broken item gets logged with a reason and a name. Not because you are building a case against your staff, but because tracking makes the team aware of the cost and naturally reduces carelessness.

Wine by the glass is the highest spillage risk because unsold portions of opened bottles get dumped. Manage this by limiting by-the-glass options, using preservation systems for premium bottles, and doing a daily end-of-shift count on open bottles.

### Theft Prevention

Beverage theft in Thai restaurants typically takes one of three forms: bartenders pouring drinks for friends without ringing them up, bartenders bringing their own spirits and selling them at your prices (pocketing the revenue), or staff consuming stock during or after shift.

**Prevention measures:**

- **Camera on the bar.** Not hidden -- visible. The deterrent effect is the point.
- **Ring-before-pour policy.** Every drink gets entered in the POS before it is made, no exceptions. This creates a paper trail that can be audited against inventory.
- **Weekly bottle counts.** Count every open and sealed bottle. Compare against POS sales. Discrepancies larger than 2-3% warrant investigation.
- **Sealed storeroom with restricted access.** Only the bar manager and one backup should have keys to the spirit store. A lock on the door is worth more than any surveillance system.

<Callout type="warning">
I once audited a bar in Sukhumvit that had a 31% blended pour cost on cocktails against a 18% target. The owner assumed it was over-pouring. After a two-week bottle count audit, we discovered the head bartender was bringing in his own bottles of cheap vodka, replacing them when they got low, and pocketing the sales revenue. The loss was approximately 45,000 THB/month. Weekly bottle counts with serial number or label tracking would have caught this in week one.
</Callout>

## Staff Training on Beverage Upselling

Your servers are the single most effective beverage sales tool you have, but only if they are trained and incentivised.

### The Beverage-First Greeting

The first interaction between server and table should be a drink order, not a food order. "What can I get you to drink while you look at the menu?" is the standard prompt, but a specific suggestion performs better: "Can I start you with a Thai iced tea, or would you like to see our cocktail menu?"

The specific prompt works because it eliminates the customer's need to think. "What would you like to drink?" requires the customer to recall their options, evaluate their preferences, and make a decision before they have even looked at the menu. "Thai iced tea or cocktail menu?" gives them two paths, both leading to a beverage order.

### Suggestive Selling That Does Not Feel Pushy

**Pairing suggestions with food orders.** When a table orders green curry, the server says: "That goes really well with our lemongrass cooler -- or if you prefer something stronger, the Thai basil gin cocktail is a great match." This frames the suggestion as expertise, not salesmanship.

**Second-round prompts.** When glasses are half-empty, the server approaches: "Can I get you another round, or would you like to try something different?" The timing matters -- half-empty glasses, not empty ones. By the time the glass is empty, the customer has mentally moved on to food or the bill.

**Dessert drink pairing.** When presenting the dessert menu: "We have a coconut cocktail that pairs perfectly with the mango sticky rice, if you are interested." Layering a drink suggestion onto a dessert prompt captures revenue from both categories.

### Incentive Structures

**Shift competition.** The server (or team) that generates the highest beverage revenue per cover during a shift wins a small prize -- 200-500 THB cash, a free meal, or early pick of shifts. This costs you almost nothing relative to the incremental revenue and creates a competitive dynamic where servers actively sell beverages.

**Product knowledge.** Run a 30-minute tasting session once a month where staff try the signature cocktails, key wines, and new additions. A server who has tasted the butterfly pea gin cocktail and can describe it from personal experience sells it 3-4x more effectively than one reading a description off a card.

## Happy Hour Economics: Does It Actually Work?

Happy hour is the most overused and least analysed promotion in Bangkok's restaurant and bar scene. Every second venue offers "buy one get one" or "50% off cocktails" from 4-7 PM, and most operators have no idea whether it is profitable.

### The Math on Buy-One-Get-One

A cocktail that costs you 35 THB to make, sold at 300 THB, gives you a 265 THB gross profit. During BOGO happy hour, the customer gets two cocktails for 300 THB. Your cost is now 70 THB, revenue is 300 THB, gross profit is 230 THB. You have given up 35 THB in profit (265 minus 230) to sell twice the volume.

The question is whether the discounted pricing brings in enough incremental traffic to offset the margin sacrifice. If the happy hour table would have come in anyway at full price, you lost money. If the table is genuinely incremental -- people who would not have visited without the promotion -- and they also order food at full margin, then happy hour works.

### When Happy Hour Makes Sense

**New venues building a customer base.** The first 3-6 months after opening, when you need bodies in seats and social proof. Happy hour drives trial visits. Once you have a regular crowd, reduce or eliminate the discount.

**Low-traffic time slots with fixed costs running.** If your bar is empty from 4-6 PM anyway, your rent, electricity, and staff costs are running regardless. Beverage sales at reduced margin are better than zero sales. But this logic has limits -- if happy hour cannibalises your 7-9 PM full-price trade (customers come early for the deal and leave before dinner), it is a net loss.

**As a food revenue driver, not a beverage promotion.** The most profitable happy hour I have seen in Bangkok was structured as "50% off cocktails with any food order over 300 THB." The discount brought customers in early, the food minimum ensured full-margin food sales, and the blended profitability of food plus discounted drinks still exceeded what an empty restaurant would have generated.

### When Happy Hour Destroys Value

**When it trains customers to wait for discounts.** If your regulars learn that cocktails are half price from 5-7 PM, they shift their visits to that window and stop coming at full price. You have not gained customers -- you have discounted your existing ones.

**When the discount is too deep.** Fifty percent off spirits-forward cocktails pushes your pour cost to 35-40%, which is breakeven or negative after labour and other costs. If you must discount, 20-30% off or a fixed-price deal (200 THB for a cocktail plus bar snack) protects your margin better than a percentage discount on drinks alone.

**When you are already full.** Discounting when demand already fills your seats is giving away money. If your bar is busy at 6 PM without a promotion, a happy hour from 5-7 PM is pure margin destruction.

## Seasonal Beverage Strategies

Thailand's seasons create natural demand shifts in beverages, and smart operators ride these instead of fighting them.

### Hot Season (March-May): Cold Drink Dominance

Temperatures hit 35-40 degrees Celsius. Cold beverages outsell everything else, and volume peaks.

**Push:** Fresh juices, Thai iced tea, frozen cocktails (frozen margarita, frose), beer towers. These are all high-margin categories and customer demand is at its highest -- you are not discounting to drive volume, you are meeting natural demand at full price.

**Seasonal specials:** "Mango Mojito" (mango is in season, so costs are lowest while demand is highest), "Watermelon Cooler," frozen Thai iced tea. Limited-time items create urgency and social media content.

**Skip:** Hot toddy, mulled wine, warm cocktails. Nobody orders these when it is 38 degrees outside. Remove them from the menu to reduce visual clutter and direct attention to what sells.

### Rainy Season (June-October): Comfort and Warmth

**Push:** Warm cocktails (hot buttered rum, warm toddy), hot chocolate, hot coffee, warm sake. Pair warm beverage promotions with comfort food specials (soups, curries, hot pots). "Rainy Day Set: Tom Yum + Thai Iced Coffee + Sticky Rice Dessert" at a bundled price.

**Beer note:** Beer sales typically dip 10-15% during rainy season as fewer customers walk in off the street. Counter this with [set meal bundles that include a beer](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok), which maintains volume while attaching a food order.

### Cool Season (November-February): Wine and Cocktail Peak

Tourist arrivals peak. Cocktail and wine consumption rises because the tourist segment drinks more than the Thai average, especially at dinner.

**Push:** Wine by the glass (tourist customers order wine at a higher rate than Thai customers), premium cocktails, and your highest-priced signature drinks. This is not the time to discount -- demand is up, so protect your margins.

**Wine dinners and tasting events.** Cool season is the time to host a "Wine Pairing Dinner" at 2,500-4,000 THB per head. The food cost is 30-35%, the wine cost is 25-30%, and the blended margin is excellent. These events also build your reputation as a serious F&B destination and generate content for social media and press.

## Delivery Beverage Considerations

Delivery platforms present a challenge for beverage sales because you cannot deliver alcohol on most platforms (GrabFood and LINE MAN prohibit alcohol delivery in Thailand), and non-alcoholic beverages travel poorly compared to food.

### What Works on Delivery

**Bottled and canned drinks:** Thai iced tea in sealed cups, bottled water, canned coffee. Low cost, no spillage risk, and they add 30-60 THB to the order value at 80%+ margin.

**Delivery-specific beverage bundles:** "Add a Thai Iced Tea for 49 THB" as an upsell prompt on the platform. Most delivery platforms let you configure add-on items at checkout. A 49 THB Thai iced tea costs you 8-12 THB. After 35% commission on the 49 THB (17 THB), your net is 32 THB minus 10 THB cost = 22 THB profit per add-on. Not huge on a per-unit basis, but across 50 delivery orders per day, that is 33,000 THB/month in incremental profit.

**Sealed packaging is mandatory.** Any drink going into a delivery bag needs a sealed lid, preferably a heat-sealed cup. Spilled drinks in delivery bags generate refund requests, bad ratings, and lost customers. Invest in proper sealed cups (1-3 THB each) -- the packaging cost is trivial compared to the refund and rating cost of a spill.

### What Does Not Work on Delivery

**Fresh juice (unless sealed immediately).** Juice oxidises and separates in transit. Watermelon juice turns brown, orange juice loses its fizz. If you offer juice on delivery, use sealed cups and note "best consumed within 30 minutes" -- but expect complaints regardless.

**Cocktail kits (mixed results).** Some Bangkok restaurants sell "cocktail kits" for delivery -- a bottle of premixed cocktail base with instructions. This can work for a brand-building exercise but it is not a volume play. The logistics are complex (ice melts, spirits cannot go through most delivery platforms), and the unit economics rarely justify the effort.

## Putting It All Together: A Beverage Engineering Checklist

Here is a 10-step checklist for engineering your beverage program.

1. **Calculate your current beverage-to-food revenue ratio.** If below 25%, your beverage program needs investment.

2. **Audit your pour costs by category.** Compare against the benchmarks in this article. If any category is more than 3 percentage points above target, investigate.

3. **Evaluate your beer selection.** Are you carrying brands nobody orders? Are you stocking craft beer that sits in the fridge for weeks? Streamline to 3-5 brands that actually sell.

4. **Launch or refine a by-the-glass wine program.** Limit to 4-6 options, match to your food, and track daily pours to manage waste.

5. **Build a signature cocktail menu.** 5-8 drinks that use Thai ingredients, photograph well, and carry 82%+ margins. Rotate quarterly.

6. **Invest in your non-alcoholic program.** Thai iced tea, 3-4 fresh juices, and 4-5 mocktails. This category serves every customer and carries the highest margins.

7. **Design a separate beverage menu** (or a prominent beverage section that appears before food). Add pairing suggestions, photos of signature drinks, and descriptions that justify your pricing.

8. **Train your servers.** Beverage-first greeting, pairing suggestions with food orders, second-round prompts, dessert drink pairing. Run monthly tastings.

9. **Implement inventory controls.** Jiggers mandatory, daily waste log, weekly bottle counts, ring-before-pour policy, locked storeroom.

10. **Decide on happy hour with clear metrics.** If you run one, track incremental covers (not just revenue) and compare blended profitability of happy hour tables versus regular-price tables. If the math does not work after 60 days, kill it.
