A restaurant I consult for in Sathorn added catering to their business 18 months ago. They started with a single corporate lunch order for 30 people that came through a regular customer. Today, catering accounts for 22% of their total revenue at a blended food cost of 24% -- six points lower than their dine-in operation. Their kitchen, which used to sit idle from 10 AM to 11:30 AM while the lunch crew prepped, now produces catering orders during that window and generates an additional 180,000-250,000 THB per month.

That is the promise of catering for Thai restaurants: additional revenue from an asset (your kitchen) that is already underutilized, at margins that are often better than dine-in because you control the menu, the portions, and the timing.

But catering is not just "make more food and deliver it." The menu engineering, pricing structure, logistics, and operational management are different from dine-in service in ways that catch operators off guard. A dish that is a Star on your restaurant menu might be a disaster in a catering context because it does not hold temperature, transport well, or present attractively at scale. A price point that works for a table of four does not translate directly to a per-head price for 80 people.

This guide covers how to build a catering service from your existing Thai restaurant operation: what to put on the menu, how to price it, how to manage the logistics, how to integrate catering with your restaurant schedule, and how to scale from the occasional event to a reliable revenue stream.

## Why Thai Restaurants Should Consider Catering

Before diving into the mechanics, here is the business case. Catering addresses three structural challenges that most restaurants face.

**Off-peak kitchen utilization.** Your kitchen is your most expensive asset -- rent, equipment, exhaust systems, utilities -- and it sits largely idle for significant portions of the day. Most Bangkok restaurants do meaningful volume from 11:30 AM to 1:30 PM (lunch) and 6 PM to 9 PM (dinner). That leaves the morning, mid-afternoon, and late evening as dead time. Catering orders, particularly corporate lunch deliveries and event prep, fill those gaps and generate revenue from an asset you are already paying for.

**Higher margins than dine-in.** Catering food costs typically run 20-28%, compared to 30-35% for dine-in at most Bangkok casual dining restaurants. The reasons: you control the menu (choosing your highest-margin items), you set minimum order sizes (eliminating low-ticket orders), you eliminate plate waste (portioning is predetermined), and you have no table service labor cost. When I run the math with clients, catering contribution margins are consistently 8-12 percentage points higher than dine-in.

**Revenue diversification.** A restaurant that depends entirely on dine-in is vulnerable to anything that reduces foot traffic: construction on the street, a new competitor, rainy season, a global pandemic. Catering creates a separate revenue channel with a different customer base (corporate offices, event planners, wedding coordinators) and different demand patterns. My Sathorn client's catering revenue actually increased during a two-month period when street construction reduced their dine-in traffic by 30%.

**Incremental revenue, not replacement.** Done correctly, catering revenue adds to your restaurant revenue without cannibalizing it. Your dine-in customers are not the same people ordering catering -- the corporate event planner and the couple looking for a Friday dinner are different buyers with different needs. This changes if you push too hard (covered in the cannibalization section at the end), but for most restaurants, catering is genuinely additive.

<Callout type="info">
The numbers in this article are based on casual and mid-range Thai restaurants in Bangkok with 40-80 seats and monthly dine-in revenue of 600,000-1,500,000 THB. Fine dining catering and large-scale event catering (200+ guests) have different economics and logistics that are beyond this article's scope.
</Callout>

## Types of Catering for Thai Restaurants

Not all catering is the same. Each type has different menu requirements, pricing models, logistics, and customer acquisition channels.

### Corporate Events and Meetings

**What it is:** Lunch or dinner service for corporate meetings, conferences, workshops, and company celebrations. Typical group size: 20-100 people.

**Menu format:** Buffet-style or set boxes. Thai food works exceptionally well for corporate catering because it is designed for communal eating, accommodates various dietary preferences, and has familiar options even for conservative eaters. A standard corporate catering menu includes 3-5 shared dishes plus rice, with optional add-ons for dessert and beverages.

**Pricing model:** Per-head pricing in tiers. A basic tier (3 dishes + rice) and a premium tier (5 dishes + rice + dessert + drink) let the client choose based on budget. Minimum order: 15-20 people.

**Frequency potential:** High. Corporate clients who are happy with your first delivery become repeat customers -- weekly lunch meetings, monthly team events, quarterly celebrations. One satisfied corporate client can generate 30,000-80,000 THB/month in recurring catering revenue.

**How to find them:** Direct outreach to office buildings near your restaurant, partnerships with building management, LinkedIn marketing, corporate event platforms, and word-of-mouth from your existing business-lunch customers.

### Weddings

**What it is:** Full catering service for wedding receptions, engagement parties, and pre-wedding events. Typical group size: 50-300 people.

**Menu format:** Multi-course served meals or premium buffets. Thai weddings typically feature 8-12 courses for formal receptions, or a lavish buffet with 10-15 options for more casual celebrations. The food is a centerpiece of the event and quality expectations are high.

**Pricing model:** Per-head pricing at premium rates (2-3x your corporate catering price), usually including service staff, setup, and equipment. Wedding catering is a high-margin, high-effort category.

**Frequency potential:** Seasonal (peak: November-February, the cool season). Each wedding is a one-time event, but wedding planners who trust your work will refer you repeatedly. One strong relationship with a busy wedding planner can generate 3-5 bookings per year.

**How to find them:** Partnerships with wedding planners and event coordinators, wedding venue relationships, social media portfolio (Instagram is the primary wedding discovery platform in Thailand), and bridal expos.

### Private Parties and Home Events

**What it is:** Catering for private birthday parties, housewarming events, family gatherings, and holiday celebrations. Typical group size: 15-50 people.

**Menu format:** Flexible -- buffet, family-style, or set menus depending on the client's preference and space. Thai home catering often features a mix of cooked-to-order items (fried dishes, stir-fries) and pre-prepared shared items (curries, soups, salads).

**Pricing model:** Per-head or flat-rate packages. Home events require more flexibility in menu customization than corporate events, so a consultation-based pricing approach works well.

**Frequency potential:** Moderate. Repeat business comes from clients who host frequently (some Thai families celebrate multiple events per year) and from referrals.

**How to find them:** Social media marketing (especially Facebook and Instagram), LINE Official Account promotions, existing restaurant customer outreach, and community group participation.

### Corporate Lunch Delivery (Daily/Weekly)

**What it is:** Pre-ordered lunch boxes or buffet-style setups delivered to offices on a recurring schedule. Typical group size: 10-50 per delivery.

**Menu format:** Boxed lunches with a rotating daily menu (one or two options per day) or weekly buffet setups. This is the catering type that integrates most naturally with restaurant operations because it uses your existing lunch menu items in a delivery-friendly format.

**Pricing model:** Per-box pricing (120-220 THB per box for casual Thai, 250-400 THB for premium) with minimum order quantities. Weekly or monthly contracts with set delivery schedules.

**Frequency potential:** Very high. A single office contract for 30 daily lunch boxes generates 72,000-132,000 THB/month at 120-220 THB per box. Two or three contracts and you have built a significant revenue stream that arrives predictably every week.

**How to find them:** Direct sales visits to office buildings within 5 km of your restaurant, building management partnerships, cold outreach to office managers via LinkedIn, and promotions through delivery platforms (GrabFood for Business, LINE MAN for Business).

### Event Pop-Ups

**What it is:** Setting up a temporary stall or cooking station at festivals, markets, corporate events, or public gatherings. You bring a simplified menu, cook on-site, and sell directly to attendees.

**Menu format:** 3-5 items maximum, chosen for speed of preparation, visual appeal, and eat-while-standing portability. Thai street food-inspired dishes work best: pad thai, satay, mango sticky rice, Thai iced tea.

**Pricing model:** Per-item pricing, typically at a premium over your restaurant prices (event pricing expectations are 15-30% higher than restaurant pricing). Some events charge a booth fee (5,000-30,000 THB) or take a revenue percentage (10-20%).

**Frequency potential:** Low to moderate. Events are sporadic and weather-dependent, but they provide excellent brand exposure and customer acquisition for your restaurant and catering services.

<Callout type="tip">
Start with corporate lunch delivery. It requires the least additional equipment, integrates most easily with your existing kitchen schedule, and builds a recurring revenue base. Once you have the logistics and production capacity figured out for daily corporate deliveries, expanding into larger events and weddings is a natural progression.
</Callout>

## Catering Menu Design: What Travels and What Does Not

The biggest menu engineering mistake in catering is putting your full restaurant menu on a catering flyer and assuming everything will work. It will not. Catering imposes three constraints that your dine-in menu does not face: transport time, holding temperature, and presentation at scale.

### Transport and Holding Quality

Between your kitchen and the client's table, your food sits in containers for 30-90 minutes. During that time, some dishes improve (curries meld, flavors develop) and some deteriorate catastrophically (crispy items go soggy, delicate presentations collapse, noodle dishes clump).

**Dishes that transport well:**
- **Curries** (green, red, massaman, panang) -- actually improve during transport as flavors merge. The best catering items in Thai cuisine.
- **Braised and stewed dishes** (pork belly, beef rendang-style) -- hold temperature and texture for 2+ hours
- **Fried rice and rice dishes** -- hold reasonably well if packed hot with proper insulation
- **Salads served at room temperature** (som tam, larb, yum) -- serve fresh or prepare components separately and assemble on-site
- **Soups and broths** (tom yum, tom kha) -- transport in insulated containers, hold well
- **Desserts** (sticky rice, Thai custards, tropical fruit platters) -- stable at room temperature

**Dishes that do NOT transport well:**
- **Pad thai and stir-fried noodles** -- clump within 20 minutes. If you must include them, cook to 80% and finish on-site, or accept that quality will be 70% of dine-in.
- **Deep-fried items** (fish cakes, spring rolls, tempura) -- lose crispness immediately. Only include if you can fry on-site.
- **Delicate plated presentations** -- anything that depends on vertical structure or precise placement will not survive transport. Design catering presentations for horizontal stability.
- **Items requiring immediate sauce application** -- keep sauces separate and apply at the destination

### Simplified Catering Menu vs. Full Menu

Your catering menu should be a curated subset of your restaurant menu, not a copy. I recommend 15-20 items total, organized into clear tiers.

**Base menu (8-10 items):** Your highest-margin dishes that transport well and appeal broadly. These form the core of your standard packages.

**Premium add-ons (5-7 items):** Higher-end options (seafood, premium proteins, specialty dishes) that clients can add to upgrade their package. These are where your margin expands.

**Desserts (2-3 items):** Simple, transportable options that complete the package without adding complexity.

This is a direct application of the [menu engineering framework](/blog/menu-engineering-thailand) -- your catering menu should be built from Stars (high popularity, high margin) and promoted Puzzles (high margin, lower popularity that you want to push). Plowhorses (popular but low margin) and Dogs (low on both) have no place on a catering menu where you control what gets offered.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not let clients build fully custom menus. Customization kills catering margins because it prevents batch production, complicates procurement, and increases kitchen errors. Offer 3-4 set packages with limited upgrade options. If a client wants a fully bespoke menu, charge a custom menu surcharge (15-25%) that covers the additional planning, procurement, and production complexity.
</Callout>

### Presentation at Scale

A green curry that looks beautiful in a single-serve bowl at your restaurant looks like a brown puddle in a hotel pan for 50 people unless you think about catering-specific presentation.

**Garnish for impact.** Fresh herbs, chili slices, kaffir lime leaves, and edible flowers cost almost nothing but transform the visual appeal of large-format dishes. Budget 5-10 THB per serving in garnish for catering.

**Use appropriate serving vessels.** Invest in attractive catering-grade serving bowls, platters, and chafers. The container is part of the presentation. Aluminum foil pans signal "cheap catering." Ceramic or stainless steel vessels signal "restaurant quality."

**Portion visually.** In buffet format, replenish dishes before they look depleted. A half-empty chafing dish looks sad; a three-quarters-full one looks abundant. For corporate deliveries, pack boxes to look generous -- food should touch all edges of the container.

## Pricing Catering: Per-Head Models and Package Tiers

Catering pricing works differently from restaurant menu pricing. You are not pricing individual dishes -- you are pricing a per-person experience that includes food, often service staff, and sometimes equipment.

### Per-Head Pricing Structure

### Setting Your Per-Head Price

The per-head price needs to cover four components: food cost, packaging, labor (including any on-site staff), and your margin. Here is how I calculate it with clients.

**Step 1: Determine your food cost per head.** Add up the ingredient cost of all dishes in the package, divided by the number of servings. For a standard tier package with 4 dishes + rice + dessert serving 30 people, calculate the total recipe cost for 30 portions of each item, then divide by 30. This gives you the per-head food cost.

**Step 2: Add packaging cost.** Boxes, containers, utensils, napkins, bags. For corporate lunch boxes: 15-25 THB per box. For buffet-style: 5-10 THB per head (amortized across reusable equipment and disposable items).

**Step 3: Add labor cost.** Kitchen labor for preparation (allocated from your existing team or additional catering prep staff) and, if applicable, on-site service staff (600-1,200 THB per server per event). For small corporate deliveries with no on-site staff, labor allocation is 15-30 THB per head. For served events, labor can reach 80-150 THB per head.

**Step 4: Calculate your target margin.** After food cost, packaging, and labor, your target margin should be 45-55% of the per-head price. This is higher than typical dine-in margins because catering has no rent allocation (you are using your existing kitchen), no table service overhead, and no front-of-house costs.

**Example calculation for a standard tier (4 dishes + rice + dessert, 30 people):**
- Food cost per head: 85 THB
- Packaging per head: 8 THB
- Labor allocation per head: 25 THB
- Total cost per head: 118 THB
- Target price at 50% margin: 236 THB
- Rounded to: 250 THB per head
- Revenue for 30 people: 7,500 THB
- Gross profit: 3,960 THB

At that margin, a restaurant fulfilling 4-5 catering orders per week at 30 people each generates 60,000-75,000 THB in monthly gross profit from catering alone -- with minimal additional overhead.

### Minimum Orders

Set minimum order sizes to ensure each catering job is worth your kitchen's time and logistics effort. For corporate lunch boxes, minimum 10-15 boxes. For buffet-style catering, minimum 20 people. For event catering with on-site staff, minimum 30 people. Below these thresholds, the per-unit economics degrade because the fixed costs of preparation, packaging, and delivery do not scale down proportionally.

### Surcharges and Add-Ons

**Weekend and holiday surcharge:** 15-20% markup for orders requiring weekend or public holiday preparation and delivery. Your staff costs increase and your opportunity cost (lost restaurant prep time) is higher.

**Rush order surcharge:** 20-30% for orders placed less than 48 hours in advance. Standard lead time should be 3-5 business days for events and 24-48 hours for lunch box orders.

**On-site service staff:** 800-1,500 THB per server, depending on event duration and service complexity. Clearly define what "service" includes: setup, replenishment, cleanup.

**Equipment rental:** If you provide chafing dishes, serving equipment, or tables, charge a flat equipment fee (2,000-8,000 THB depending on the scale) or include it in the premium tier pricing.

**Delivery fee:** Based on distance. Free within 5 km of your restaurant (incentivizes nearby offices), 300-800 THB for 5-15 km, and case-by-case pricing beyond 15 km. For events requiring a van or truck, the delivery fee should cover actual vehicle and driver costs plus a margin.

## Food Cost Targets for Catering

Catering food costs should be lower than your dine-in food costs because you have more control over the menu, portions, and procurement.

**Target ranges by catering type:**
- **Corporate lunch boxes:** 22-28% food cost. You control the daily menu, buy in known quantities, and can use cost-effective proteins and preparations.
- **Corporate buffet catering:** 24-30% food cost. Slightly higher because buffets require visual abundance (you prepare 10-15% more food than you expect people to eat).
- **Wedding and formal event catering:** 28-32% food cost. Premium ingredients (seafood, imported items) push costs up, but per-head pricing is also significantly higher.
- **Event pop-ups:** 20-25% food cost. Limited menu, high-volume production, event pricing premiums.

Compare these to the typical dine-in food cost of 30-35% for Bangkok casual dining, and the margin advantage of catering becomes clear. For a detailed breakdown of food cost management techniques, including purchasing strategies and waste reduction, see [Food Cost Control for Bangkok Restaurants](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok).

<Callout type="info">
The biggest food cost lever in catering is waste reduction. In dine-in service, customers leave food on plates, orders get returned, and prep waste is ongoing. In catering, you prepare exact quantities for a known headcount, portion is predetermined, and plate waste is zero (the client manages leftovers, not you). This structural difference is why catering food costs can run 6-10 percentage points below dine-in.
</Callout>

## Catering Logistics: Transport, Equipment, and Staffing

Logistics is where catering either works smoothly or falls apart. The food can be perfect, but if it arrives late, cold, or disheveled, the client will not call you again.

### Transport

**Temperature control.** Hot food must stay above 60 degrees Celsius; cold food must stay below 5 degrees Celsius. In Bangkok's heat, uninsulated containers will push hot food into the danger zone within 30-40 minutes. Invest in insulated transport containers (cambros or equivalent). Budget 3,000-8,000 THB per container; you need 4-6 for a small catering operation.

**Vehicle.** For lunch box deliveries (10-30 boxes), a motorcycle with an insulated delivery box or a compact car works. For buffet catering (20-50 people), you need a van or small truck. For large events (50+ people), a full-size van or multiple trips. Some operators lease a van for catering days (1,500-2,500 THB/day); others invest in a dedicated vehicle once catering revenue justifies the fixed cost.

**Timing.** Build a 30-minute buffer into every delivery estimate. Bangkok traffic is unpredictable, and arriving 15 minutes early is professional while arriving 15 minutes late is a crisis. For morning corporate deliveries, production must start by 7-8 AM, which means your kitchen opens earlier on catering days.

### Staffing for Catering

**Kitchen production.** For small corporate deliveries (10-30 people), your existing kitchen staff can handle catering prep during off-peak hours with minimal additional labor. For larger events (50+ people), you may need additional prep cooks on catering days. Budget 400-600 THB per extra prep cook per day.

**Delivery.** A dedicated driver for catering deliveries costs 500-800 THB per trip (motorcycle) or 1,000-1,500 THB per trip (van with driver). For daily corporate deliveries, a part-time driver on a monthly arrangement (8,000-12,000 THB/month for 5 days/week) is more economical.

**On-site service.** For events that include service staff, hire experienced part-time servers at 600-1,200 THB per event (4-6 hours). Event staffing agencies in Bangkok can provide trained servers on short notice, typically at 800-1,200 THB per person including agency commission. [Staff management for event-specific hires](/blog/restaurant-staffing-thailand) follows similar principles to restaurant staffing, with the additional requirement of briefing staff on the specific event and menu.

## Thai Catering Traditions

Thailand has several cultural contexts where catering is not just a business opportunity but an expectation. Understanding these traditions helps you position your catering service and design appropriate menus.

### Monks' Morning Alms and Merit-Making

**Tak bat (ตักบาตร):** The daily morning alms round where monks collect food from laypeople. Restaurants are sometimes engaged to prepare food for organized group alms-giving events, particularly at temples during important Buddhist holidays. The food must be prepared before dawn and is typically simple, generous, and vegetarian-friendly.

**Corporate merit-making events (ทำบุญบริษัท):** Thai companies frequently organize merit-making ceremonies at the office, inviting monks for morning chanting and an alms meal. This is one of the most consistent corporate catering opportunities in Thailand -- typically 9-13 place settings for monks plus food for 20-100 employees. The menu includes both monk-appropriate food (served before noon, no alcohol) and a general lunch spread for staff.

**Pricing for merit-making catering:** 200-400 THB per head for the staff meal, plus 500-1,000 THB per monk setting (premium ingredients, more elaborate presentation, specific dietary requirements). A typical corporate merit-making event for 9 monks and 50 staff generates 14,500-24,500 THB in revenue.

### Weddings (Thai Style)

Thai weddings involve multiple food-serving occasions: the morning monks' chanting ceremony (breakfast for monks and family), the afternoon reception (the main event, 100-500 guests), and sometimes a post-ceremony dinner for close family. Traditional Thai wedding menus include auspicious dishes: whole fish (abundance), glass noodle dishes (long life), sweets shaped like gold (prosperity), and nine-dish courses (the number nine is auspicious in Thai culture).

### Funerals and Memorial Services

Thai funeral catering is a significant and overlooked market. Funeral ceremonies at temples typically span 3-7 days, with food provided to monks and attendees at each evening ceremony. The food is traditionally donated by the bereaved family and their associates, but many families now hire caterers to handle the volume. Menus are simpler and more conservative than wedding menus, with an emphasis on familiar comfort food served in generous quantities.

### Seasonal Events

**Songkran (April):** Company Songkran parties and family gatherings create a concentrated catering demand in the first two weeks of April.

**Loy Krathong (November):** Riverside and outdoor celebrations, often with event catering.

**Chinese New Year (January/February):** Corporate and family banquets, particularly in Bangkok's Chinese-Thai business community. Menus feature auspicious Chinese-Thai dishes.

**Year-end parties (December):** The single busiest period for corporate catering in Thailand. Office holiday parties, year-end celebrations, and client appreciation events create high demand. Book early -- popular caterers are fully committed by mid-November.

<Callout type="tip">
Build a "merit-making catering package" specifically designed for corporate religious ceremonies. This is a niche that most competing caterers ignore, but the demand is consistent (many Thai companies do merit-making events 1-4 times per year) and the margins are strong because the expectations are well-defined and the menu is traditional.
</Callout>

## Marketing Your Catering Service

Catering customers find you through different channels than restaurant customers. Your marketing strategy needs to target these specific channels.

### Corporate Sales

**Direct outreach to nearby offices.** Walk into office buildings within 5 km of your restaurant with a printed catering menu and a sample box of your food. The office manager or admin coordinator who handles catering decisions is your target. A cold visit with a free sample is dramatically more effective than an email that will be ignored.

**Building management partnerships.** Talk to the management offices of commercial buildings near your restaurant. Many building managers maintain a list of recommended caterers that they share with tenants. Getting on that list provides a steady stream of inquiries.

**LinkedIn marketing.** For targeting HR managers, office managers, and event coordinators at specific companies. A simple LinkedIn post showing a recent corporate catering setup with a brief testimonial generates more qualified leads than paid advertising for most restaurant-based caterers.

### Wedding and Event Channels

**Wedding planner relationships.** Identify the 10-15 most active wedding planners in Bangkok and schedule tasting sessions with each. A planner who trusts your food and reliability will recommend you to multiple clients per year. This is the single highest-ROI marketing investment for wedding catering.

**Event agency partnerships.** Corporate event agencies handle large-scale company events and need reliable catering partners. The agency takes a 10-15% commission but provides a stream of large, high-value bookings.

**Instagram portfolio.** Create a dedicated section of your restaurant's Instagram (or a separate catering-focused account) showcasing your best catering setups. Professional photos of buffet spreads, boxed lunches, and event presentations serve as your visual portfolio. Wedding clients in particular discover caterers through Instagram.

### Digital Presence

**Catering page on your restaurant website.** A dedicated page with your catering menu, pricing tiers, photos, and a contact form or LINE Official Account link. This page should target "[your area] catering" and "Thai restaurant catering Bangkok" search queries.

**Google Business Profile.** Add "catering" as a service category on your Google Business listing. Many corporate event coordinators search "catering near [location]" on Google Maps.

**LINE Official Account.** A dedicated LINE account (or a menu within your existing restaurant LINE account) for catering inquiries and orders makes communication easy for Thai corporate clients who live on LINE.

For a broader look at [restaurant marketing strategies in Thailand](/blog/restaurant-marketing-thailand), the digital and relationship-building approaches apply directly to catering promotion.

## Managing Catering Alongside Restaurant Operations

The operational challenge of catering is that it uses the same kitchen and many of the same staff as your restaurant. Poor integration leads to either catering quality suffering (because the kitchen prioritizes dine-in) or restaurant quality suffering (because catering prep overwhelms the kitchen during service).

### Kitchen Scheduling

**Dedicated catering production windows.** Block specific times for catering prep that do not overlap with your dine-in service peaks. For most Bangkok restaurants:
- **6:00-10:00 AM:** Catering prep (before lunch service begins)
- **2:00-4:30 PM:** Afternoon catering prep (between lunch and dinner service)
- **After closing:** Large event prep for next-day delivery

If catering volume grows to the point where these windows are insufficient, you have outgrown kitchen-sharing and need to consider a dedicated catering prep space (covered in the scaling section).

**Separate prep lists.** Catering prep should have its own written production list, separate from the restaurant prep list. The kitchen manager assigns specific staff to catering prep during designated windows. Mixing catering and restaurant prep on the same list leads to confusion and forgotten items.

**Advance scheduling.** Catering orders should be confirmed 3-5 days in advance (24-48 hours for lunch boxes). This lead time allows the kitchen manager to plan procurement, allocate staff, and schedule production without disrupting restaurant operations. Rush orders disrupt the system and should be charged a premium.

### Staff Allocation

**Cross-training.** At minimum, 2-3 of your kitchen staff should be capable of executing the full catering menu without supervision. This provides flexibility for scheduling -- you do not want catering to collapse because one person calls in sick.

**Overtime management.** Catering prep often means earlier start times for kitchen staff. Budget for the overtime cost and build it into your catering pricing. A cook starting at 6 AM instead of 9 AM to handle morning catering prep generates 3 hours of overtime per shift -- at 1.5x pay, that is an additional 150-225 THB per cook per catering day.

**Event-day staffing.** For catered events that require on-site service, schedule event staff separately from your restaurant floor team. Pulling your dine-in servers to work a catering event leaves your restaurant understaffed. Use part-time event staff or an event staffing agency for on-site catering.

<Callout type="warning">
The number-one operational mistake in restaurant catering: accepting a catering order that your kitchen cannot fulfill without compromising restaurant quality. If a large event order arrives for the same evening you expect a busy dinner service, and your kitchen cannot handle both, decline the catering order or negotiate a different date. Your restaurant reputation is worth more than one catering job.
</Callout>

## Catering Contracts and Deposits

Protect yourself with clear terms. Verbal agreements with corporate clients and event hosts lead to disputes over headcount, timing, and payment.

**Written orders for every job.** Even a simple 20-person lunch delivery should have a written order confirmation specifying: date, time, delivery address, headcount, menu selection, per-head price, total cost, payment terms, and cancellation policy. A simple order form template takes 30 minutes to create and prevents 90% of catering disputes.

**Deposit policy:** Require 50% deposit upon booking for events over 30 people. For weddings and large events, require 50% at booking and the remaining 50% at least 7 days before the event. No deposit, no confirmed booking -- this protects you from last-minute cancellations after you have already purchased ingredients and allocated staff.

**Cancellation terms:** Full refund if cancelled 7+ days before the event. 50% refund if cancelled 3-7 days before. No refund if cancelled less than 3 days before. For perishable ingredient orders already placed, consider no refund regardless of timing for the ingredient cost portion.

**Payment terms for corporate clients:** Net 30 is standard for corporate accounts with established relationships. For first-time corporate clients, require payment on delivery or net 7. Do not extend net 30 terms until a client has completed at least 3 orders without payment issues.

**Headcount adjustments:** Allow headcount adjustments up to 48 hours before the event, with a floor of 80% of the original booking. If the client booked for 100 people and reduces to 60 at the last minute, you invoice for at least 80 people. This protects you from the scenario where procurement and prep are complete for 100 and the client decides 60 is enough.

## Common Catering Mistakes

These are the errors I see repeatedly when Thai restaurants add catering to their business.

**Underpricing to win the first job.** Operators who price their first catering orders at near-cost to "get the foot in the door" set a price expectation they cannot raise without losing the client. Price fairly from the start. If your food and service are good, clients will pay the real price.

**Over-promising on menu customization.** Saying "we can do anything you want" to every catering inquiry leads to a different menu for every order, which destroys kitchen efficiency and procurement planning. Offer set packages with limited customization. Unlimited flexibility sounds customer-friendly but produces operational chaos.

**Underestimating logistics.** "We will just put the food in the car and drive it over" is not a logistics plan. Without proper insulated transport containers, timing discipline, and a setup process, your beautifully prepared food arrives lukewarm, disorganized, and unpresentable. Invest in the logistics infrastructure before accepting your first order.

**No dedicated catering procurement.** Buying catering ingredients alongside your restaurant order and hoping the kitchen sorts it out leads to shortages, substitutions, and confusion. Catering procurement should be a separate order placed 2-3 days before the event, based on the confirmed headcount and menu.

**Ignoring the cost of free samples.** Tasting sessions for prospective wedding clients and corporate decision-makers are a marketing expense, not free food. Budget 500-1,500 THB per tasting session and track your conversion rate (tasting sessions that become bookings). If your conversion rate is below 30%, your food or presentation needs work; if it is above 50%, your tastings are effective marketing spend.

**No feedback loop.** After every catering job, ask the client for feedback: Was the food good? Was the quantity sufficient? Was the timing acceptable? Was the presentation appropriate? This information is how you improve. [Menu engineering for catering](/blog/menu-pricing-psychology-thailand) is an ongoing process -- your first catering menu will not be your best.

## Scaling from Occasional to Regular Catering

The transition from "we do catering sometimes" to "catering is 15-25% of our revenue" requires deliberate scaling.

### Phase 1: Testing (Months 1-3)

Accept 2-4 catering orders per month, primarily from existing restaurant customers and nearby offices. Use your existing kitchen, staff, and basic packaging. Focus on learning: what sells, what transports well, what clients complain about, what the real costs are. Keep detailed records of every order's revenue, cost, and margin.

### Phase 2: Systemizing (Months 3-6)

Based on Phase 1 data, finalize your catering menu (cutting items that do not transport well or sell poorly), set your pricing tiers, create your order form and contract templates, invest in insulated transport containers and basic catering equipment, and train 2-3 kitchen staff specifically on catering production. Target: 6-10 orders per month.

### Phase 3: Marketing and Growth (Months 6-12)

Launch dedicated catering marketing: website page, social media content, direct outreach to offices, and wedding planner relationships. Hire a part-time catering coordinator (or assign an existing staff member) to handle inquiries, bookings, and logistics coordination. Target: 12-20 orders per month.

### Phase 4: Dedicated Operations (Month 12+)

If catering exceeds 20% of total revenue, consider separating catering operations: a dedicated catering prep area (even a small section of your existing kitchen partitioned for catering), a dedicated catering coordinator (full-time), a delivery vehicle, and potentially a small team of part-time event staff. At this point, catering is no longer a side project -- it is a business unit that needs its own P&L tracking and management attention.

## When Catering Cannibalizes Your Restaurant

There is a point where catering stops being additive and starts competing with your restaurant for kitchen capacity, staff attention, and management focus. Watch for these signals.

**Kitchen bottlenecks during service.** If your cooks are still finishing catering prep when lunch or dinner service starts, catering has exceeded your kitchen's off-peak capacity. Either reduce catering volume, extend your kitchen hours (with corresponding staff costs), or invest in a separate prep space.

**Staff burnout.** Cooks and managers handling both catering and restaurant operations work longer hours and make more decisions per day. If your catering volume requires consistent overtime from your core team, you need additional dedicated catering staff -- not more hours from the same people.

**Quality decline in the restaurant.** If your restaurant's food quality or service drops after you added catering, you have overextended. This is the clearest sign that catering has grown beyond what your existing operation can support without investment in additional capacity.

**Management distraction.** If you (the owner or GM) are spending more time on catering logistics than on restaurant management, your dine-in business will suffer. Delegate catering coordination to a dedicated person before it absorbs too much of your attention.

The solution to cannibalization is not to stop catering -- it is to invest in the infrastructure that separates catering operations from restaurant operations. A dedicated prep space, dedicated staff, and a dedicated coordinator allow both businesses to run at full capacity without competing for the same resources.

<Callout type="info">
My rule of thumb: once catering consistently exceeds 15% of your total revenue for three consecutive months, it needs its own budget, its own staff allocation, and its own P&L tracking. Treating it as a casual add-on beyond this point leads to one or both businesses underperforming.
</Callout>
