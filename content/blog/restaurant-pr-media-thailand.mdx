A client opened a modern Isaan restaurant in Ekkamai last year with a genuinely interesting concept -- northeastern Thai food reinterpreted through a fine dining lens, with a fermentation program and a natural wine list that actually made sense with the food. The cooking was excellent. The space was beautiful. And for the first three months, the only coverage was a single Instagram post from a friend of the sous chef with 800 followers.

The owner had assumed that being good was enough. That the Michelin inspectors would find him, that BK Magazine would wander in, that word of mouth among Bangkok's food community would do the rest. None of that happened, because none of that happens on its own. Bangkok opens hundreds of restaurants every month. Without a deliberate PR strategy, even a strong concept disappears into the noise.

I stepped in during month four. We organized a media dinner, built relationships with three key food editors, submitted for Michelin consideration, and launched a targeted outreach campaign. Within eight weeks, the restaurant had been featured in BK Magazine, Wongnai editorial, two Thai food YouTube channels, and a Timeout Bangkok list. Covers went from 60% to full most nights.

The food was always good. The PR is what made people aware it existed.

This guide covers Thailand's food media ecosystem, how to get coverage, when to hire a PR agency, how to handle press events, award submissions, and crisis management -- with real costs and practical steps that work in the Thai market.

## Thailand's Food Media Ecosystem

Understanding who covers food in Thailand, and how they operate, is the first step. The food media world here is smaller and more relationship-driven than in New York or London, which works in your favor once you understand the dynamics.

### English-Language Food Media

**BK Magazine (bk.asia-city.com).** Bangkok's longest-running English-language lifestyle publication and still the most influential for restaurant coverage among expats and English-speaking Thais. Their restaurant reviews carry weight. A positive BK review drives measurable traffic, particularly from the affluent expat community and young Thai professionals who consume English-language media.

BK publishes restaurant reviews, "Best of" lists, new opening coverage, and sponsored content. They have a small editorial team -- roughly 3-4 dedicated food writers at any given time -- so building a personal relationship with even one of them is achievable and valuable. Their annual Best Restaurants list is widely followed.

**Timeout Bangkok (timeout.com/bangkok).** Part of the global Time Out network with a dedicated Bangkok food section. Their "Best Restaurants" and "Best New Restaurants" lists generate significant click-through traffic, while their editorial team accepts pitches for new openings and food stories. Timeout also runs sponsored content programs -- paid features that are clearly labelled as such -- starting around 30,000-60,000 THB.

**BK and Timeout** together reach the majority of Bangkok's English-language dining audience. If you get a positive mention in both, you have effectively covered the English-speaking market.

**The Standard (thestandard.co).** A newer entrant that has gained traction with Bangkok's creative and design-conscious crowd. Their food coverage is less frequent than BK or Timeout but tends toward more in-depth features. Good for concepts with a strong design or cultural story.

**Eater Bangkok.** Eater's Bangkok coverage is sporadic and typically handled by freelance contributors based in the city. A feature on Eater reaches an international audience that includes travelers planning Bangkok trips, which makes it particularly valuable for tourist-adjacent restaurants. Pitching requires reaching the right freelancer, as there is no dedicated Bangkok editor.

### Thai-Language Food Media

**Wongnai Editorial.** Beyond its review platform, Wongnai publishes curated editorial content -- "Top 10" lists, neighborhood guides, and restaurant features. A spot on a Wongnai editorial list drives enormous Thai customer traffic because it reaches the platform's 10+ million monthly users within the context of their restaurant search behavior. This is not a magazine feature they might forget -- it is a recommendation that appears when they are actively deciding where to eat.

**Silpa Wattanatham (ศิลปวัฒนธรรม).** Thailand's cultural affairs magazine, published by Matichon Group. Their food coverage focuses on heritage cuisine, traditional cooking techniques, and the cultural stories behind food. If your restaurant has a cultural narrative -- preserving regional recipes, working with traditional artisans, celebrating a specific culinary heritage -- Silpa Wattanatham provides the kind of credibility that advertising cannot buy.

**Thai PBS Food Segments.** Thailand's public broadcaster runs food segments across several programs that reach a broad Thai audience including demographics that do not engage with digital food media. Coverage on Thai PBS is particularly valuable for restaurants outside Bangkok, as their programming reaches nationwide viewers.

**Sanook Food Section.** One of Thailand's highest-traffic web portals, and their food content -- restaurant recommendations, food trends, cooking guides -- reaches a massive mainstream Thai audience. Less influential per-reader than BK or Wongnai editorial, but the sheer reach makes it worth pursuing.

**Pantip (pantip.com).** Thailand's largest online forum, where food discussions in the "Blue Planet" (ห้องสีน้ำเงิน) food subforum can generate significant buzz. Pantip is not media you pitch to -- it is organic conversation. But a popular Pantip thread about your restaurant can drive more Thai customers than a magazine feature. Monitor it, but do not try to manufacture it.

### Food YouTube and Content Creators

**Mark Wiens.** The most-watched food YouTuber in Thailand with over 10 million subscribers. His content reaches a global audience of food travelers. A feature from Mark Wiens puts a restaurant on the international food tourism map. However, he is selective, typically covers street food and traditional restaurants rather than fine dining, and is not available for paid features -- his content is editorially independent. If your restaurant fits his niche, reach out with a genuine invitation rather than a business proposal.

**Thai food YouTube channels.** Channels like Gindai, Kin Kub Pee, Tan Jiab Gang, and dozens of mid-tier food review channels (50K-500K subscribers) produce regular restaurant coverage in Thai. These creators often accept invitations for complimentary meals and some accept paid features (5,000-30,000 THB depending on channel size and scope). Their audience skews younger Thai, and a positive video review generates a visible spike in reservations that typically lasts 2-4 weeks.

**The overlap with [influencer marketing](/blog/influencer-marketing-restaurants-thailand).** There is a blurry line between food media creators and influencers. The distinction matters: food media creators produce content that informs (reviews, guides, comparisons), while influencers produce content that promotes (aesthetic photos, lifestyle integration). Both are useful, but they serve different functions in your PR strategy. Media creators build credibility. Influencers build awareness.

<Callout type="info">
Bangkok's food media community is small enough that the same 30-40 people -- editors, freelance writers, YouTubers, and photographers -- show up at most restaurant events. This is an advantage. Building relationships with a relatively small group of people can generate outsized coverage. The downside: the community talks, so a bad press event or difficult interaction circulates fast.
</Callout>

## English vs. Thai Media Strategy

This is a strategic decision that too many restaurant owners skip past. Your media strategy should be determined by your customer base, not by your personal media consumption habits.

**If your customers are primarily Thai:** Invest 70-80% of your PR effort in Thai-language media. Wongnai editorial, Thai food YouTubers, Sanook, Pantip presence, and Thai-language social media coverage will generate more foot traffic than any English publication. The exception is Michelin -- that audience is language-agnostic.

**If your customers are primarily expat or tourist:** Flip the ratio. BK Magazine, Timeout, Eater, TripAdvisor editorial, and English-language food bloggers are your targets. Google Maps reviews in English matter more than Wongnai for this audience.

**If you want both (most ambitious restaurants do):** Run parallel campaigns. A media dinner with a mixed guest list -- 60% Thai media, 40% English media -- covers both audiences in one event, but the follow-up and relationship management should be tailored to each group separately.

**The language of your press materials matters.** If you are targeting Thai media, your press kit needs to be in Thai. Not translated from English -- written in Thai, with Thai-appropriate framing. A press release that reads like a translation will be deprioritized or ignored by Thai editors. Budget for a Thai copywriter if your team does not include a native Thai writer.

## Building Relationships with Food Journalists and Editors

PR in Thailand is relationship-driven to a degree that surprises many foreign operators. The formal pitch-to-editor process that works in New York is less effective here. Personal relationships, LINE contacts, and face-to-face interaction carry more weight.

### How to Start

**Follow and engage first.** Before you pitch anything, follow the food writers who cover your type of cuisine and your neighborhood. Read their work. Comment thoughtfully on their social media posts. Share their content. This is not flattery -- it is showing that you are part of the conversation, not just someone who appears when they want something.

**Invite them to eat, no strings attached.** Once you have a sense of which writers might be interested in your restaurant, send a personal invitation for a complimentary meal. Not a mass email to 50 editors. A specific, personalized message to one person, referencing something they have written that connects to what you are doing. "I read your piece on fermented ingredients in modern Thai cooking and thought you might be interested in what we are doing with our fermentation program. Would love to have you in for dinner."

**Be a source, not just a restaurant.** Food journalists need expert sources for trend pieces, market analysis, and industry stories. If you can be the restaurant operator who provides intelligent commentary on food trends in Thailand, supplier issues, or the business side of restaurants, you become a contact they call for quotes -- which keeps your name and your restaurant in their editorial consciousness.

**Respect their independence.** A complimentary meal is not a purchased review. Good food journalists maintain editorial independence, and the ones worth getting coverage from will write honestly about your restaurant. Do not ask to review the piece before publication. Do not get angry if the review includes criticism. Professionals respect operators who respect the process.

### Maintaining Relationships

**Seasonal updates.** When you launch a new menu, source a notable ingredient, or make a meaningful change to your concept, send a brief update to your media contacts. Not every update justifies coverage, but it keeps you visible and gives them the option to write about it if it fits an upcoming story.

**Include them in genuine milestones.** Anniversaries, awards, chef changes, expansion plans -- share these with your media contacts before they become public news. Giving a journalist the story first builds goodwill and makes them more likely to cover it.

**LINE, not email.** In Thailand, most professional food media communication happens on LINE. Email pitches get buried or ignored. A LINE message gets read within hours. Once you have established a relationship with a writer or editor, exchange LINE contacts and use that as your primary communication channel.

<Callout type="tip">
Keep a media contacts spreadsheet with each journalist's name, outlet, coverage focus, LINE ID, email, recent articles about restaurants similar to yours, and the date of your last interaction. Review this quarterly. If you have not contacted someone in 3+ months, find a reason to reach out -- even if it is just sharing a relevant industry article or congratulating them on a piece they published.
</Callout>

## Writing a Press Release That Gets Attention

Most restaurant press releases are terrible. They read like the restaurant's "About" page crossed with a real estate listing -- full of adjectives, empty of information. Editors scan dozens of these per week, and the bad ones get deleted in seconds.

### What Works

**Lead with the story, not the restaurant.** "A new Isaan restaurant opens in Ekkamai" is not a story. "A former Nahm chef is reinterpreting northeastern fermented ingredients through a fine dining lens" is a story. Editors cover stories, not openings.

**Be specific.** "Our farm-to-table concept sources locally" is meaningless. "We buy directly from four organic farms in Nakhon Pathom, including Rai Ruen Rom, and receive deliveries every 48 hours" is specific and verifiable. Specific details make a journalist's job easier, which makes coverage more likely.

**Include the numbers that matter.** Seat count, average check price, opening date, address, opening hours. Journalists need these logistics, and burying them or leaving them out means they have to follow up, which means they might not bother.

**Keep it to one page.** Anything longer gets skimmed. One page of tight, informative writing with a clear story angle, key logistics, and contact information. Attach high-resolution photos as separate files, not embedded in the document.

**Write a subject line that works.** For email pitches: "[Restaurant Name] Opening [Date] -- [One-Line Story Hook]." For LINE messages: even shorter. The subject line determines whether the message gets opened.

### What Fails

**Superlative overload.** "The best new dining experience in Bangkok" -- says who? "An unparalleled culinary journey" -- stop. Let the journalist decide if your restaurant is remarkable. Your job is to give them the facts and the story that let them reach that conclusion.

**Mass distribution.** Sending the same press release to 100 media contacts is obvious and ineffective. Personalize. At minimum, change the opening line to reference the journalist's coverage area or a recent piece they wrote. Ideally, tailor the pitch angle to each publication's audience.

**No photos.** A press release without professional food photography is incomplete. Editors need images to run a story. Send 5-10 high-resolution images (minimum 300 DPI, 2000+ pixels on the long edge) as a downloadable link -- not as email attachments that clog their inbox.

## Media Kit: What Every Restaurant Needs

Your media kit is a package of materials ready to send to any journalist, editor, or influencer at a moment's notice. Having it prepared means you can respond to media interest within minutes instead of scrambling to pull materials together.

### What to Include

**Fact sheet (one page).** Restaurant name, concept in one sentence, cuisine type, head chef name and brief bio, seat count, opening date, address, neighborhood, nearest BTS/MRT, opening hours, average check, reservation method (LINE, phone, walk-in), social media handles, website.

**Concept narrative (one page).** The story behind your restaurant. Why does it exist? What problem does it solve or what experience does it create? What is the chef's background and point of view? This is the page a journalist uses to understand the "why" behind your restaurant.

**Menu (current).** A PDF or high-quality image of your current menu. Update this in your media kit whenever the menu changes.

**Photography (10-15 images).** 5-7 food shots of signature dishes, 2-3 interior/atmosphere shots, 1-2 chef/team portraits, 1 exterior shot. All high-resolution, professionally shot, with captions listing dish names for the food shots. Store these as downloadable links (Google Drive, Dropbox) rather than sending full-size files. Include a note granting media permission to use the images with credit.

**Contact information.** A direct contact (name, phone, LINE, email) who can respond to media inquiries within a few hours. Not a general info@ email. A person.

**Press coverage (if applicable).** Links to existing reviews, features, and mentions. Once you have coverage, it snowballs -- journalists are more likely to cover restaurants that other journalists have already covered.

<Callout type="warning">
Update your media kit every quarter, or whenever you make a significant change (new chef, new menu, renovation, concept pivot). Sending a journalist a media kit with last year's menu and photos of a dining room that has been redesigned signals that you are not paying attention to your own PR. A current media kit signals professionalism.
</Callout>

## Press Events and Media Dinners

A well-executed media dinner is the single highest-ROI PR activity for a restaurant. It gives journalists the full experience of your food, your space, and your team in a setting that is designed for them to understand and evaluate your concept. A poorly executed one wastes food, money, and relationships.

### Planning

**Guest list size.** 10-20 people is the right range. Fewer than 10 feels too intimate (some guests will not come if the table is too small), while more than 20 dilutes the experience and makes it harder for you and your chef to interact meaningfully with guests. For a new opening, aim for 12-15.

**Guest list composition.** Mix food editors (BK, Timeout, Wongnai editorial), freelance food writers, 2-3 food YouTubers or content creators, and 1-2 key influencers who overlap with media (people who both post and write). Include both Thai and English media if your audience spans both.

**Timing.** Hold the event 1-3 weeks before your public opening or major menu launch. This gives attendees time to write, edit, and publish before the news goes stale. Tuesday through Thursday evenings work best -- editors avoid Friday and Saturday events because those are their busiest nights for other coverage.

**Format.** Serve a curated menu of 5-7 dishes that represents your concept's range, including your signature items. Not your entire menu -- a focused selection that tells a coherent story. Have the chef present each course briefly (30-60 seconds per dish), explaining the inspiration, ingredients, or technique. Keep it conversational, not scripted. Guests should feel like they are getting insight, not sitting through a presentation.

### Execution

**No hard sell.** Do not hand guests a press release and ask them to write about you. The food and the experience are the pitch. If the food is good and the story is interesting, they will write. If you have to ask, the food was not good enough or the story was not clear enough.

**Make photography easy.** Good lighting is non-negotiable. If your dining room is dark and moody (many Bangkok restaurants are), set up the media dinner table in the best-lit area or bring in supplemental lighting that does not destroy the ambiance. Journalists who cannot photograph the food will not write about it because they have no visuals to accompany the story.

**Beverages matter.** Offer your beverage program -- cocktails, wine pairings, or a curated drinks selection. Journalists who have had a good meal with well-paired drinks write more enthusiastically than those who ate great food with water. Budget accordingly.

**Follow up within 48 hours.** Send a thank-you message (LINE, individually, not a mass broadcast) with a link to your media kit and high-resolution photos of the dishes served that evening. Make it easy for them to write the piece by providing every image and fact they might need.

## Working with PR Agencies in Thailand

Not every restaurant needs a PR agency, and not every PR agency is worth the retainer. Here is when to hire one, what they actually do, and what it costs.

### What a Restaurant PR Agency Does

**Media relationships.** The agency has established relationships with food editors, writers, and creators. They know who to pitch, how to pitch them, and when. This relationship capital takes years to build, and a good agency gives you access to it immediately.

**Press event management.** Planning, inviting, coordinating, and following up on media events. This includes managing RSVPs (journalists are notorious for last-minute cancellations), handling dietary restrictions, and ensuring the guest list is balanced across publications.

**Ongoing media outreach.** Pitching seasonal stories, new menu launches, chef profiles, and feature ideas on your behalf. A good agency places your restaurant in trend pieces, roundups, and editorial features that you would not have access to on your own.

**Award submissions.** Preparing and submitting applications for Michelin, Asia's 50 Best, Tatler Dining, and other awards. These submissions have specific formats, deadlines, and insider knowledge that agencies possess.

**Crisis management.** Handling negative press, food safety incidents, social media crises, and reputation damage. This is the scenario where an agency earns its retainer in a single incident.

### Costs

**Full-service food PR agency (Bangkok):** 25,000-80,000 THB/month on retainer, typically with a 6-12 month minimum commitment. Premium agencies serving fine dining and international brands charge at the higher end. Expect the retainer to cover a set number of pitch activities, 1-2 press events per quarter, and ongoing media monitoring.

**Freelance PR consultant:** 10,000-30,000 THB/month. Often a former agency professional working independently. Less overhead, more personal attention, but narrower media reach than a well-connected agency.

**Project-based PR:** 30,000-100,000 THB per project (e.g., a restaurant opening campaign covering 2-3 months). Good for operators who need help with a launch but not ongoing retainer.

### When to Hire an Agency

**Fine dining and premium concepts.** If your average check exceeds 2,000 THB per person, a PR agency makes sense because your target media and your target customer both respond to the type of coverage agencies generate -- editorial features, award submissions, and curated media events.

**International brands entering Thailand.** An agency with local media relationships bridges the gap between your global brand and Thailand's specific media ecosystem.

**Restaurants pursuing awards.** If a Michelin star or a 50 Best placement is part of your business strategy, an agency with experience in award submissions significantly improves your odds.

### When You Do Not Need One

**Casual dining under 800 THB average check.** The ROI on a 40,000 THB/month PR retainer is hard to justify for a casual concept. The same money invested in [social media marketing](/blog/restaurant-marketing-thailand) and [influencer partnerships](/blog/influencer-marketing-restaurants-thailand) will generate more covers.

**Early-stage restaurants still refining their concept.** PR amplifies what you already are. If your menu changes every month and your service is still inconsistent, PR coverage will lock in an impression that may not match where you end up. Get the fundamentals right first, then tell the media about it.

<Callout type="info">
A useful test: if you can clearly articulate your restaurant's story in two sentences and a journalist would find it interesting, you are ready for PR. If you struggle to explain what makes your restaurant worth writing about, you need to sharpen your concept before investing in media outreach.
</Callout>

## Social Media as PR: Creating Shareable Moments

In 2026, the line between social media and PR is nearly gone. A viral Instagram Reel or TikTok video generates more reach than most magazine features, and the content creates itself if you design your restaurant with shareability in mind.

### Designing for the Camera

**One signature visual moment.** Every restaurant should have at least one dish, drink, or presentation that makes people reach for their phone. A tableside flambe. A dessert with a dramatic reveal. A cocktail poured over a carved ice ball. This is not about gimmicks -- it is about creating a visual that communicates your concept in a single image or 15-second video. The restaurants that generate the most organic social media coverage are the ones that give people something worth sharing.

**Plating for the overhead shot.** Instagram food photography is overwhelmingly shot from above. Consider how your dishes look from that angle. Plates with height and dimension photograph well, while flat dishes with scattered garnish look better from above. Your plating style should account for both the diner's perspective and the camera's.

**Interior photo opportunities.** A statement wall, a unique light fixture, a beautiful bar setup, or an open kitchen that frames the action -- these become the backdrop for the guest photos that populate your tagged posts. The cost of one well-designed photo-worthy element (20,000-50,000 THB) generates free social content for years.

### Turning Social Media Into Press Coverage

Social media buzz generates press coverage, not the other way around. When a journalist sees your restaurant generating organic social media activity -- tagged posts, Reels, TikTok videos -- they are more likely to cover you because the social proof confirms audience interest. This means your social media strategy is foundational to your PR strategy.

**Track your user-generated content.** Monitor your tagged posts, check-ins, and mentions. Repost the best user-generated content (with credit). When you see consistent organic posting from guests, document it -- this social proof is ammunition for your media pitches. "Our guests generated 450 tagged Instagram posts in our first three months" is a data point that interests editors.

## Award Submissions

Awards provide a different kind of credibility than media coverage. A Michelin star, a spot on Asia's 50 Best, or a Tatler Dining Award positions your restaurant in a competitive set that signals quality to diners who use these lists as decision-making shortcuts.

### Michelin Guide Thailand

**How it works.** Michelin inspectors visit anonymously, pay for their meals, and evaluate based on five criteria: quality of ingredients, mastery of cooking techniques, harmony of flavors, personality of the chef in the cuisine, and consistency. You do not apply for a Michelin star -- inspectors find you. But you can increase the likelihood of being noticed.

**What you can do:**
- Ensure your restaurant appears on Google Maps with accurate information and your concept is clearly communicated on your website and social media
- Generate media coverage that raises your profile with inspectors who track restaurant openings and food media
- Maintain consistency -- inspectors visit multiple times, often months apart
- Submit your restaurant to the Michelin guide's submission portal (yes, this exists and it signals that you want to be considered)
- Focus on execution, not on trying to cook "Michelin food" -- the guide rewards authentic excellence in any cuisine

**Timeline.** The Thailand Michelin Guide is typically announced in November/December. Inspections happen year-round. New restaurants need at least 6-12 months of operation before realistic consideration.

### Asia's 50 Best Restaurants

**How it works.** The list is compiled from votes by a panel of over 300 regional food writers, chefs, restaurateurs, and food industry professionals. Each panelist casts 10 votes, and they must have eaten at the restaurants they vote for within the past 18 months.

**What you can do:**
- Identify and build relationships with voters in the Thai and Southeast Asian panel (many are known food writers and industry figures)
- Host voters for dinner and ensure they experience your best
- Generate international press coverage that raises awareness among the broader voter base
- Submit to the "discovery" program for newer restaurants

**The cost of chasing 50 Best.** Hosting voters, maintaining an international PR profile, and traveling to the annual ceremony adds up. Budget 200,000-500,000 THB annually in direct and indirect costs. The return -- increased international bookings, media coverage, and brand value -- is significant for restaurants that place, but the investment is a gamble for those that do not.

### Tatler Dining Awards

**How it works.** Tatler's team and advisory panel evaluate restaurants across categories. The awards carry weight in Bangkok's affluent dining scene. Submission is typically invitation-based, but proactive outreach to the Tatler editorial team can get your restaurant on their radar.

### BK Best Restaurant Awards

**How it works.** BK Magazine's annual awards combine editorial evaluation with reader voting. Categories span cuisines, price points, and dining styles. Winning a BK award drives immediate reservations from the English-speaking Bangkok dining community. Submit by contacting the BK food team directly.

<Callout type="tip">
Award submissions are a long game. Do not expect results in year one. Build relationships with the judging community, maintain consistent quality, and submit annually. Most award-winning restaurants in Bangkok were considered for 2-3 years before their first recognition. The restaurants that win are the ones that made it easy for judges to experience their best -- not the ones that lobbied hardest.
</Callout>

## Crisis PR: When Things Go Wrong

Every restaurant will face a PR crisis at some point. A food safety incident. A viral negative review. A staff member caught on camera behaving badly. A social media pile-on. The question is not whether it will happen -- it is whether you are prepared when it does.

### Food Safety Incidents

**Scenario:** A customer posts on social media that they got food poisoning after eating at your restaurant. The post gains traction, and comments pile on with "me too" claims (some real, some opportunistic).

**Response framework:**

**First 2 hours:** Acknowledge the post publicly. "We take this seriously and are looking into it." Do not admit fault. Do not deny the claim. Do not ignore it. Contact the original poster privately (via LINE or DM) to gather details -- what they ordered, when they visited, their symptoms.

**First 24 hours:** If there is a genuine food safety concern, conduct an internal investigation. Check ingredient storage temperatures, inspect the dish in question, review the prep process. If you find a problem, fix it immediately. Communicate your response publicly: "We have conducted a thorough review of our kitchen procedures and [specific action taken]."

**If the claim is false or exaggerated:** Respond factually and calmly. "We have reviewed our records for that date. Our kitchen temperature logs, ingredient sourcing records, and preparation procedures are all within safety standards. We welcome any customer with concerns to contact us directly."

**What not to do:** Do not attack the poster. Do not threaten legal action publicly. Do not delete negative comments on your social media (this accelerates the pile-on). Do not go silent -- silence in a crisis is interpreted as guilt.

### Negative Viral Content

**Scenario:** A TikTok video of a bad experience at your restaurant goes viral -- 500,000+ views, hundreds of negative comments.

**Response:** Speed matters. Respond within hours, not days. Post a video response from the owner or chef (not a text statement -- video feels more personal and sincere). Acknowledge the specific issue. Explain what you are doing to address it. Invite the creator to return for a corrected experience. Most viral negativity can be defused by a human, empathetic response that demonstrates you care about getting it right.

### Staff Misconduct

**Scenario:** A video of a staff member mishandling food, being rude to a customer, or behaving inappropriately in your restaurant goes viral.

**Response:** Address the specific individual and action. "This behavior does not represent our standards. We have [specific disciplinary action]. We are [retraining / reviewing procedures / etc.]." Do not publicly name the staff member. Be firm about your standards but fair to the individual.

### When to Bring in Professional Help

If a crisis involves potential legal liability (food poisoning requiring hospitalization, a customer injury, or an employee lawsuit), bring in a PR consultant with crisis experience immediately. This is not the time for DIY. A professional crisis manager costs 30,000-80,000 THB for an engagement, but the alternative -- a mishandled crisis that damages your reputation permanently -- costs much more.

<Callout type="warning">
Have a crisis communication plan before you need one. Designate a single spokesperson (usually the owner or GM). Prepare template responses for common scenarios (food safety complaint, negative review, service failure). Save your PR contact's phone number where you can reach it at 11 PM on a Saturday, because that is when crises happen.
</Callout>

## Measuring PR Success

PR is harder to measure than [digital marketing](/blog/restaurant-marketing-thailand), but it is not unmeasurable. Track these metrics monthly.

### Quantitative Metrics

**Media mentions.** Count the number of times your restaurant appears in media coverage -- reviews, features, lists, roundups. Track by publication and by language (Thai vs. English). A healthy PR program for an active restaurant should generate 2-5 media mentions per month after the initial launch period.

**Coverage reach.** Estimate the audience size of each piece of coverage. A BK Magazine feature reaches approximately 200,000-400,000 monthly readers. A Wongnai editorial list reaches millions. A mid-tier Thai food YouTube video reaches 50,000-200,000 viewers. Multiply mentions by estimated reach for a total coverage reach figure.

**Reservation attribution.** Ask guests how they heard about you. Train your host team to ask every new customer. "A BK Magazine article" or "I saw you on a Wongnai list" is directly attributable to PR. Track this weekly.

**Social media lift.** After a major piece of coverage, measure the change in followers, tagged posts, and engagement. A good media feature typically generates a 5-15% spike in Instagram followers and a measurable increase in tagged posts for 1-2 weeks.

### Qualitative Metrics

**Sentiment.** Is the coverage positive, neutral, or negative? One negative review that reaches 200,000 people is worse than no coverage at all. Monitor not just volume but tone.

**Quality of outlets.** Coverage in BK Magazine carries different weight than a mention in a small blog. Track the prestige and audience alignment of your coverage, not just the count.

**Award recognition.** Track submissions, shortlists, and wins. Even being shortlisted for a Michelin Bib Gourmand or a BK Best Restaurant award has measurable value.

## DIY PR for Budget Restaurants

Not every restaurant can afford a PR agency, and not every restaurant needs one. Here is how to handle your own PR effectively on a minimal budget.

### The Minimum Viable PR Program

**Build your media kit (one-time, 2-4 hours).** Write your fact sheet and concept narrative. Get professional food photography -- even a single session (8,000-15,000 THB for 20-30 images) provides enough material for your media kit and 2-3 months of social media content. Organize everything in a shareable Google Drive folder.

**Identify your 10 key media contacts (2-3 hours).** Research the food writers who cover your cuisine type and neighborhood. Follow them on social media. Note their LINE IDs if publicly available. This is your target list.

**Send personalized invitations (ongoing).** Invite 1-2 media contacts per month for a complimentary meal. Personalize every invitation. Follow up once if you do not hear back; do not pester.

**Write your own press announcements (as needed).** For menu launches, anniversaries, or notable events, write a brief announcement (half a page) with 3-5 photos and send it to your 10 contacts via LINE. Keep it factual and brief.

**Monitor and respond (15 minutes daily).** Check your restaurant name on Google, Instagram, Wongnai, and Pantip. Respond to mentions, thank people for coverage, and address negative feedback promptly.

**Total DIY PR cost: 8,000-15,000 THB for photography, plus 2-3 hours per week of your time.** This is not as effective as a professional agency, but it is infinitely better than doing nothing -- and it builds the foundation for professional PR when your budget allows.

## Building a PR Calendar

Timing your PR activities around openings, menu changes, seasonal events, and awards creates a rhythm that keeps your restaurant in the media conversation year-round.

### Annual PR Calendar Framework

**January-February:** Submit for Asia's 50 Best discovery list. Plan Chinese New Year media outreach for relevant concepts. Start Michelin submission if applicable.

**March-April:** Launch new spring/summer menu with media tasting. Songkran-themed events or closures -- communicate both to media. Begin planning for rainy season promotions.

**May-June:** Pre-rainy season media push. Submit for Tatler Dining Awards. Host a mid-year media dinner to maintain relationships.

**July-August:** Rainy season. Focus on delivery PR and comfort food stories. Pitch seasonal ingredient stories to food media.

**September-October:** Plan year-end campaigns. Submit for BK Best Restaurant Awards. Begin outreach for Christmas and New Year coverage.

**November-December:** Michelin Guide Thailand announcement -- leverage any inclusion. Year-end "best of" list season -- respond to journalist inquiries quickly. Host year-end media appreciation dinner.

**Ongoing (monthly):** Send 1-2 updates to media contacts. Invite 1-2 media for meals. Monitor and respond to coverage. Track metrics.

The restaurants that maintain a consistent PR presence -- not just around openings and major events, but month after month -- are the ones that editors think of when they need a restaurant for a feature, a list, or a comment on a food trend. Showing up once is an introduction. Showing up consistently is how you become part of the story.
