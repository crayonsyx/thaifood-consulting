A client in Sathorn called me in January because her food cost had crept from 32% to 41% over six months and she could not figure out why. She had receipts from suppliers in a folder, a whiteboard in the walk-in with par levels that nobody updated, and a general sense that "things were going missing." No counts. No tracking. No system.

We spent a full day doing a physical inventory count, cross-referencing it against her purchase records and POS sales data. The result: she was losing roughly 85,000 THB per month to a combination of over-ordering (produce rotting in the walk-in), portion drift (cooks eyeballing instead of weighing), and what turned out to be a prep cook walking out with 3-4 kg of premium beef every week. None of this was visible because she had no inventory system -- just a feeling that costs were "a bit high."

That 85,000 THB per month was more than twice what a proper inventory management system would have cost for the year.

This guide covers what a restaurant inventory system actually does, which options work in Thailand, how to implement one without disrupting your operation, and the realistic savings you can expect. If your food cost percentage is a number you calculate once a quarter (or guess), this is for you.

## Why Most Thai Restaurants Still Use Paper and Excel

I estimate that 70-80% of independent restaurants in Thailand manage inventory with some combination of paper counts, Excel spreadsheets, and the chef's memory. There are real reasons for this, and understanding them helps explain why switching to a system requires more than just installing software.

**The chef-owner model.** In many Thai restaurants, the owner is the chef, and the chef keeps ingredient knowledge in their head. They know what needs to be ordered because they cooked yesterday and they will cook tomorrow. This works until the chef takes a day off, gets sick, or opens a second location -- at which point the knowledge walks out the door.

**Low staff tech comfort.** Kitchen staff at many Thai restaurants are not comfortable with software. Asking a 55-year-old prep cook to log waste on a tablet is a different proposition than asking a 25-year-old server to use a [POS system](/blog/restaurant-pos-systems-thailand). The resistance is real, and it needs to be planned for.

**"We are not big enough."** Small restaurants assume inventory systems are for large operations. A 30-seat noodle shop does not think of itself as needing "inventory management software." But a 30-seat noodle shop buying 150,000 THB of ingredients per month absolutely benefits from knowing where that money goes.

**Cost perception.** Restaurant operators see inventory software as an expense, not an investment. When you are watching every baht, a 2,000 THB/month subscription feels like a cost you can avoid. The problem is that the cost of not tracking inventory -- waste, theft, over-ordering, portion drift -- is almost always larger than the subscription. You just cannot see it without a system.

<Callout type="info">
A common rule of thumb: if your restaurant spends more than 100,000 THB per month on food and beverage purchases, a dedicated inventory system will likely pay for itself within 3-6 months through reduced waste and better ordering alone. Below that threshold, a well-structured spreadsheet may be sufficient -- I will cover that option too.
</Callout>

## What Inventory Management Software Actually Does

Before comparing specific products, it helps to understand what these systems handle. "Inventory management" sounds like counting things on shelves, but a proper restaurant system covers a chain of connected processes.

### Receiving

When ingredients arrive from a [supplier](/blog/food-suppliers-bangkok-restaurants), the system records what was delivered, in what quantity, at what price, and compares it against what was ordered. This catches short deliveries (you ordered 20 kg of chicken but only received 18), price discrepancies (the invoice says 280 THB/kg but your agreed price was 260), and wrong items. Without a receiving system, these errors -- which happen weekly at most restaurants -- go unnoticed and add up to thousands of baht per month.

### Tracking

Once ingredients are received, the system tracks their movement. Raw ingredients go into storage. When a cook pulls 5 kg of shrimp for prep, that movement is recorded (either manually or automatically based on sales). The system always knows -- in theory -- how much of each ingredient should be on hand. The gap between "should be" and "actually is" tells you where your problems are.

### Counting

Physical inventory counts verify what the system thinks you have against what is actually on the shelves. Every restaurant needs to do this, whether by hand or with the system's counting tools. Good inventory software makes counting faster with barcode scanning, organized count sheets that match your storage layout, and the ability to count on a phone or tablet while walking the storeroom.

### Ordering

Based on current stock levels, par levels (the minimum amount you want on hand), and upcoming demand, the system calculates what you need to order. Some systems generate purchase orders automatically and send them directly to suppliers. This eliminates the guesswork and the "we ran out of limes at 7 PM on a Saturday" problem.

### Reporting

The system produces reports that answer the questions every restaurant operator should be asking: What is my actual food cost this week? Which ingredients have the highest waste? Which supplier's prices increased most? Am I over-ordering anything? How does my theoretical food cost (based on recipes) compare to my actual food cost (based on purchases and inventory changes)?

## Key Features to Look For in Thailand

Not every feature matters equally in the Thai market. Here is what to prioritize when evaluating systems.

### Must-Have Features

**Thai language interface.** Your receiving staff and kitchen team need to use this system. If it is English-only, adoption will suffer. Some international systems offer Thai translations, but the quality varies -- a poorly translated interface is worse than no translation because it creates confusion.

**Supplier integration for Thai suppliers.** Thai restaurant supply chains involve a mix of large distributors (Makro, Siam Makro), specialist suppliers (seafood markets, organic farms), and informal vendors (the uncle who delivers fresh herbs from his garden every morning). Your system needs to handle all of these, including suppliers who do not use email, do not send digital invoices, and deliver on a motorcycle with a handwritten receipt.

**Recipe costing.** This connects your inventory to your menu. You define each menu item's recipe (ingredients and quantities), and the system calculates the ingredient cost per dish based on current purchase prices. When the price of pork belly rises from 180 to 220 THB/kg, recipe costing shows you exactly which menu items are affected and by how much -- so you can adjust portions, prices, or suppliers before your food cost spirals.

**Waste tracking.** The ability to log waste separately from usage. Spoilage, over-production, staff meals, spills, and prep trim all consume inventory but do not generate revenue. If you cannot separate waste from sales usage, you cannot identify where your losses are coming from.

**Multi-unit support.** If you have more than one location, or plan to, the system needs to consolidate inventory data across branches while allowing each location to manage its own counts and orders. Transferring inventory between locations should be trackable.

### Nice-to-Have Features

**Barcode and QR scanning.** Speeds up receiving and counting. Most packaged ingredients from large suppliers have barcodes; fresh market items do not. Useful but not a dealbreaker.

**Mobile app.** Counting inventory on a phone is faster than carrying a clipboard. Most modern systems offer this, but test it -- some mobile apps are barely functional afterthoughts.

**Accounting integration.** Direct export to Thai accounting software like Express or PEAK, or to international tools like Xero. This saves your bookkeeper time when reconciling purchases against your [financial statements](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand).

**Shelf-life tracking.** Alerts when ingredients are approaching expiration. Particularly useful for restaurants handling fresh seafood, dairy, or specialty ingredients with short windows.

**Automatic purchase orders.** The system monitors stock levels against par levels and generates purchase orders when items fall below threshold. Useful for high-volume operations, but many Thai restaurants prefer the manual control of deciding what to order each day.

<Callout type="warning">
Be skeptical of any inventory system that claims "automatic" tracking without physical counts. No software magically knows what is on your shelves. Even the best systems with POS integration and recipe-based deductions drift from reality within a week. Physical counts are not optional -- they are the calibration that keeps your data honest.
</Callout>

The Thai market has fewer dedicated restaurant inventory options than the US or UK, but several workable choices exist. I have used or evaluated all of these with clients.

- Purpose-built for restaurants with recipe costing, waste tracking, and menu engineering tools
- Strong supplier management -- can handle multiple suppliers per ingredient, price comparison, and order history

**Best for:** International restaurant groups operating in Thailand where English-language operations are standard, and operators who need deep recipe costing and supplier management.

**Overview:** BlueCart focuses on the ordering and supplier management side of inventory. It started as a procurement platform and expanded into inventory tracking. Strong on purchasing workflow, lighter on kitchen-side inventory management.

**Strengths:**

- Order approval workflows for multi-branch operations

**Pricing:** Starts around 3,500 THB/month for basic plans.

**Best for:** Operators whose primary pain point is supplier management and ordering, rather than full kitchen inventory tracking.

### POS-Integrated Inventory (FoodStory, Ocha, StoreHub)

### The Spreadsheet Option

For smaller operations, a well-structured Google Sheets or Excel system can handle basic inventory management at zero software cost. I have built spreadsheet inventory systems for clients that work well when the operation is small enough.

**What a good inventory spreadsheet includes:**
- **Ingredient master list** with unit of measure, par level, current price, and primary supplier
- **Receiving log** where each delivery is recorded with date, supplier, items, quantities, and prices
- **Weekly count sheet** organized by storage area (dry store, walk-in, freezer, bar) with columns for count date and quantity
- **Variance report** that compares expected usage (from POS sales data and recipes) against actual usage (from inventory change plus purchases)
- **Order calculator** that compares current stock against par levels and outputs what needs to be ordered

**When a spreadsheet is enough:** Single-location restaurant with fewer than 50 unique ingredients, monthly food purchases under 100,000 THB, and a manager who is disciplined about updating the sheet daily. If any of those conditions breaks -- more ingredients, higher spend, inconsistent updating -- the spreadsheet starts hiding problems instead of revealing them.

**When you need software:** The moment you realize nobody is updating the spreadsheet consistently, or when your food cost variance exceeds 3% and you cannot identify why, or when you open a second location. Software enforces the process; spreadsheets only work when people voluntarily follow the process.

## Cloud vs. On-Premise Inventory Systems

This decision is simpler than it is for [POS systems](/blog/restaurant-pos-systems-thailand) because nearly every modern inventory platform is cloud-based. MarketMan, BlueCart, and all the POS-integrated options run in the cloud.

**Cloud advantages for inventory specifically:**
- Supplier price data updates in real-time across locations
- Your chef can check stock levels from home before placing morning orders
- Purchase history and cost trends are always accessible
- Automatic backups -- no risk of losing years of cost data
- Updates and new features deploy automatically

**The one scenario where local matters:** If you are doing inventory counts in a storeroom or walk-in cooler with no WiFi signal, you need an app with offline counting that syncs later. MarketMan and FoodStory both handle this. Test the offline mode in your actual storeroom before committing -- some apps claim offline support but lose data during sync.

## Integrating Inventory with Your POS and Accounting

An inventory system that does not talk to your POS and your accounting software creates more work, not less. Integration is where the real value compounds.

### POS Integration

**What good POS-inventory integration does:** When a customer orders pad thai, the POS records the sale. The inventory system deducts the pad thai recipe's ingredients (noodles, shrimp, egg, bean sprouts, tamarind sauce, etc.) from your theoretical stock. At the end of the week, you count actual stock and compare it to what the system thinks you should have. The gap is your variance -- the combination of waste, theft, portioning errors, and unrecorded usage.

**Without POS integration:** You are manually entering sales data into your inventory system, or worse, trying to calculate theoretical usage by hand. This is where most spreadsheet systems break down -- the math is tedious and prone to error, so it stops getting done.

**Integration options in Thailand:**
- FoodStory, Ocha, StoreHub -- inventory is built into the POS, so integration is automatic
- MarketMan -- connects via API to FoodStory and several international POS systems
- BlueCart -- limited POS integration for Thai-market systems

### Accounting Integration

Your inventory purchases are a major expense line, and your accountant needs accurate data. The flow should work like this: inventory system records all purchases with dates, amounts, and supplier details, then exports this data in a format your [accounting software](/blog/restaurant-accounting-thailand) can import.

**For Thai accounting (Express, PEAK):** FoodStory exports purchase data that can be imported into Express. MarketMan exports CSV files that your bookkeeper can adapt for any accounting system. The process is not seamless, but it beats manually re-entering every purchase invoice.

**For international accounting (Xero, QuickBooks):** MarketMan and Lightspeed have direct integrations. If you are using Xero, MarketMan's connection is the smoothest path for purchase data reconciliation.

<Callout type="tip">
Set up a weekly routine: every Monday morning, export the previous week's purchase data from your inventory system and send it to your bookkeeper. This takes 5 minutes and prevents the end-of-month scramble where your accountant is chasing you for three weeks of missing purchase receipts.
</Callout>

## Implementing Inventory Management: The Phased Approach

Do not try to implement everything at once. I have watched restaurants buy MarketMan, spend two weeks entering recipes and supplier data, launch the full system on a Monday, and abandon it by Friday because the staff was overwhelmed and the data was wrong. A phased approach works.

### Phase 1: Master List and Receiving (Weeks 1-2)

Start with the basics. Create your ingredient master list -- every item you purchase, with the unit of measure, current price, primary supplier, and storage location. This alone takes 1-3 days for a typical restaurant with 80-150 ingredients.

Set up receiving. When deliveries arrive, someone logs what was delivered, checks it against the order, and records any discrepancies. This is the single highest-impact change you can make. Most restaurants lose 2-5% of their food purchases to receiving errors -- short deliveries, wrong items, and price overcharges -- that nobody catches.

### Phase 2: Counting and Par Levels (Weeks 3-4)

Establish your counting routine. Start with weekly full counts and daily counts of your 10-15 highest-cost items (proteins, seafood, premium ingredients). Enter par levels for every item based on your sales patterns, and use these to guide ordering.

This phase gives you your first real data on stock levels, usage rates, and ordering patterns. Do not skip it or rush through it -- the data from these first few weeks of counting calibrates everything that follows.

### Phase 3: Recipe Costing (Weeks 5-8)

Enter your recipes into the system. Every menu item needs a recipe with ingredient quantities. This is the most labor-intensive phase -- a restaurant with 40 menu items and an average of 8 ingredients per recipe needs 320 ingredient-recipe links entered and verified.

Do it in batches: start with your top 10 selling dishes, then your top 10 highest-cost dishes, then work through the rest. Once recipes are entered, the system can calculate theoretical food cost per dish and compare it to actual usage.

### Phase 4: Waste Tracking and Variance Analysis (Weeks 9-12)

Add waste logging to your daily routine. Every time something is thrown away -- spoiled produce, burnt items, over-produced prep, staff meals -- it gets logged with a reason code. After a month of waste data, you will have a clear picture of where your losses are coming from.

Variance analysis becomes meaningful once you have reliable data from Phases 1-3. The system compares theoretical usage (what you should have used based on sales) against actual usage (what you actually used based on inventory changes and purchases). A variance above 2-3% signals a problem worth investigating.

<Callout type="warning">
The most common implementation failure: trying to enter every recipe with perfect accuracy before going live. Perfection is the enemy of progress here. Start with approximate recipes using your cooks' best estimates, then refine based on actual usage data. A recipe that is 90% accurate is infinitely more useful than a perfect recipe you never finish entering.
</Callout>

## Par Levels and Automatic Reordering

Par levels are the minimum quantities you want on hand for each ingredient. Get them right, and your ordering becomes systematic instead of reactive. Get them wrong, and you either run out during service or bury money in ingredients that spoil before they are used.

### Setting Par Levels

**Start with sales data.** If your POS shows you sell an average of 25 orders of green curry per day, and each order uses 200g of chicken breast, you need 5 kg of chicken breast per day for green curry alone. Add chicken usage across all menu items, multiply by the number of days between deliveries, add a safety buffer, and you have your par level.

**The formula:**

**Par level = (average daily usage x days between deliveries) + safety stock**

Safety stock is typically 20-30% above the calculated need, accounting for demand variability and delivery delays. For perishable items in Thailand's heat, err toward tighter pars with more frequent deliveries rather than large buffer stocks that spoil.

**Seasonal adjustment.** Thai [ingredient costs and availability shift with the seasons](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality). Your par levels for fresh herbs should be different in the wet season (abundant, cheap) versus the dry season (scarcer, more expensive). Review and adjust par levels monthly, or quarterly at minimum.

### Automatic Reordering

Some inventory systems can generate purchase orders automatically when stock falls below par level. In practice, I recommend semi-automatic ordering for most Thai restaurants: the system flags what needs to be ordered and suggests quantities, but a human reviews and approves before the order goes to the supplier.

Why not fully automatic? Because Thai restaurant supply chains have variables that software cannot account for. Your seafood vendor called to say he has exceptional quality sea bass today at a good price -- do you want to feature it as a special? Your regular pork supplier is delayed by a day -- should you order from the backup supplier or adjust tomorrow's menu? These judgment calls need a human.

## Inventory Counting Best Practices

Counting is the discipline that makes everything else work. Without accurate counts, your variance analysis is fiction, your par levels are guesses, and your food cost percentage is a number you calculated from incomplete data.

### Frequency

**Daily counts (high-value items):** Count your top 10-15 most expensive ingredients every day. Proteins, seafood, premium cuts, expensive imported items. This takes 10-15 minutes and catches theft or waste within 24 hours.

**Weekly full counts:** Count everything in your inventory once per week, on the same day each week. This is your calibration point. Most restaurants do this on the quietest day (Monday or Tuesday in Bangkok) when stock levels are at their lowest and counts are fastest.

**Monthly deep counts:** A thorough count that includes items you might skip weekly -- cleaning supplies, disposables, dry goods in bulk storage, bar stock (every bottle, not just open ones). This feeds into your monthly food cost calculation.

### Counting Methods

**Spot counts:** Quick checks of specific items during or after service -- a manager walks the line at close and counts proteins. Takes 5 minutes, catches the biggest issues.

**Full counts:** Every item in every storage area. Use a count sheet organized by location (walk-in shelf 1, walk-in shelf 2, dry store left, dry store right) so the counter moves logically through the space instead of hunting for items. With a well-organized count sheet, a full count of 100-150 items takes 30-45 minutes.

**Blind counts vs. book counts:** In a blind count, the counter does not see what the system thinks they should have -- they count what is physically there, and discrepancies surface afterward. This prevents the human tendency to "find" 5 kg of chicken because the system says there should be 5 kg. Blind counts produce more honest data.

### Who Counts

**Never let the same person count and order.** This is basic loss prevention. If the person who orders ingredients is also the person who counts them, they can order excess, remove it, and adjust the count to hide the theft. Separate the duties.

**Rotate counters.** If the same person always counts the walk-in, they develop blind spots and shortcuts. Rotate counting responsibilities monthly.

**Manager verification.** After a count is completed, a manager should spot-check 5-10 high-value items. This takes 3 minutes and catches both honest mistakes and dishonest counts.

<Callout type="tip">
The best time to count walk-in inventory in a Bangkok kitchen is before the morning prep shift starts -- typically 7-8 AM. The kitchen is cool, the walk-in has not been opened and closed 50 times yet, and the count reflects the true overnight stock level. Counting during service is inaccurate because items are constantly moving between storage and prep stations.
</Callout>

## Waste Tracking and Loss Prevention

Waste is the silent killer of food cost. Every restaurant has it, but most restaurants do not measure it -- which means they cannot reduce it.

### Types of Waste

**Prep waste (trim).** The skin, bones, fat, and outer leaves you remove during preparation. This is unavoidable, but it should be accounted for in your recipes. If your recipe says "500g chicken breast" but you are buying whole chickens and yielding 60% breast meat, your recipe needs to reflect the whole-chicken cost, not the breast-meat cost.

**Spoilage.** Ingredients that expire before use. This is a purchasing and storage problem -- you bought too much, stored it wrong, or did not rotate stock properly (first in, first out). Track spoilage by ingredient and by reason. If your cilantro spoilage rate is 30%, you are either buying too much or storing it poorly.

**Over-production.** Prep items made in excess of demand. The kitchen preps 10 liters of tom yum base but only uses 6 -- the remaining 4 liters either get used tomorrow (acceptable) or thrown away (waste). Track over-production as a separate category from spoilage.

**Plate waste.** What comes back from the dining room uneaten. While this is harder to track precisely, high plate waste on a specific dish signals a portion size problem or a quality issue.

**Theft.** It exists. Pretending it does not is expensive. Inventory tracking is the most effective theft deterrent because it creates accountability -- when the count shows 3 kg of salmon missing and there is no waste log to explain it, questions get asked.

### Waste Reduction Strategies

**Daily waste logs.** A simple sheet in the kitchen where staff record every item that gets thrown away, with the reason. This is not about blame -- it is about data. After a month, you will see patterns: the same ingredients appearing repeatedly, the same shift producing more waste, specific prep items consistently over-produced.

**FIFO enforcement.** First In, First Out. Date-label everything that enters storage. New deliveries go behind existing stock. This sounds basic, and it is, but I audit restaurant storage weekly and find FIFO violations in about half of them. A roll of masking tape and a marker in every storage area solves this.

**Batch size reduction.** If you are throwing away 20% of your daily soup prep, make less soup. Track daily usage for two weeks, then set prep quantities at actual average usage plus 10-15% buffer. Adjust seasonally and day-of-week.

**Cross-utilization.** Use trim and excess in other preparations. Vegetable trim becomes stock. Leftover rice becomes fried rice for staff meals. Herb stems go into marinades. This requires kitchen creativity but reduces waste cost meaningfully -- a 10% reduction in waste at a restaurant spending 300,000 THB/month on food saves 30,000 THB annually.

## Recipe Costing: Linking Inventory to Menu Items

Recipe costing is where inventory management transforms from a "counting things on shelves" exercise into a strategic tool that drives menu profitability. When your inventory system knows the recipe for every dish and the current price of every ingredient, it can tell you things that directly affect your bottom line.

### What Recipe Costing Reveals

**Actual cost per dish.** Not the cost you estimated when you designed the menu six months ago -- the cost right now, based on today's ingredient prices. In Thailand, where [ingredient prices fluctuate seasonally](/blog/thai-ingredient-costs-seasonality) and with supply chain disruptions, this number changes more than most operators realize.

**Menu item profitability.** Your best-selling dish might not be your most profitable. Recipe costing shows the contribution margin (selling price minus ingredient cost) for every item. Combined with sales volume from your POS, you can identify which dishes deserve promotion, which need a price adjustment, and which are dragging your food cost up.

**Price sensitivity analysis.** When pork prices rise 15%, recipe costing shows which dishes are affected and by how much. Instead of a blanket price increase (which customers notice and resent), you can make targeted adjustments to the specific items that need them.

**Portion cost drift.** Over time, cooks start adding a little more of this, a heavier pour of that. Recipe costing sets the standard. When the actual food cost for a dish consistently exceeds the recipe cost by more than 5%, you have a portioning problem -- and now you can see it.

### Setting Up Recipe Costing

**Step 1: Standardize recipes.** Every dish needs a written recipe with exact quantities in consistent units (grams, milliliters, pieces). This is where many Thai restaurants hit resistance, because experienced cooks work by feel rather than by measurement. The compromise: have the cook prepare the dish while someone else weighs and records each ingredient addition. Do this three times and average the quantities.

**Step 2: Include yield percentages.** A kilo of shrimp is not a kilo of cleaned, deveined shrimp. Record the yield percentage for every ingredient that has trim loss. Shell-on shrimp might yield 65% usable meat, meaning your 400 THB/kg purchase is actually costing 615 THB/kg of usable product.

**Step 3: Link to current prices.** Connect each recipe ingredient to your inventory system's current purchase price. When the price changes -- and in Thailand, fresh market prices change weekly -- the recipe cost updates automatically. This is where spreadsheets fail and software earns its subscription cost.

**Step 4: Calculate and set targets.** For each dish, calculate: ingredient cost, selling price, food cost percentage, and contribution margin. Set target food cost percentages by category -- typically 28-32% for mains, 20-25% for appetizers, 15-20% for beverages, and 25-30% overall for a healthy Thai restaurant operation.

<Callout type="info">
Recipe costing is the bridge between your [food cost control efforts](/blog/food-cost-control-bangkok) and your daily operations. Without it, food cost management is reactive -- you notice the problem at month-end. With it, food cost management is proactive -- you see the problem forming in real-time and adjust before it hits your P&L.
</Callout>

## Reporting and Analytics

Data is only useful if it turns into decisions. Here are the reports that matter and what to do with them.

### Food Cost Variance Report

**What it shows:** The difference between your theoretical food cost (what you should have spent based on sales and recipes) and your actual food cost (what you actually spent based on purchases and inventory changes).

**What the numbers mean:**
- **0-2% variance:** Excellent. Your kitchen is running tight.
- **2-4% variance:** Acceptable but worth investigating. Some waste and portioning drift is normal.
- **4-6% variance:** Problem. Something is going wrong -- waste, theft, receiving errors, or recipe inaccuracy.
- **6%+ variance:** Serious problem. Investigate immediately. At 500,000 THB in monthly food purchases, a 6% unexplained variance is 30,000 THB per month walking out the door.

### Usage Trend Report

**What it shows:** How your ingredient usage changes over time -- week to week, month to month, season to season.

**What to do with it:** Spot trends before they become problems. If your chicken usage is increasing but your chicken-dish sales are flat, someone is over-portioning. If your waste on leafy greens spikes every July (rainy season), you know to adjust ordering and storage practices before next July.

### Supplier Spend Report

**What it shows:** How much you spend with each supplier, how their prices have changed over time, and how your purchase patterns compare to your actual needs.

**What to do with it:** Negotiate. When you can show a supplier that you have spent 2.4 million THB with them over the past year and their prices have increased 8% while the market increased 3%, you have leverage for a conversation. You can also identify supplier consolidation opportunities -- reducing from 12 suppliers to 8 saves time, simplifies receiving, and gives your remaining suppliers larger orders that justify better pricing.

### Dead Stock Report

**What it shows:** Ingredients sitting in storage that have not moved in 7, 14, or 30+ days.

**What to do with it:** Dead stock is money rotting on your shelves. Create specials to use it up, adjust par levels to prevent reordering, or remove the associated menu items. A monthly dead stock review takes 15 minutes and prevents the accumulation of inventory that will eventually be written off as waste.

### Calculating Your ROI

The savings from inventory management come from four sources.

**Reduced waste (1-3% of food cost).** Better ordering, proper FIFO, and waste awareness cut spoilage. At 300,000 THB/month in food purchases, a 2% waste reduction saves 6,000 THB/month.

**Reduced theft (0.5-2% of food cost).** The presence of a tracking system deters theft, and variance reports catch what deterrence does not. At 300,000 THB/month, a 1% theft reduction saves 3,000 THB/month.

**Better purchasing (1-2% of food cost).** Price tracking, supplier comparison, and order optimization reduce purchasing costs. At 300,000 THB/month, a 1.5% purchasing improvement saves 4,500 THB/month.

**Portion control (0.5-1% of food cost).** Recipe costing and variance analysis identify portioning drift. At 300,000 THB/month, a 0.5% improvement saves 1,500 THB/month.

**Total typical savings: 2-5% of food cost, or 6,000-15,000 THB/month for a restaurant spending 300,000 THB/month on food.**

Against a system cost of 1,500-8,000 THB/month, the ROI is almost always positive within the first quarter. The restaurant I mentioned at the start of this article was losing 85,000 THB/month -- an extreme case, but even moderate savings of 10,000 THB/month against a 2,500 THB/month system cost produces a 4:1 return.

## Common Mistakes

**Implementing everything at once.** Follow the phased approach. Trying to launch receiving, counting, recipe costing, waste tracking, and automatic ordering on the same day guarantees failure and frustration.

**Not entering recipes accurately.** If your pad thai recipe says 200g of shrimp but your cooks actually use 250g, your variance analysis will always show a phantom loss. Get real quantities from your actual cooks, not from the recipe book you wrote before opening.

**Treating counts as optional.** "We were too busy to count this week" is how small problems become big ones. Counting is not something you do when you have time. It is a non-negotiable part of your weekly operation, like cleaning the hood vents or reconciling the cash drawer.

**Ignoring the data.** The system produces reports. Someone needs to read them, understand them, and act on them. I have seen restaurants with perfectly configured inventory systems and variance reports that nobody opens. The software is a tool -- it does not manage your inventory for you.

**Over-complicating par levels.** Start with simple par levels based on 3-4 weeks of actual usage data. Do not try to build complex demand forecasting models on day one. Simple and maintained beats sophisticated and abandoned.

**Forgetting about staff buy-in.** Your kitchen team needs to understand why they are logging waste, why they are being asked to weigh portions, and why the counts matter. If they see inventory management as surveillance rather than a tool that helps the restaurant operate better, they will resist or sabotage the process.

## When a Spreadsheet Is Enough vs. When You Need Software

This is a question I get at nearly every new client meeting, and the answer is straightforward.

**A spreadsheet works when:**
- You have one location with 50 or fewer unique ingredients
- Your monthly food purchases are under 100,000 THB
- One person (usually the owner or head chef) manages all purchasing and inventory
- That person is disciplined enough to update the spreadsheet daily
- You do not need real-time data or multi-user access

**You need software when:**
- You have multiple locations, or plan to within 12 months
- Your monthly food purchases exceed 100,000 THB
- Multiple people need to interact with the system (receiving staff, kitchen, manager, accountant)
- Your food cost variance consistently exceeds 3% and you cannot identify why
- You want recipe costing that updates automatically with price changes
- You need audit trails showing who recorded what, and when

The transition from spreadsheet to software is not a failure -- it is growth. Most successful restaurants in Thailand start with a spreadsheet and move to software when the operation outgrows manual tracking. The mistake is waiting too long to make the switch, not making the switch at all.
