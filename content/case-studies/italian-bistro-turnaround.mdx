## The Situation

When the owner of this Italian bistro on Sukhumvit Soi 31 first contacted us, the restaurant had been open for fourteen months and was losing money every single one of them. Monthly revenue sat at around $15,000 against fixed costs that demanded at least $20,000. The dining room could seat 60, but on most weeknights fewer than 15 covers came through.

The problems were layered. Food cost was running at 38 percent, driven by an oversized menu of 47 items that required expensive imported ingredients with short shelf lives. Walk-in cooler waste alone accounted for nearly $2,000 per month. The kitchen brigade was demoralized, turning over at a rate that made consistency impossible. Front-of-house staff lacked structured training, and the guest experience varied wildly depending on who was working that shift.

The owner, a first-time restaurateur who had invested his savings into the concept, was three months away from a very difficult conversation with his investors.

## Our Approach

We began with a five-day operational audit. Two consultants embedded in the restaurant, observing every service from prep through close. We reviewed purchasing records, POS data, staffing schedules, supplier contracts, and guest feedback across Google, TripAdvisor, and social media.

### Menu Engineering

The existing menu was the core problem. Using a standard menu engineering matrix, we classified every item by profitability and popularity. The results were stark: 19 of the 47 items were what we call "dogs" in the matrix, dishes that neither sold well nor contributed meaningful margin. Another 8 were "puzzles," profitable but rarely ordered.

We redesigned the menu from 47 items down to 28. Every remaining dish was re-costed, re-portioned, and repriced. We introduced three high-margin signature dishes positioned at the center of the new menu layout, where eye-tracking studies show diners look first. We added a daily special rotation that utilized ingredients nearing the end of their optimal freshness, cutting walk-in waste by 70 percent.

The new menu told a story. It opened with shared plates that encouraged larger group orders, moved through pastas and mains with clear price anchoring, and closed with a dessert section designed to increase per-table revenue by an average of $8.

### Supplier Renegotiation

We identified that the restaurant was sourcing from seven different suppliers with no volume leverage on any of them. We consolidated to three primary suppliers and negotiated new terms based on projected volume from the streamlined menu. On imported Italian cheeses and cured meats alone, we secured a 15 percent cost reduction.

Where possible, we substituted imported ingredients with high-quality local alternatives. Thai basil replaced Genovese basil in two dishes without any loss of character. A local artisan mozzarella producer in Pak Chong replaced the imported buffalo mozzarella at roughly 40 percent of the cost.

### Standard Operating Procedures

The kitchen had no documented recipes, no standardized prep lists, and no structured mise en place system. We wrote recipe cards for every dish on the new menu with exact weights, plating photos, and expected plate cost. We built daily prep checklists and introduced a simple inventory count system that tracked the ten highest-cost ingredients weekly.

### Staff Training and Retention

We restructured the kitchen brigade to reduce headcount by two positions that overlapped, then redirected those labor savings into a modest pay increase for the remaining team. We introduced a structured onboarding program, clear shift expectations, and a weekly team briefing.

For front-of-house, we created a service script covering greeting, upselling, wine pairing suggestions, and check presentation. We ran three evening training sessions over two weeks, including a full tasting of the new menu so every server could speak confidently about the food.

## The Turnaround

Within the first month after relaunch, average covers per night increased from 15 to 28. The combination of a tighter menu, better plating, and renewed staff confidence generated immediate word-of-mouth. The restaurant began appearing consistently on "best Italian in Bangkok" lists compiled by local food bloggers and expat communities.

By month two, food cost had dropped to 30 percent and continued falling as the team became more disciplined with portioning and waste. Revenue hit $32,000 that month. The owner reinvested a portion into a modest interior refresh and launched a weekend brunch service based on our recommendation, which added an additional $4,000 in monthly revenue with minimal incremental cost.

By the end of month three, monthly revenue reached $42,000. Food cost stabilized at 28 percent. Staff turnover, which had been constant, dropped by 60 percent. The restaurant went from losing money to operating at a healthy margin, and the weekend reservation book was full through the following month.

## Key Takeaways

This project reinforced several principles we see across struggling restaurants in Bangkok. First, an oversized menu is almost always the root cause of high food costs and inconsistent quality. Second, supplier consolidation creates immediate margin improvement with no impact on the guest experience. Third, staff retention improves dramatically when people feel supported by systems and structure rather than left to figure things out on their own.

The owner has since repaid a portion of the initial investment and is exploring a second location. We continue to advise on a quarterly basis.
